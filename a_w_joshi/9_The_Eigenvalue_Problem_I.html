0. Roadmap

Most vectors change direction when a matrix acts on them. A few special vectors keep their direction and only get stretched or shrunk. Those are the eigenvectors, and the stretch factors are the eigenvalues. Once you know them, the matrix becomes simple: in the eigenvector basis it is just a diagonal list of stretch factors.

Ax = λx→det(A − λI) = 0→Σλ = Tr A, Πλ = det A→eigenvectors, degeneracy→P⁻¹AP = Λ→commuting ⇒ common eigenvectors→normal modes
Needed from earlier Homogeneous systems: (A − λI)x = 0 has a nonzero solution ⇔ det(A − λI) = 0 (Section 8). Also similarity transformations (Section 5), hermitian and unitary matrices (Sections 3 and 5), and rank and the number of free parameters (Section 8).

1. The eigenvalue problem (Eq. 1)

Definition A is a square n×n matrix. If a nonzero column vector x and a scalar λ satisfy
Ax = λx
then x is an eigenvector of A and λ is the eigenvalue belonging to x. ("Eigen" is German for "own" or "characteristic". Other names are characteristic values or proper values.)
  • x = 0 always satisfies Ax = λx, so it is excluded. Otherwise every λ would count.
  • λ = 0 is allowed. It means Ax = 0 for some x ≠ 0, i.e. A is singular (Exercises 2, 12).
  • Rewrite it as (A − λI)x = 0 (Eq. 3b). The I is needed: "A − λ" makes no sense, because A is n×n and λ is a number.

🔬 Lab 1: hunt for the special directions

The blue arrow is a unit vector x and the orange arrow is Ax. Turn x with the slider, or press Sweep. Most of the time Ax points somewhere else. At a few special angles it lines up exactly with x (or points exactly opposite), and those are the eigenvectors (dashed lines). The length ratio |Ax|/|x| with a sign is λ. The rotation matrix has no real eigen-direction: its eigenvalues are complex. The shear has only one.

2. Finding the eigenvalues: the characteristic equation (Eqs. 2–9)

(A − λI)x = 0 is a homogeneous system of n equations. It has a nonzero solution if and only if

D(λ) ≡ det(A − λI) = = 0   (Eq. 4)
  • D(λ) is a polynomial of degree n, the characteristic polynomial: D(λ) = c₀λⁿ + c₁λⁿ⁻¹ + … + cₙ.
  • From the determinant you can read off c₀ = (−1)ⁿ, c₁ = (−1)ⁿ⁻¹(a₁₁ + … + aₙₙ), and cₙ = D(0) = det A (Eq. 6).
  • D(λ) = 0 is the characteristic equation. A polynomial of degree n has exactly n roots (counted with multiplicity, possibly complex). So:
Every n×n matrix has exactly n eigenvalues, namely the n roots λ₁, …, λₙ of its characteristic polynomial. They need not be distinct or real. The set of eigenvalues is the spectrum of A.

2×2λ² − (Tr A)λ + det A = 0  ⇒  λ = [t ± √(t² − 4d)]/2  (Exercise 25)
3×3λ³ − (Tr A)λ² + (sum of the 3 principal 2×2 minors)λ − det A = 0

Book Example 2: eigenvalues of A⁻¹

From Ax = λx, multiply by A⁻¹: x = λA⁻¹x. A is nonsingular, so λ ≠ 0. Divide by λ: A⁻¹x = (1/λ)x. So the eigenvalues of A⁻¹ are the reciprocals 1/λ, with the same eigenvectors.

Book Example 3: eigenvalues of a unitary matrix have |λ| = 1

Ux = λx. Taking the dagger gives x†U† = λ*x†. Multiply the two equations: x†U†Ux = λ*λ x†x. Since U†U = I, this is x†x(|λ|² − 1) = 0. And x†x = ‖x‖² > 0, so |λ| = 1.

5. Eigenvectors and their properties

  • Any nonzero multiple of an eigenvector is again an eigenvector with the same λ: A(cx) = cAx = λ(cx). We don't count these separately. An eigenvector really stands for a direction (a line). You may normalise it to length 1 if you like.
  • To find them: put each eigenvalue λᵢ back into (A − λᵢI)x = 0 and solve (Section 8).
  • Since det(A − λᵢI) = 0, its rank s is less than n, and the solution has n − s free parameters. That is the number of independent eigenvectors for λᵢ.
Multiplicity and degeneracy If λ is a root k times, k is its multiplicity. For a diagonalizable matrix (one with n independent eigenvectors; the book deals only with these in this section), an eigenvalue of multiplicity k has exactly k independent eigenvectors, i.e. rank(A − λI) = n − k. These are called degenerate eigenvectors. Any combination of degenerate eigenvectors is again an eigenvector: they fill a k-dimensional subspace.
Not every matrix is diagonalizable has λ = 1 twice, but (A − I)x = 0 gives only x = t(1, 0): one eigenvector, not two. Such "defective" matrices are treated in Section 10. In general: number of independent eigenvectors for λ ("geometric multiplicity") ≤ multiplicity as a root ("algebraic multiplicity"). Distinct eigenvalues always give independent eigenvectors, so n distinct eigenvalues ⇒ diagonalizable.

6. Feel degeneracy in 3D

A real symmetric 3×3 matrix turns the unit sphere into an ellipsoid. The eigenvectors are its principal axes, and the eigenvalues are the semi-axis lengths. When two eigenvalues are equal, the ellipsoid becomes round around the third axis, and then every direction in that equator is an eigenvector. That is degeneracy.

7. Hermitian matrices: real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors (Example 5)

Theorem (a) All eigenvalues of a hermitian matrix are real.
(b) Eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

(a) Hx = λx. Its dagger is x†H = λ*x† (using H† = H). Multiply the first by x† on the left and the second by x on the right, then subtract: (λ − λ*)x†x = 0. Since x†x > 0, λ* = λ.

(b) Hx₁ = λ₁x₁ and Hx₂ = λ₂x₂. The dagger of the second, using λ₂ real, is x₂†H = λ₂x₂†. Then x₂†Hx₁ equals both λ₁x₂†x₁ and λ₂x₂†x₁, so (λ₁ − λ₂)x₂†x₁ = 0. Since λ₁ ≠ λ₂, x₂†x₁ = 0.

Why physicists love this In quantum mechanics observables are hermitian operators. Their eigenvalues are the possible measurement results, so they must be real, and the theorem guarantees it. Orthogonal eigenvectors mean different outcomes are perfectly distinguishable states. Real symmetric matrices (inertia tensor, stress tensor, normal-mode problems) are the real special case.

The same argument for unitary matrices gives orthogonal eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues too (Exercise 17). Within a degenerate eigenvalue you can always choose orthogonal eigenvectors (Gram–Schmidt). In Example 4, (1,0,−1) and (0,1,−1) are not orthogonal to each other, but (1,0,−1) and (1,−2,1) are.

8. Diagonalization (Eqs. 37–46, Example 6)

Suppose A has n independent eigenvectors x₁, …, xₙ with Axᵢ = λᵢxᵢ. Put them as columns of P:

P = [x₁ x₂ … xₙ],    Λ = diag(λ₁, λ₂, …, λₙ)

P is nonsingular, because its columns are independent (Corollary 2 of Example 8.6). Column i of AP is Axᵢ = λᵢxᵢ, which is also column i of PΛ. So AP = PΛ, and

Diagonalization theorem
P⁻¹AP = Λ = diag(λ₁, …, λₙ)
A matrix with n linearly independent eigenvectors is similar to a diagonal matrix whose entries are its eigenvalues. Conversely, if P⁻¹AP is diagonal, the columns of P are eigenvectors (Exercise 18).
  • P is not unique. Reordering the columns reorders the eigenvalues in Λ in the same way (Eqs. 45–46). Scaling a column, or picking other vectors in a degenerate subspace, also works.
  • Hermitian A: you can choose orthonormal eigenvectors, so P is unitary and P⁻¹ = P†. Real symmetric A: P is orthogonal, P⁻¹ = Pᵀ.
  • Payoff: Aᵏ = PΛᵏP⁻¹ and f(A) = P f(Λ) P⁻¹, e.g. e^{At} = P e^{Λt} P⁻¹.

9. Commuting matrices share eigenvectors (Eqs. 47–57, Example 7)

Theorem If AB = BA (and both are diagonalizable), a common set of eigenvectors of A and B can be found. Equivalently: mutually commuting matrices can be diagonalized simultaneously, by the same P. This extends to any number of mutually commuting matrices.

Proof, part 1 (non-degenerate λ). Ax = λx. Multiply by B: BAx = A(Bx) = λ(Bx). So Bx is also an eigenvector of A with the same λ. But λ is non-degenerate, so its eigenvectors form a single line, and Bx must be a multiple of x: Bx = μx. So x is an eigenvector of B.

Part 2 (λ of multiplicity k, eigenvectors x₁, …, xₖ). Each Bxᵢ is again an eigenvector of A with eigenvalue λ, so Bxᵢ = Σⱼ cᵢⱼxⱼ (Eq. 53). Diagonalize the small k×k matrix C = [cᵢⱼ]: DCD⁻¹ = diag(μ₁, …, μₖ). Then the new combinations yᵢ = Σₗ dᵢₗxₗ are still eigenvectors of A (with λ), and also satisfy Byᵢ = μᵢyᵢ (Eq. 57). In words: inside each degenerate subspace of A, diagonalize B. ∎

The converse is Exercise 5.9: simultaneously diagonalizable ⇒ commuting. In quantum mechanics this is why commuting observables (e.g. H, L², L_z for the hydrogen atom) can be given definite values together. Their common eigenvectors are labelled by several quantum numbers at once, and the extra observables "lift" the degeneracy.

A system dy/dt = Ay (A constant) couples all the yᵢ together. Try y = x e^{wt} with x constant. Then dy/dt = wxe^{wt} and Ay = Axe^{wt}, so

Ax = wx

The allowed growth rates w are the eigenvalues of A, and the shapes x are the eigenvectors. The general solution is a combination of these "modes":

y(t) = c₁x₁e^{w₁t} + c₂x₂e^{w₂t} + … + cₙxₙe^{wₙt}

The n constants cᵢ are fixed by the initial values y(0). (Equivalently, y = e^{At}y(0) = P e^{Λt}P⁻¹ y(0).)

14. Book's Exercise 9: all 25 problems solved

1. Show that the eigenvalues of a triangular matrix are its diagonal elements.
Show solution

T − λI is also triangular, with diagonal entries tᵢᵢ − λ. The determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal (expand repeatedly along the first column). So det(T − λI) = (t₁₁ − λ)(t₂₂ − λ)⋯(tₙₙ − λ), whose roots are λ = tᵢᵢ. ∎

2. Show that a matrix is singular iff at least one eigenvalue is zero.
Show solution

det A = λ₁λ₂⋯λₙ (Eq. 10b). A product is zero ⇔ some factor is zero. So det A = 0 ⇔ some λᵢ = 0. (Directly: λ = 0 is an eigenvalue ⇔ det(A − 0I) = det A = 0.) ∎

3. Prove Example 3 (|λ| = 1 for unitary U) using Example 2 and U⁻¹ = U†.
Show solution

Let Ux = λx. U is nonsingular, so λ ≠ 0. By Example 2, U⁻¹x = (1/λ)x, i.e. U†x = (1/λ)x. Now compute x†U†x in two ways:
(i) x†(U†x) = (1/λ)x†x.
(ii) x†U†x = (x†Ux)* = (λx†x)* = λ*x†x, since x†x is real.
So 1/λ = λ*, i.e. λλ* = |λ|² = 1. ∎

4. What are the possible eigenvalues of an orthogonal matrix?
Show solution

A real orthogonal matrix is unitary, so |λ| = 1. It is also real, so complex eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs (Ex. 7). Possible eigenvalues: +1, −1, and pairs e^{±iθ}. Example: the 2D rotation has e^{±iθ} (Ex. 15a). A 3D rotation has 1, e^{iθ}, e^{−iθ}, where the eigenvalue 1 belongs to the rotation axis.

5. With H, U as in Exercise 5.18 (U = (H − iI)(H + iI)⁻¹), assume U has no eigenvalue 1. Solve for H: show H = i(I + U)(I − U)⁻¹ (the Cayley transform).
Show solution

U(H + iI) = H − iI ⇒ UH + iU = H − iI ⇒ H − UH = iU + iI ⇒ (I − U)H = i(I + U).
1 is not an eigenvalue of U, so det(I − U) ≠ 0 and (I − U)⁻¹ exists: H = i(I − U)⁻¹(I + U).
I − U and I + U commute (both are polynomials in U), hence so do (I − U)⁻¹ and (I + U). So H = i(I + U)(I − U)⁻¹. ∎

The eigenvalues map as h ↦ u = (h − i)/(h + i): the real line goes onto the unit circle, with h → ∞ corresponding to u → 1. That is why u = 1 must be excluded.

6. Prove that a real symmetric matrix is orthogonal iff its eigenvalues are all ±1.
Show solution

(⇒) Real symmetric means hermitian, so the λ are real. Orthogonal means unitary, so |λ| = 1. Real with |λ| = 1 ⇒ λ = ±1.
(⇐) A real symmetric matrix can be diagonalized by an orthogonal P (its eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormal; proved fully in Section 10): A = PΛPᵀ with Λ = diag(±1). Then Λ² = I, so AᵀA = A² = PΛ²Pᵀ = PPᵀ = I. ∎

Such a matrix is a reflection through a subspace (+1 on the subspace, −1 on its orthogonal complement), e.g. the Householder matrix of Exercise 5.14.

7. Show that the eigenvalues of a real matrix are real or come in conjugate pairs. Deduce that a real matrix of odd order has at least one real eigenvalue.
Show solution

For real A, D(λ) = det(A − λI) has real coefficients. Conjugate D(λ) = 0: D(λ*) = [D(λ)]* = 0. So if λ is a root, so is λ*. Non-real roots therefore pair up. For odd n, pairing cannot use up all n roots, so at least one root equals its own conjugate, i.e. is real. ∎ (Alternatively, a real odd-degree polynomial changes sign between −∞ and +∞.)

8. If B = P⁻¹AP, show that A and B have the same characteristic polynomial.
Show solution

B − λI = P⁻¹AP − λP⁻¹P = P⁻¹(A − λI)P. So det(B − λI) = det P⁻¹ · det(A − λI) · det P = det(A − λI). Same polynomial ⇒ same eigenvalues with the same multiplicities, hence the same trace and determinant. ∎ (The eigenvectors transform: if Ax = λx then B(P⁻¹x) = λ(P⁻¹x).)

9. Show the eigenvalues of Aᵏ (k a positive integer) are λᵏ. If A is nonsingular, show this holds for negative k too.
Show solution

Ax = λx ⇒ A²x = A(λx) = λ²x, and by induction Aᵏx = λᵏx. If A is nonsingular, A⁻¹x = λ⁻¹x (Example 2), and repeating gives A⁻ᵏx = λ⁻ᵏx. All with the same eigenvector x. ∎

To get all eigenvalues of Aᵏ with multiplicities (not just "λᵏ is one of them"), diagonalize: Aᵏ = PΛᵏP⁻¹, so the spectrum is exactly {λᵢᵏ}.

10. If λ is an eigenvalue of A, show f(λ) = a₀ + a₁λ + … + aₖλᵏ is an eigenvalue of f(A) = a₀I + a₁A + … + aₖAᵏ.
Show solution

f(A)x = a₀x + a₁Ax + … + aₖAᵏx = (a₀ + a₁λ + … + aₖλᵏ)x = f(λ)x, using Ex. 9. ∎

Corollary (Cayley–Hamilton preview): if f is the characteristic polynomial, then f(λᵢ) = 0 for all i, and for diagonalizable A this gives f(A) = 0.

11. Show that any n-vector is an eigenvector of a constant (scalar) matrix cI.
Show solution

(cI)x = cx for every x. So every nonzero x is an eigenvector with eigenvalue c, which has multiplicity n. Conversely, if every vector is an eigenvector of A, then A = cI. (A must send e₁ and e₁ + e₂ to multiples of themselves, which forces equal eigenvalues, and so on.)

12. Show a nonzero x with Ax = 0 exists iff A is singular.
Show solution

Ax = 0 is a homogeneous system: it has a nontrivial solution ⇔ det A = 0 (Section 8). In eigen-language, Ax = 0·x, so 0 is an eigenvalue and x an eigenvector (Ex. 2). ∎

16. x is an eigenvector of hermitian H. If y ⊥ x, show Hy ⊥ x.
Show solution

x†(Hy) = (H†x)†y = (Hx)†y = (λx)†y = λ*x†y = λx†y = 0, using H† = H and λ real. ∎

So H maps the subspace orthogonal to an eigenvector into itself. Repeating this argument in that smaller subspace proves that every hermitian matrix has a full orthonormal set of eigenvectors.

17. Prove that eigenvectors of a unitary matrix for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.
Show solution

Ux₁ = λ₁x₁ and Ux₂ = λ₂x₂, with |λᵢ| = 1. Then x₂†x₁ = x₂†U†Ux₁ = (Ux₂)†(Ux₁) = λ₂*λ₁ x₂†x₁. Since λ₂* = 1/λ₂, this gives (1 − λ₁/λ₂)x₂†x₁ = 0. λ₁ ≠ λ₂ ⇒ x₂†x₁ = 0. ∎

18. If P⁻¹AP = Λ is diagonal, prove the columns of P are eigenvectors of A.
Show solution

AP = PΛ. Column i of AP is A pᵢ. Column i of PΛ is λᵢpᵢ, because Λ just scales the columns. So Apᵢ = λᵢpᵢ, and pᵢ ≠ 0 since P is nonsingular. ∎ The diagonal entries of Λ are the eigenvalues, in the order of the columns.

21. Show (A + 3I)(B − 12I) = 0 for the matrices of Eq. (58). Hence (a) if x is not an eigenvector of B with eigenvalue 12, (B − 12I)x is an eigenvector of A; (b) if y is not an eigenvector of A with eigenvalue −3, (A + 3I)y is an eigenvector of B.

The identity. 23. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the n×n matrix with q on the diagonal and p everywhere else (p ≠ 0).
Show solution

Write A = (q − p)I + pJ, where J is the all-ones matrix. J = uuᵀ with u = (1, …, 1), so Ju = nu, and Jv = 0 for every v ⊥ u (vectors with Σvᵢ = 0).

  • λ = q + (n − 1)p, eigenvector (1, 1, …, 1), non-degenerate.
  • λ = q − p, multiplicity n − 1, eigenvectors: all v with v₁ + … + vₙ = 0. A basis is e₁ − e₂, e₁ − e₃, …, e₁ − eₙ.

24. Show Tr(Aᵏ) = Σᵢ λᵢᵏ.
Show solution

The eigenvalues of Aᵏ are λᵢᵏ (Ex. 9; with multiplicities, via Aᵏ = PΛᵏP⁻¹, or in general via triangularization). The trace is the sum of the eigenvalues (Eq. 10a), so Tr Aᵏ = Σλᵢᵏ. Alternatively, Tr(PΛᵏP⁻¹) = Tr(ΛᵏP⁻¹P) = Tr Λᵏ. ∎

Use: for 2×2, λ₁² + λ₂² = Tr(A²), giving λ₁λ₂ = [(Tr A)² − Tr(A²)]/2 = det A ✓.

25. A 2×2 matrix has trace t and determinant d. Find its eigenvalues.
Show solution

λ₁ + λ₂ = t and λ₁λ₂ = d, so they are the roots of λ² − tλ + d = 0:

λ = [t ± √(t² − 4d)]/2

t² > 4d: two real eigenvalues. t² = 4d: a repeated eigenvalue. t² < 4d: a complex pair. This is the classification used in stability analysis of 2D dynamical systems.

17. Interesting facts

Where "eigen" came from David Hilbert used "Eigenwert" (own value) around 1904 while studying integral equations. The English hybrid "eigenvalue" stuck, although "characteristic value" and "proper value" were also used for decades.
Google's PageRank The original Google ranking of web pages is the eigenvector, with eigenvalue 1, of a gigantic "link matrix" with billions of rows. It is found by the power method: multiply any vector by the matrix again and again, and it turns into the dominant eigenvector.
Quantum mechanics is an eigenvalue problem The time-independent Schrödinger equation Hψ = Eψ is exactly Ax = λx. Allowed energies are eigenvalues and stationary states are eigenvectors. Heisenberg's 1925 matrix mechanics computed atomic spectra literally by diagonalizing infinite matrices.
Bridges and resonance Every structure has normal modes, the eigenvectors of its stiffness and mass matrices. If wind or marching feet push at one of the natural frequencies, the amplitude builds up. Engineers compute these eigenvalues to keep them away from expected forcing frequencies.
No formula for n ≥ 5 Abel and Galois proved that polynomials of degree 5 or more have no general formula in radicals. So eigenvalues of 5×5 and larger matrices must in general be found numerically (QR algorithm, 1961), not by a formula.
