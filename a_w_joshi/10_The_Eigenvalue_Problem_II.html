0. Roadmap

In Section 9 you found eigenvalues and eigenvectors and used them to diagonalize matrices. This section answers the questions left open there:

  • Does every matrix satisfy some polynomial equation? (Yes: its own characteristic equation. This is the Cayley–Hamilton theorem.)
  • What is the smallest such polynomial? (The minimal polynomial.)
  • Exactly when can a matrix be diagonalized? (When its minimal polynomial has no repeated roots.)
  • What does a matrix that cannot be diagonalized look like? (Too few eigenvectors. The best you can do is a triangular form.)
  • Which matrices are always diagonalizable, and by a unitary matrix? (Hermitian, unitary, and in general normal matrices.)
  • How do you cut a high power like A⁵ down to a small polynomial? (Divide by the minimal polynomial and keep the remainder.)
n²+1 matrices are dependent→D(A) = 0 (Cayley–Hamilton)→smallest p(A) = 0: minimal polynomial→distinct roots ⇔ diagonalizable→NN† = N†N ⇒ unitary diagonalization→f(A) = remainder
Needed from earlier
  • Characteristic polynomial (Section 9, Eq. 9.5): D(λ) = det(A − λI) = c₀λⁿ + c₁λⁿ⁻¹ + … + cₙ, with c₀ = (−1)ⁿ. Its roots are the eigenvalues.
  • Number of eigenvectors for an eigenvalue λ = n − rank(A − λI) (Section 8).
  • Adjoint (matrix of cofactors): M · adj M = (det M) I (Section 4, Eq. 5.6b in the book).
  • Similarity transformation P⁻¹AP and diagonalization P⁻¹AP = Λ when the columns of P are n independent eigenvectors (Section 9).

1. Why some polynomial in A must vanish

The space of all n×n matrices has dimension n² (Section 3: the n² "unit" matrices Eij form a basis). So any n² + 1 matrices are linearly dependent.

Take the n² + 1 matrices

I, A, A², A³, …, AN,    N = n²

They must be dependent, so there are numbers ci, not all zero, with

c₀I + c₁A + c₂A² + … + cNAN = 0    (1)

So every square matrix satisfies some polynomial equation of degree at most n². For a 3×3 matrix that guarantee is degree 9. The Cayley–Hamilton theorem gives a much better bound: degree n (3 instead of 9).

Plain picture Keep multiplying by A: I, A, A², … Each one is a point in an n²-dimensional space. Sooner or later a new power has to land in the span of the earlier ones. Cayley–Hamilton says this happens by the n-th power at the latest. The minimal polynomial tells you exactly when it first happens.

2. The Cayley–Hamilton theorem (Eqs. 2–10)

Theorem (Cayley–Hamilton) If the characteristic polynomial of an n×n matrix A is
D(λ) = det(A − λI) = c₀λⁿ + c₁λⁿ⁻¹ + c₂λⁿ⁻² + … + cn−1λ + cn
then replacing λ by A (and the constant cn by cnI) gives the zero matrix:
c₀Aⁿ + c₁Aⁿ⁻¹ + … + cn−1A + cnI = 0    (2)
In short: every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation. If λ₁, …, λₙ are the eigenvalues (repeats included), the same statement reads
(A − λ₁I)(A − λ₂I)…(A − λₙI) = 0    (8)

Case: 2×2 matrices (memorize this)

For A = [[a, b], [c, d]]: D(λ) = λ² − (a+d)λ + (ad − bc) = λ² − (Tr A)λ + det A. So

A² − (Tr A) A + (det A) I = 0

Check it with A = [[1, 2], [3, 4]] (Tr = 5, det = −2): A² = [[7, 10], [15, 22]], and (Tr A)A − (det A)I = 5A + 2I = [[5+2, 10], [15, 20+2]] = [[7, 10], [15, 22]]. ✓

Case: 3×3 matrices

Using the monic form det(λI − A) = λ³ − (Tr A)λ² + S₂λ − det A, where S₂ = sum of the three principal 2×2 minors:

A³ − (Tr A)A² + S₂ A − (det A) I = 0
Sign convention The book uses det(A − λI), whose leading coefficient is c₀ = (−1)ⁿ. Many other books use det(λI − A), which is monic. The two differ only by an overall factor (−1)ⁿ, so "= 0" is the same equation. Don't let the sign confuse you.

The proof, step by step

  1. Let B(λ) be the adjoint of A − λI: the transpose of its matrix of cofactors. The adjoint rule gives
    (A − λI) B(λ) = D(λ) I    (3)
  2. Each entry of B(λ) is a cofactor, i.e. an (n−1)×(n−1) determinant of entries like aij or aii − λ. So it is a polynomial in λ of degree at most n − 1. Split B(λ) into powers of λ with constant matrix coefficients:
    B(λ) = λⁿ⁻¹B₀ + λⁿ⁻²B₁ + … + λBn−2 + Bn−1    (4)
  3. Multiply out the left side of (3):
    (A − λI)B(λ) = −λⁿB₀ + λⁿ⁻¹(AB₀ − B₁) + λⁿ⁻²(AB₁ − B₂) + … + λ(ABn−2 − Bn−1) + ABn−1   (5)
    The right side is D(λ)I = c₀λⁿI + c₁λⁿ⁻¹I + … + cnI.  (6)
  4. Match the coefficients of each power of λ:
    −B₀ = c₀I
    AB₀ − B₁ = c₁I
    AB₁ − B₂ = c₂I
    …
    ABn−2 − Bn−1 = cn−1I
    ABn−1 = cnI        (7)
  5. Multiply the first line on the left by Aⁿ, the second by Aⁿ⁻¹, …, the last but one by A, the last by I, and add. The left side telescopes: −AⁿB₀ + AⁿB₀ − Aⁿ⁻¹B₁ + Aⁿ⁻¹B₁ − … all cancel, leaving 0. The right side is c₀Aⁿ + c₁Aⁿ⁻¹ + … + cnI. So that sum is 0. ∎
The tempting wrong proof "Put λ = A in D(λ) = det(A − λI). Then D(A) = det(A − A·I) = det(0) = 0." This is wrong. det(A − λI) is a number for each λ, and putting a matrix inside a determinant like this makes no sense. Also, D(A) is supposed to be the zero matrix, not the number 0. The real proof above is needed.

A² − (2q + p)A + (q² + pq − 2p²)I = 0    (13)

The general definition

Let A (order n) have l distinct eigenvalues λ₁, …, λl with multiplicities d₁, …, dl, so d₁ + … + dl = n. Cayley–Hamilton says

(λ₁I − A)d₁(λ₂I − A)d₂…(λlI − A)dl = 0    (16)
Definition: minimal polynomial If r₁, r₂, …, rl are the smallest positive integers with
(λ₁I − A)r₁(λ₂I − A)r₂…(λlI − A)rl = 0,    1 ≤ rμ ≤ dμ   (17)
then p(λ) = (λ₁ − λ)r₁(λ₂ − λ)r₂…(λl − λ)rl is the minimal polynomial of A.  (18)
(It is usually made monic, i.e. leading coefficient 1. It is the lowest-degree monic polynomial with p(A) = 0.)

Properties

  • Every eigenvalue appears at least once: rμ ≥ 1. (If Ax = λx then p(A)x = p(λ)x. Since p(A) = 0 and x ≠ 0, p(λ) must be 0.)
  • It divides the characteristic polynomial exactly, since rμ ≤ dμ. More strongly, it divides every polynomial f with f(A) = 0.
  • Degree m satisfies l ≤ m ≤ n.
  • If all eigenvalues are distinct, every dμ = 1, which forces rμ = 1. So minimal polynomial = characteristic polynomial (as in Example 1).
  • Similar matrices have the same minimal polynomial: p(P⁻¹AP) = P⁻¹p(A)P.
  • rμ = size of the largest Jordan block for λμ. This is beyond the book, but useful for NET.

4. The condition for diagonalizability (Example 3)

Theorem (stated without proof in the book) An n×n matrix is diagonalizable (equivalently, it has n linearly independent eigenvectors) if and only if its minimal polynomial is
p(λ) = (λ₁ − λ)(λ₂ − λ)…(λl − λ)
with λμ the distinct eigenvalues. In words: diagonalizable ⇔ the minimal polynomial has no repeated roots.

Equivalent tests (use whichever is quickest)

  1. The minimal polynomial has only simple roots.
  2. For every eigenvalue: geometric multiplicity = algebraic multiplicity, i.e. n − rank(A − λI) = dλ.
  3. Σλ [n − rank(A − λI)] = n: the eigenvectors fill the whole space.
  4. (A − λ₁I)(A − λ₂I)…(A − λlI) = 0, one factor per distinct eigenvalue.

Shortcuts

  • All eigenvalues distinct ⇒ diagonalizable (sufficient, not necessary).
  • Hermitian, unitary, real symmetric, orthogonal, normal ⇒ always diagonalizable (Section 7).
  • A² = A (projector), A² = I (involution), Ak = I: these polynomials have simple roots ⇒ diagonalizable.
  • Ak = 0 with A ≠ 0 (nilpotent) ⇒ minimal polynomial λr with r ≥ 2 ⇒ not diagonalizable.
  • Only one eigenvalue a, but A ≠ aI ⇒ not diagonalizable. (If it were, P⁻¹AP = aI would give A = P(aI)P⁻¹ = aI.)

Careful with the book's closing sentence The book says that a matrix similar to a triangular matrix with two equal diagonal entries (i-th and j-th) and a nonzero ij-th entry is nondiagonalizable. That is true when the two equal entries are neighbours (as in Eq. 25), and always true for 2×2. In general it can fail. Counter-example:
T = [[1, 1, 1], [0, 2, 1], [0, 0, 1]]
Here t₁₁ = t₃₃ = 1 and t₁₃ ≠ 0. But T − I = [[0, 1, 1], [0, 1, 1], [0, 0, 0]] has rank 1, so λ = 1 has 2 eigenvectors: (1, 0, 0) and (0, 1, −1). T is diagonalizable. The safe test is always rank(A − λI) or the minimal polynomial.

2×2 special case (Exercise 4)

A real 2×2 matrix [[a, b], [c, d]] that is not already diagonal is nondiagonalizable if and only if (a − d)² + 4bc = 0. This is the discriminant of λ² − (a+d)λ + (ad − bc), so the condition means "a repeated eigenvalue, but the matrix is not a multiple of I". Try it in the 2D lab below.

7. Diagonalization of hermitian (and normal) matrices

Theorem Every hermitian matrix H can be diagonalized by a unitary matrix U:
U†HU = Λ (real diagonal),   the columns of U are orthonormal eigenvectors of H.  (26)

The argument (book's outline)

  1. Every matrix can be brought to triangular form by a similarity transformation (we saw this in Example 3). With a little more care (Gram–Schmidt on the columns), the transformation can be made unitary. This is Schur's theorem.
  2. Suppose H has k orthonormal eigenvectors. Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are already orthogonal (Section 9, Example 5). Those sharing an eigenvalue can be made orthogonal by Schmidt's process. Add n − k more orthonormal vectors to get a unitary U with U†HU = T triangular.
  3. Take the hermitian conjugate: T† = (U†HU)† = U†H†U = U†HU = T. So T is hermitian and triangular at the same time.
  4. A triangular matrix that equals its own conjugate transpose has zeros both above and below the diagonal, and real diagonal entries (tii = tii*). So T is real diagonal, and all n columns of U are eigenvectors. In particular k = n: a hermitian matrix is never defective. ∎

Normal matrices: the general rule

Definition N is normal if it commutes with its hermitian conjugate: NN† = N†N.
Theorem (spectral theorem) An n×n matrix has a complete set of n orthonormal eigenvectors (that is, it is unitarily diagonalizable) if and only if it is normal.

Why "only if": if U†NU = Λ, then N = UΛU† and N† = UΛ*U†. So NN† = UΛΛ*U† = UΛ*ΛU† = N†N, because diagonal matrices commute.

8. Matrix polynomials (Eqs. 27–34, Example 4)

An expression F(A) = akAk + ak−1Ak−1 + … + a₁A + a₀I is a matrix polynomial of degree k.  (27)

Key fact If the minimal polynomial p has degree m, then every polynomial in A equals a polynomial of degree ≤ m − 1.
Method: divide F(λ) by p(λ): F(λ) = Q(λ)p(λ) + R(λ), with deg R ≤ m − 1. Since p(A) = 0, we get F(A) = R(A).
(You may also divide by the characteristic polynomial. The remainder then has degree ≤ n − 1, which is fine but sometimes larger than needed.)

Worked Example 4 (book)

A is the matrix of Example 3. Its minimal polynomial is (λ − 1)(λ − 2)² = λ³ − 5λ² + 8λ − 4, so

A³ − 5A² + 8A − 4I = 0   (29)

Reduce F(A) = 2A⁵ + 4A⁴ − 6A³ + A² − 3A − 3I.  (31)

  1. Kill the A⁵ term: subtract 2A² × (29), i.e. 2A⁵ − 10A⁴ + 16A³ − 8A² = 0  (30):
    F(A) = 14A⁴ − 22A³ + 9A² − 3A − 3I.  (32)
  2. Kill A⁴: subtract 14A × (29) = 14A⁴ − 70A³ + 112A² − 56A:
    F(A) = 48A³ − 103A² + 53A − 3I.  (33)
  3. Kill A³: subtract 48 × (29) = 48A³ − 240A² + 384A − 192I:
    F(A) = 137A² − 331A + 189I.  (34)

This is ordinary polynomial long division, with the quotient 2λ² + 14λ + 48 thrown away.

Faster trick: match values at the eigenvalues Write F(A) = αA² + βA + γI. The polynomial F(λ) − (αλ² + βλ + γ) must vanish at the roots of the minimal polynomial, with multiplicity:
  • λ = 1: F(1) = 2 + 4 − 6 + 1 − 3 − 3 = −5 = α + β + γ
  • λ = 2: F(2) = 64 + 64 − 48 + 4 − 6 − 3 = 75 = 4α + 2β + γ
  • λ = 2 is double, so the derivatives must also match: F′(λ) = 10λ⁴ + 16λ³ − 18λ² + 2λ − 3, F′(2) = 160 + 128 − 72 + 4 − 3 = 217 = 4α + β
Solving: subtract the first two to get 3α + β = 80. With 4α + β = 217 this gives α = 137, β = −331, γ = −5 − 137 + 331 = 189. ✓ Same answer, without long division.

9. Spectral projectors (Exercises 6 and 7): splitting A into pieces

Let a 3×3 matrix A have distinct eigenvalues λ₁, λ₂, λ₃. Define

P₁ = (A − λ₂I)(A − λ₃I) / [(λ₁ − λ₂)(λ₁ − λ₃)],   and P₂, P₃ the same way (cyclically).

These are the Lagrange interpolation polynomials with A put in place of λ. They satisfy:

  • P₁ + P₂ + P₃ = I (the pieces add up to the identity)
  • Pᵢ² = Pᵢ (each is a projector), and PᵢPⱼ = 0 for i ≠ j
  • λ₁P₁ + λ₂P₂ + λ₃P₃ = A, and in general f(A) = f(λ₁)P₁ + f(λ₂)P₂ + f(λ₃)P₃

Why: Pᵢ acting on eigenvector xj gives [Πₖ≠ᵢ(λⱼ − λₖ)/(λᵢ − λₖ)]xj. That is xj if j = i and 0 otherwise. So Pᵢ keeps the xi part of a vector and deletes the rest. Two matrices that agree on a basis of eigenvectors are equal, which proves every identity above.

2. Show that A = HU (H hermitian, U unitary) is normal iff H² commutes with U.
Show solution

A† = U†H† = U†H. Then AA† = HUU†H = H·I·H = H², and A†A = U†HHU = U†H²U.

Normal ⇔ H² = U†H²U. Multiply on the left by U: UH² = H²U. Every step can be reversed (multiply by U† again). ∎

4. A real 2×2 matrix [[a, b], [c, d]] that is not diagonal: show it is nondiagonalizable iff (a − d)² + 4bc = 0.
Show solution

Characteristic polynomial λ² − (a + d)λ + (ad − bc). Discriminant Δ = (a + d)² − 4(ad − bc) = (a − d)² + 4bc.

If Δ ≠ 0 there are two distinct eigenvalues (real or complex), so A is diagonalizable.

If Δ = 0 there is a single eigenvalue μ = (a + d)/2. A is diagonalizable ⇔ A − μI = 0 ⇔ A = μI. But A is not diagonal (b or c ≠ 0), so A ≠ μI and A is nondiagonalizable. Hence nondiagonalizable ⇔ Δ = 0. ∎

Example: [[3, 1], [−1, 1]]: (3 − 1)² + 4(1)(−1) = 0, so it is defective with λ = 2, 2.

6. A = matrix of Eq. (9). P₁ = (A + 6I)(A + I)/50, P₂ = (A − 4I)(A + I)/50, P₃ = −(A − 4I)(A + 6I)/25. Show (a) P₁ + P₂ + P₃ = I, (b) 4P₁ − 6P₂ − P₃ = A, (c) 16P₁ + 36P₂ + P₃ = A², (d) Pᵢ² = Pᵢ.
Show solution

These are the projectors of Section 9 with λ₁ = 4, λ₂ = −6, λ₃ = −1. The denominators: (4 + 6)(4 + 1) = 50, (−6 − 4)(−6 + 1) = 50, (−1 − 4)(−1 + 6) = −25 ✓.

Explicit: P₁ = (A + 6I)(A + I)/50 = [[−5, −10, 5], [5, 10, −5], [−20, −20, 10]]·[[−10, −10, 5], [5, 5, −5], [−20, −20, 5]]/50 = [[−100, −100, 50], [100, 100, −50], [−100, −100, 50]]/50, so

P₁ = [[−2, −2, 1], [2, 2, −1], [−2, −2, 1]],   P₂ = [[0, 0, 0], [1, 1, 0], [2, 2, 0]],   P₃ = [[3, 2, −1], [−3, −2, 1], [0, 0, 0]]

(a) Adding: [[−2+0+3, −2+0+2, 1+0−1], [2+1−3, 2+1−2, −1+0+1], [−2+2+0, −2+2+0, 1+0+0]] = I ✓. Each Pᵢ has trace 1, since it is a rank-1 projector. You can check the rest by hand or in Lab G. Here is a cleaner proof using eigenvectors:

Let x₁, x₂, x₃ be the eigenvectors. Pᵢxⱼ = δᵢⱼxⱼ (Section 9). Apply each side to x₁, x₂, x₃:

  • (a) (P₁ + P₂ + P₃)xⱼ = xⱼ = Ixⱼ
  • (b) (4P₁ − 6P₂ − P₃)xⱼ = λⱼxⱼ = Axⱼ
  • (c) gives λⱼ²xⱼ = A²xⱼ
  • (d) Pᵢ²xⱼ = δᵢⱼxⱼ = Pᵢxⱼ

Two matrices that agree on a basis are equal. ∎ (Alternatively, each identity is a polynomial identity in λ of degree ≤ 2 that holds at the three points 4, −6, −1, so it holds identically.)

7. General 3×3 with distinct λ₁, λ₂, λ₃ and Pᵢ as in Section 9. (a) Prove (i) ΣPᵢ = I, (ii) ΣλᵢPᵢ = A, (iii) Σλᵢ²Pᵢ = A², (iv) Pᵢ² = Pᵢ. (b) Express A³ via the Pᵢ.
Show solution

(a) Polynomial argument. Let Lᵢ(λ) be the Lagrange polynomials, e.g. L₁(λ) = (λ − λ₂)(λ − λ₃)/[(λ₁ − λ₂)(λ₁ − λ₃)]. Then Lᵢ(λⱼ) = δᵢⱼ.

  • (i) L₁ + L₂ + L₃ − 1 has degree ≤ 2 and three roots λ₁, λ₂, λ₃, so it is identically 0. Put λ → A: ΣPᵢ = I.
  • (ii), (iii): the same with λ and λ² in place of 1.
  • (iv) Lᵢ² − Lᵢ vanishes at all three λⱼ (since Lᵢ(λⱼ) is 0 or 1), so it is divisible by the characteristic polynomial (λ − λ₁)(λ − λ₂)(λ − λ₃). By Cayley–Hamilton that polynomial gives 0 at A, so Pᵢ² − Pᵢ = 0.

(b) Apply to eigenvectors, or use the same argument: A³ = λ₁³P₁ + λ₂³P₂ + λ₃³P₃. More generally f(A) = Σf(λᵢ)Pᵢ for any polynomial f.

P6. A ≠ 0, I and A² = A. Is A diagonalizable? What are its eigenvalues? Show Tr A = rank A. projector
Show solution

λ² − λ = λ(λ − 1) kills A. Since A ≠ 0, I, this is the minimal polynomial. Its roots are simple ⇒ diagonalizable, with eigenvalues 0 and 1 only. Then A ~ diag(1, …, 1, 0, …, 0), so Tr A = number of 1's = rank A.

P7. A real 3×3 matrix A ≠ 0 satisfies A³ = 0. Is it diagonalizable? What are Tr A and det A? nilpotent
Show solution

The minimal polynomial divides λ³ and is not λ (since A ≠ 0), so it is λ² or λ³, with a repeated root ⇒ not diagonalizable. All eigenvalues are 0 ⇒ Tr A = 0, det A = 0.

P8. A² = I but A ≠ ±I. Diagonalizable? involution
Show solution

Minimal polynomial = λ² − 1 = (λ − 1)(λ + 1): simple roots ⇒ yes. Eigenvalues ±1 (both occur). Example: the swap matrix [[0, 1], [1, 0]].

Show solution

First: single eigenvalue 2, diagonalizable ⇔ matrix = 2I ⇔ k = 0. Second: eigenvalues 2 ≠ 3 are distinct ⇒ diagonalizable for every k.

P10. Is N = [[1, 1], [−1, 1]] normal? Find a unitary U diagonalizing it. normal
Show solution

NNᵀ = [[2, 0], [0, 2]] = NᵀN ⇒ normal. Eigenvalues 1 ± i. For 1 + i: (N − (1 + i)I)x = 0 ⇒ −ix₁ + x₂ = 0 ⇒ x = (1, i)/√2. For 1 − i: (1, −i)/√2. These are orthogonal: (1, i)†(1, −i) = 1 + (−i)(−i) = 1 − 1 = 0. U = (1/√2)[[1, 1], [i, −i]], U†NU = diag(1 + i, 1 − i).

P12. H = [[2, i], [−i, 2]]. Diagonalize it by a unitary matrix. hermitian
Show solution

Tr = 4, det = 4 − 1 = 3 ⇒ λ = 1, 3. λ = 3: −x₁ + ix₂ = 0 ⇒ (i, 1)/√2. λ = 1: x₁ + ix₂ = 0 ⇒ (−i, 1)/√2. U = (1/√2)[[i, −i], [1, 1]] ⇒ U†HU = diag(3, 1). Orthogonality: (i, 1)†(−i, 1) = (−i)(−i) + 1 = 0 ✓.

P13. J₄ = 4×4 matrix of all 1's. Minimal polynomial? Diagonalizable? Eigenvalues? all-ones
Show solution

J² = 4J (each entry of J² is 1 + 1 + 1 + 1). So J² − 4J = 0, i.e. λ(λ − 4) kills J, and since J ≠ 0, 4I, it is minimal. Simple roots ⇒ diagonalizable. Rank 1 ⇒ eigenvalue 0 three times. Trace 4 ⇒ the remaining eigenvalue is 4.

P14. N = [[0, 1], [0, 0]]. Find eN and etA for A = [[λ, 1], [0, λ]]. functions
Show solution

N² = 0 ⇒ eN = I + N = [[1, 1], [0, 1]]. A = λI + N with λI and N commuting ⇒ etA = eλt(I + tN) = eλt[[1, t], [0, 1]]. The "t eλt" term is the signature of a defective matrix. It is the same as the critically damped oscillator in ODEs.

14. Interesting facts

History Cayley stated the theorem in 1858 and checked it only for 2×2 and 3×3 matrices, writing that he had "not thought it necessary to undertake the labour of a formal proof". Hamilton had proved a version for quaternions earlier. Frobenius gave the first general proof in 1878.
Defective matrices are rare but important Change the entries of a defective matrix by a tiny random amount and it almost surely becomes diagonalizable, because the repeated eigenvalue splits. In the 2D lab, nudge c away from 0 in the shear preset. But the two eigenvectors are then nearly parallel, so P is nearly singular and the computation is numerically unstable. This is why physicists like hermitian matrices.
Physics where it shows up Critically damped oscillators (te−γt terms) come from a defective 2×2 matrix. In quantum mechanics, observables are hermitian, so they always have a complete orthonormal set of eigenstates. That is the spectral theorem at work. Non-hermitian "exceptional points", where two eigenvectors merge exactly like in the 2D lab, are studied in optics and open quantum systems.
Companion matrix Every monic polynomial is the characteristic and minimal polynomial of its companion matrix. So minimal polynomials can be anything, and finding eigenvalues is as hard as finding polynomial roots. That is why there is no algebraic formula for eigenvalues of 5×5 matrices (Abel–Ruffini).
Computing the characteristic polynomial without determinants The Faddeev–LeVerrier algorithm used in Lab A computes the coefficients and the matrices Bk of the proof (Eq. 4) using only matrix products and traces. It gives A⁻¹ for free.
Works over any number system Cayley–Hamilton holds over any commutative ring, so the proof works even when the entries are polynomials or integers mod p.
