0. Roadmap

Coordinates are just labels. Polar coordinates (r, θ) say nothing by themselves about distances: a step dθ is a short distance near the origin and a long one far away. The metric tensor gij is the dictionary that turns coordinate steps into real distances:

ds² = gij dxi dxj

Once you have gij you can measure everything: lengths, angles, areas and volumes. You can also convert any contravariant index into a covariant one and back. This is why it is called the fundamental tensor.

coordinates xi→ds² = gijdxidxj→gij = inverse matrix→Ai = gijAj, Ai = gijAj→|A|², cos θ, √g dV

1. The metric and Riemannian space (Eqs. 1, 17)

Definition If the squared distance between neighbouring points xi and xi + dxi is a quadratic form
ds² = gij dxi dxj   (1)
with g = det(gij) ≠ 0, the space is a Riemannian space. The gij may depend on position. If they are all independent of xi, the space is called Euclidean (in the book's sense).
  • Symmetric by choice: ds² sees only gij + gji (Exercise 17.1), so one takes gij = gji.  (17)   Then gij is a symmetric covariant tensor of rank two: the fundamental or metric tensor. In N dimensions it has N(N+1)/2 independent components.
  • Orthogonal coordinates: gij = 0 for i ≠ j. Then ds² = h₁²(dx¹)² + h₂²(dx²)² + …, with scale factors hi = √gii.
  • Cartesian coordinates: gii = 1 and gij = 0 (i ≠ j), i.e. gij = δij.
A note on the word "Euclidean" Joshi calls a space Euclidean when the gij are constants in the chosen coordinates. The flat plane in polar coordinates has g22 = r², which is not constant, yet the plane is obviously flat. Whether a space is truly curved is decided by the curvature tensor (Section 24), not by the look of gij in one coordinate system. The sphere's surface is genuinely curved: no coordinates can make its metric constant.
Volume element The area or volume of a small coordinate cell is dV = √|g| dx¹dx²…dxN. Polar: r dr dθ. Spherical: r² sin θ dr dθ dφ. This is the √g used later in div and ∇² (Exercise 15.8).

Example 2: polar coordinates, the picture (Fig. 9)

From P = (r, θ) go to R = (r + dr, θ + dθ). The step has a radial part PQ = dr and a sideways part QR = (r + dr)dθ ≈ r dθ (dropping second-order terms). PQR is a right angle, so by Pythagoras PR² = dr² + r²dθ². The factor r² in g22 says that the same dθ covers more ground farther out.

Example 4: a non-orthogonal system

x = vw, y = uw, z = uv.  (14)   Then dx = w dv + v dw, dy = w du + u dw, dz = v du + u dv. Squaring and adding:

ds² = (v² + w²)du² + (w² + u²)dv² + (u² + v²)dw² + 2uv du dv + 2vw dv dw + 2uw du dw   (15)
[gij] =
v² + w²uvuw
uvw² + u²vw
uwvwu² + v²
,   g = 4u²v²w²   (16)

Short cut: gij = (∂x/∂qi)·(∂x/∂qj), the dot products of the tangent vectors. In matrix form g = JᵀJ with J = ∂(x, y, z)/∂(u, v, w), so g = (det J)². Here det J = 2uvw, so g = 4u²v²w² ✓. The off-diagonal terms uv, vw, uw ≠ 0 mean the coordinate lines are not perpendicular.

Example 5: gij in spherical coordinates

For a diagonal metric the inverse is just the reciprocals:

g11 = 1,   g22 = 1/r²,   g33 = 1/(r²sin²θ),   gij = 0 (i ≠ j)   (20)

For Example 4, gij is no longer simple: e.g. g11 = (u² + v² + w²)/(4v²w²) and g12 = (w² − u² − v²)/(4uvw²).

4. The contravariant metric tensor gij (Eqs. 18–20)

gij is symmetric with g ≠ 0, so by Section 17 it has a conjugate:

gij = gji = (cofactor of gij)/g,    gijgjk = δki   (18, 19)
  • As matrices, [gij] = [gij]⁻¹.
  • gij and gij are best thought of as the covariant and contravariant components of the same tensor, the metric.
  • Mixed components: gik = gki = δki (27). The mixed metric is the Kronecker delta.
  • Exercise 8 consequence: if you "lower" δ, you get δjk ≡ gjlδlk = gjk, the metric itself. It equals the identity matrix only in Cartesian coordinates.

5. Associate tensors: raising and lowering indices (Eqs. 21–27)

Lowering and raising
Aj = gijAi  (lower),     Ak = gjkAj  (raise)   (21, 22)
Ai and Ai are the contravariant and covariant components of one and the same vector. They are called associate tensors. Raising undoes lowering: Ajgjk = Aigijgjk = Aiδki = Ak.

This is the precise version of the picture in Section 15: contravariant components lie along the coordinate directions and covariant components are perpendicular projections. In polar coordinates: Ar = Ar, Aθ = r²Aθ.

A rank-2 tensor has four associates

Aijgjk = Ai·k (lower the second),   Aijgik = Ak·j (lower the first),   Aijgikgjl = Akl   (23)

The dot marks the empty place so you can see which index was moved. In matrix language, with G = [gij]: Ai·k = (AG), Ak·j = (GA), Akl = (GAG). Inverse relations (26): Ai·kgkj = Aij, Ak·igkj = Aji, Aijgikgjl = Akl.

6. The order of indices matters (Eqs. 24–32)

Ai·j = Aikgkj,    Aj·i = Akigkj   (24)

These are different, Ai·j ≠ Aj·i (25), unless Aik is symmetric. So for a non-symmetric tensor, writing Aij without saying which slot came first is ambiguous. In matrix language this is AG versus (GA)ᵀ = GᵀAᵀ = GAᵀ.

For a rank-4 tensor Aijkl, lowering the first, second, third or fourth index gives four different mixed tensors (28a–d), e.g. Ai·jkl = Apjklgpi and Ai·j·kl = Aipklgpj. Swapping i and j in the second one does not give the first (29, 30) unless A is symmetric in its first two indices. Lowering two indices gives six forms (31), and lowering all four gives the single covariant Aijkl = Apqrsgpigqjgrkgsl (32).

Rule Keep track of the left-to-right position of every index, whether it is up or down. Upper indices among themselves, lower indices among themselves, and the relative order of upper and lower indices are all significant.

Example 6: you may move a dummy pair up/down freely

AijBij = AijBij.  (33)   Proof: write Aij = Aklgkiglj and Bij = Bmngmignj (34). Multiply: gkigmi = δkm and gljgnj = δln, leaving AklBkl. ∎ In general, in any contraction you can swap the heights of the two members of a dummy pair (the "see-saw" rule).

Example 7: in Cartesian coordinates the distinction vanishes

Ai = gijAj and gij = δij give Ai = Ai.  (35, 36)   The same holds for every index of every tensor (Exercise 7). That is why elementary physics never needed upper and lower indices.

7. Length of a vector and angle between vectors (Eqs. 37–39)

A² = AiAi = gijAiAj = gijAiAj   (37)
AB cos θ = AiBi = gijAiBj = gijAiBj,    cos θ = AiBi / √(AjAj · BkBk)   (38, 39)

All of these are full contractions, i.e. scalars: the same number in every coordinate system, exactly as a length or an angle should be.

Example: angle between coordinate lines The tangent vectors of the q¹ and q² lines are A = (1, 0) and B = (0, 1) in contravariant components. Then cos θ₁₂ = g₁₂/√(g₁₁g₂₂). Orthogonal coordinates ⇔ g₁₂ = 0. For oblique axes, cos θ₁₂ = cos α ✓.

10. Book's Exercise 18: all problems solved

1. Obtain Eqs. (26) from Eqs. (24).
Show solution

Multiply Ai·k = Ailglk by gkj: Ai·kgkj = Ailglkgkj = Ailδlj = Aij ✓.

Multiply Ak·i = Aliglk by gkj: Aliδlj = Aji ✓.

Multiply Aij = Apqgpigqj by gikgjl: Apqδpkδql = Akl ✓. ∎

2. Using Exercise 15.4, show that the covariant components of velocity in spherical polar coordinates are ṙ, r²θ̇, r²sin²θ φ̇.
Show solution

Contravariant velocity: vi = (ṙ, θ̇, φ̇). Lower with gij = diag(1, r², r²sin²θ): vi = gijvj = (ṙ, r²θ̇, r²sin²θ φ̇). ∎

Check: vivi = ṙ² + r²θ̇² + r²sin²θ φ̇², the familiar speed squared ✓. (In mechanics, m vi is the canonical momentum pi = ∂T/∂q̇i, e.g. pφ = mr²sin²θ φ̇ = Lz.)

3. Using the covariant components of the gradient (Exercise 15.6), find its contravariant components in spherical coordinates.
Show solution

(∇f)i = (∂f/∂r, ∂f/∂θ, ∂f/∂φ). Raise with gij = diag(1, 1/r², 1/(r²sin²θ)):

(∇f)i = (∂f/∂r,  (1/r²)∂f/∂θ,  (1/(r²sin²θ))∂f/∂φ)

This is the book's answer. (Physical components = (∂f/∂r, (1/r)∂f/∂θ, (1/(r sin θ))∂f/∂φ).)

5. Show that in an orthogonal coordinate system a covariant component of a tensor is related only to the corresponding contravariant component.
Show solution

Orthogonal ⇒ gij = 0 for i ≠ j, so Ai = gijAj = giiAi (no sum): only the same-numbered component appears. Likewise Ai = Ai/gii, and for higher rank Aij = giigjjAij (no sum). ∎ In a non-orthogonal system each Ai mixes several Aj (see the vector lab).

6. The contravariant components of acceleration in spherical coordinates are given in Eqs. (15.50). Obtain the covariant components.
Show solution

Use the corrected (15.50) (see the Chapter 15 page: aθ contains +2ṙθ̇/r). Lower with diag(1, r², r²sin²θ):

ar = r̈ − rθ̇² − r sin²θ φ̇²
aθ = r²θ̈ + 2rṙθ̇ − r²sin θ cos θ φ̇² = d(r²θ̇)/dt − r²sin θ cos θ φ̇²
aφ = r²sin²θ φ̈ + 2r sin²θ ṙφ̇ + 2r²sin θ cos θ θ̇φ̇ = d(r²sin²θ φ̇)/dt

Checked symbolically. The last form shows that if there is no φ-force, aφ = 0 ⇒ r²sin²θ φ̇ = const: conservation of Lz.

7. Show that in a Cartesian coordinate system the distinction between the contravariant, covariant and mixed components of a tensor vanishes.
Show solution

gij = gij = δij. Every lowering or raising is A…i… = δijA…j… = A…i…: the components keep their values. Apply this to each index in turn: Aij = Ai·j = Ai·j = Aij numerically. ∎ This is the basis of Section 19 (Cartesian tensors).

8. Define δjk by lowering the upper index of δjk. In 3D, obtain its components in (a) Cartesian, (b) spherical, (c) the (u, v, w) coordinates of Example 4.
Show solution

δjk ≡ gjlδlk = gjk. So "lowered δ" is just the metric:

  • (a) Cartesian: diag(1, 1, 1), a genuine Kronecker delta.
  • (b) Spherical: diag(1, r², r²sin²θ).
  • (c) Example 4: the matrix (16a), [[v² + w², uv, uw], [uv, w² + u², vw], [uw, vw, u² + v²]].

So δjk is a Kronecker delta only in Cartesian coordinates. The mixed δjk is the true isotropic tensor (Section 16).

9. Show that AijkBijk = Ai·j·kBi·j·k = Aij··kBij··k (and similar forms).
Show solution

Apply the see-saw step of Example 6 to one dummy pair at a time. For the middle index: AijkBijk = AijkgjlBi·l·k, since Bijk = gjlBi·l·k. And Aijkgjl = Ai·l·k, so the product is Ai·l·kBi·l·k ✓. Doing the same for the last index gives the third form. Any dummy pair can be see-sawed independently, as long as each index keeps its left-to-right position. ∎

11. Practice set (CSIR-NET / GATE style)

P1. Find gij, g and the area element for parabolic coordinates x = στ, y = (τ² − σ²)/2. metric
Show solution

∂r/∂σ = (τ, −σ), ∂r/∂τ = (σ, τ). Dot products: gσσ = gττ = σ² + τ², gστ = τσ − στ = 0. Orthogonal, g = (σ² + τ²)², dA = (σ² + τ²)dσ dτ.

P2. Find the length of the circle of latitude θ = θ₀ on a sphere of radius a. length
Show solution

dθ = 0 ⇒ ds = a sin θ₀ dφ ⇒ L = 2πa sin θ₀.

P3. Area of the sphere from the metric. area
Show solution

√g = a²sin θ ⇒ A = ∫₀^π∫₀^{2π} a²sin θ dφ dθ = 4πa².

P4. In polar coordinates, Ai = (2, 1/r). Find Ai and |A|. lower
Show solution

Ai = (2, r²·(1/r)) = (2, r). |A|² = AiAi = 4 + 1 = 5 ⇒ |A| = √5, independent of r.

P5. With oblique axes at 60°, find the angle between Ai = (1, 0) and Bi = (1, 1). angle
Show solution

g = [[1, ½], [½, 1]]. A·B = gijAiBj = 1 + ½ = 3/2. |A|² = 1, |B|² = 1 + 1 + 2·½ = 3. cos θ = (3/2)/√3 = √3/2 ⇒ θ = 30° (B bisects the two axes ✓).

P6. Show gijgij = N. trace
Show solution

gijgij = gijgji = δii = N.

P7. The Minkowski metric is η = diag(1, −1, −1, −1). Lower the 4-momentum pμ = (E/c, px, py, pz) and compute pμpμ. relativity
Show solution

pμ = (E/c, −px, −py, −pz). pμpμ = E²/c² − |p|² = m²c², an invariant. (Section 20.)

P8. Is the metric ds² = dθ² + sin²θ dφ² Euclidean in the book's sense? Is the surface flat? concept
Show solution

Not Euclidean in the book's sense (g22 depends on θ), and the unit sphere is genuinely curved: its Gaussian curvature is 1, and no coordinate change can make g constant. Contrast: the polar plane dr² + r²dθ² also has a non-constant g but is flat.

13. Interesting facts

Riemann's lecture (1854) Bernhard Riemann introduced ds² = gijdxidxj in his habilitation lecture "On the hypotheses which lie at the foundations of geometry". Gauss, then 77, chose that topic from the three Riemann offered and was reportedly deeply impressed. Sixty years later it became the language of Einstein's gravity, where gμν is the gravitational field.
Maps of the Earth Every flat map distorts distances because the sphere's metric cannot be made constant (Gauss's Theorema Egregium). The Mercator projection keeps angles (its metric is a multiple of dx² + dy²) but blows up areas near the poles. Compare the metric lab's sphere mode, where the coordinate ellipse stretches near the poles.
GPS Satellite clocks run faster by about 38 μs per day, because the spacetime metric g00 depends on height in Earth's gravity. Without metric-tensor corrections, GPS positions would drift by about 10 km per day.
