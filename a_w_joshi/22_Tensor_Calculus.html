0. Roadmap

In Cartesian coordinates, the derivative of a vector field is easy: differentiate each component. In curved coordinates (polar, spherical) or on a curved surface, this fails. The basis vectors themselves change from point to point, so a field with "constant components" can actually be turning. The fix is to add correction terms built from the metric. These are the Christoffel symbols Γ, and the corrected derivative is the covariant derivative, which is a tensor.

∂Ai/∂xj not a tensor→Γkij from ∂g→Ai;j = Ai,j − AkΓkij (a tensor)→gij;k = 0→DA/Dτ along a curve→geodesics (Section 23), curvature (Section 24)
Notation (Eqs. 1–4) A comma means an ordinary partial derivative, a semicolon the covariant derivative: Ai,j = ∂Ai/∂xj,   φ,ij = ∂²φ/∂xi∂xj,   xi,α = ∂xi/∂x̄α,   x̄α,j = ∂x̄α/∂xj. So xi,αx̄α,j = δij (2). Latin letters label the unbarred system, Greek letters the barred one. The xi,α themselves are not tensors.
Needed from earlier: transformation laws (Section 15), quotient law (17), metric gij, gij and raising/lowering (18), and Exercise 15.7 (second derivatives are not a tensor).

1. The problem (Eqs. 5–6)

The gradient Ai = φ,i of a scalar is a covariant vector: Āα = xi,αAi.  (5)   Differentiate once more with respect to x̄β:

Āα,β = xi,αβAi + xi,αxj,βAi,j   (6)

The second term is the correct rank-2 tensor law. The first term spoils it. It vanishes only when the transformation is linear (xi,αβ = 0), i.e. for Cartesian-to-Cartesian changes. In general coordinate derivatives of a tensor (other than a scalar) are not tensors.

Geometric reason: Ai,j compares the components of A at two neighbouring points. But in curvilinear coordinates the basis vectors at those two points differ, so the difference of components mixes "A really changed" with "the basis turned". Γ measures how the basis turns.

2. Christoffel symbols (Eqs. 7–10, Example 1)

Definitions
First kind:   [ij, k] = ½(gik,j + gjk,i − gij,k)   (7a)
Second kind:   Γkij = gkm[ij, m] = ½gkm(gim,j + gjm,i − gij,m)   (7b)
Γkij is also written {kij}.
  • Symmetry: [ij, k] = [ji, k] and Γkij = Γkji (8). In N dimensions there are N·N(N+1)/2 independent symbols of each kind (6 in 2D, 18 in 3D, 40 in 4D).
  • Memory aid: "first index plus second index minus the shared one": the pattern is gik,j + gjk,i − gij,k, with k always in the metric and the two lower indices i, j appearing once as the derivative.
  • Cartesian coordinates (constant g): every Γ = 0. That is why ordinary derivatives worked there.
  • Lowering: [ij, k] = gkmΓmij (from (7b) with gkmgmn = δ).

Example 1: gij,k = [ik, j] + [jk, i]  (9)

[ik, j] = ½(gij,k + gkj,i − gik,j) and [jk, i] = ½(gji,k + gki,j − gjk,i) (10). Adding, and using the symmetry of g, the last four terms cancel in pairs: [ik, j] + [jk, i] = gij,k. ∎ So the derivatives of the metric are completely determined by the Christoffel symbols, and vice versa.

Diagonal metrics: the short cut (used in Example 5)

If gij = 0 for i ≠ j, then (no summation) with distinct i, j, k:

all indices equal[ii, i] = ½∂igiiΓiii = ∂igii/(2gii) = ∂i ln√gii
i = k ≠ j[ij, i] = ½∂jgiiΓiij = ∂jgii/(2gii)
i = j ≠ k[ii, k] = −½∂kgiiΓkii = −∂kgii/(2gkk)
i, j, k all different00

4. Christoffel symbols are not tensors (Example 2, Eqs. 11–18)

Start from ḡαβ = xi,αxj,βgij (11), differentiate with respect to x̄γ (12), write the two cyclic permutations (13, 14), add two and subtract one, and use the symmetry of g. The result:

[αβ, γ]̄ = xi,αβxj,γgij + xi,αxj,βxk,γ[ij, k]   (16)
Γ̄γαβ = xi,αβx̄γ,i + xi,αxj,βx̄γ,kΓkij   (18)

The second terms are tensor laws; the first (bold) terms are not. So Γ is not a tensor. The clearest evidence: Γ = 0 in Cartesian coordinates but Γ ≠ 0 in polar coordinates of the same flat plane. A true tensor that is zero in one system is zero in every system (Section 16).

Index rule: the second term of (16) and (18) shows how the indices behave: in [ij, k] and Γkij, i, j, k (resp. i, j) act as covariant indices and the upper k of Γ as contravariant. Use this to check equations, even though Γ is not a tensor.

The non-tensor terms are exactly what is needed Solving (18) for xk,αβ gives
xk,αβ = xk,ρΓ̄ραβ − xi,αxj,βΓkij   (19)

which expresses the troublesome second derivatives of (6) through Γ's. This is how the cancellation in the next section works.

5. The covariant derivative (Eqs. 19–28, Example 3)

Put (19) into (6). The bad term splits into a Γ̄ part, which joins the left side, and a Γ part, which joins the right side (20, 21):

Āα,β − ĀρΓ̄ραβ = xi,αxj,β(Ai,j − AkΓkij)
Covariant derivative
Ai;j = Ai,j − AkΓkij  (covariant vector, 22a)     Ai;j = Ai,j + AkΓikj  (contravariant vector, 25)
Then Āα;β = xi,αxj,βAi;j (23): Ai;j is a rank-2 tensor, covariant in j.
Do not confuse Ai,j and Ai;j The comma version is the plain partial derivative, not a tensor. The semicolon version is the covariant derivative, a tensor. Other books write ∇jAi or DjAi for Ai;j.

The general rule (27)

For any tensor: take the partial derivative, then add +Γ for every upper index and −Γ for every lower index:

Ai₁…ipj₁…jq;k = Ai₁…j₁…,k + Σr A…l…j₁…Γirlk − Σs Ai₁……l…Γljsk

The result has one more covariant index. A scalar has no correction: φ;i = φ,i (26).

Example 3: rank-2 tensors (28)

Aij;k = Aij,k − ApjΓpik − AipΓpjk
Aij;k = Aij,k + ApjΓipk + AipΓjpk
Aij;k = Aij,k + ApjΓipk − AipΓpjk

Example 7: a fluid with vi = (x², y²) in polar coordinates

  1. Polar components (contravariant, Eq. 15.24): ur = r²(cos³θ + sin³θ),   uθ = r sin θ cos θ(sin θ − cos θ).  (66)
  2. ui;j = ∂ui/∂xj + ukΓikj (67) with Γ from (68):
ur;r = ∂ur/∂r = 2r(cos³θ + sin³θ)  (69)
ur;θ = ∂ur/∂θ + uθΓrθθ = 2r² sin θ cos θ(sin θ − cos θ)  (70)
uθ;r = ∂uθ/∂r + uθΓθθr = 2 sin θ cos θ(sin θ − cos θ)  (71)
uθ;θ = ∂uθ/∂θ + urΓθrθ = 2r sin θ cos θ(sin θ + cos θ)  (72)

Checked symbolically. Sanity check: the contraction ui;i = 2r(cos³θ + sin³θ) + 2r sin θ cos θ(sin θ + cos θ) = 2r(sin θ + cos θ) = 2x + 2y, which equals the Cartesian divergence ∂(x²)/∂x + ∂(y²)/∂y. A scalar must agree in every coordinate system ✓.

6. 🔬 Polar-field lab: why the Γ terms are needed

It shows the ordinary derivatives ui,j, the covariant derivatives ui;j, and converts ui;j back to Cartesian components (with the Jacobians). 7. Ricci's lemma: the metric is covariantly constant (Example 4, Eqs. 29–38)

Ricci's lemma   gij;k = 0,   gij;k = 0,   δij;k = 0.
  1. (a) gij;k: by (28a), gij;k = gij,k − gpjΓpik − gipΓpjk (29). Lowering, gpjΓpik = [ik, j] and gipΓpjk = [jk, i] (30). By Example 1 the result is gij,k − [ik, j] − [jk, i] = 0.  (31)
  2. gij;k: from (28b), gij;k = gij,k + gpjgiqgpq,k (32). Differentiating gpjgqigpq = gij two ways (33–35) shows that this combination equals 0.  (36)
  3. (b) δij;k = δij,k + δpjΓipk − δipΓpjk = 0 + Γijk − Γijk = 0.  (37, 38)

Why it matters: g and g-1 pass straight through a covariant derivative, so raising and lowering indices commutes with ";": (gijAj);k = gijAj;k, i.e. Ai;k = gijAj;k. Lengths and angles are preserved by parallel transport (Section 9).

8. Example 5 (sphere) and Example 6 (Γiij)

Example 5: sphere of radius a, x¹ = θ, x² = φ

g₁₁ = a², g₂₂ = a²sin²θ, g₁₂ = 0. Only g₂₂ depends on a coordinate: ∂g₂₂/∂θ = 2a² sin θ cos θ (44). Using the diagonal short cut, the eight symbols of the first kind are

[ii, i] = 0  (48a),   [12, 1] = [21, 1] = 0  (48b),   [12, 2] = [21, 2] = a² sin θ cos θ  (48c),   [11, 2] = 0  (48d),   [22, 1] = −a² sin θ cos θ  (48e)

With g11 = 1/a², g22 = 1/(a² sin²θ) (50) and Γkij = gkk[ij, k] (49):

Γ212 = Γ221 = cot θ,   Γ122 = −sin θ cos θ, all others 0  (51)

They are independent of the radius a: scaling a metric by a constant does not change Γ.

Example 6: Γiij = ∂(ln√g)/∂xj  (52)

  1. Γiij = gih[ij, h] = ½gih(gih,j + gjh,i − gij,h).  (53, 54)
  2. i and h are both dummies and gih is symmetric, so the last two terms cancel after swapping i ↔ h. That leaves ½gihgih,j.
  3. gih = (cofactor of gih)/g, and Σ(cofactor)(∂gih/∂xj) = ∂g/∂xj (the derivative of a determinant, Eq. 4.6).  (55)
  4. So Γiij = (1/2g)∂g/∂xj = ∂(ln√g)/∂xj. ∎  (56)
The payoff: divergence in any coordinates Ai;i = Ai,i + AkΓiki = Ai,i + Ak∂kln√g, i.e.
div A = Ai;i = (1/√g) ∂(√g Ai)/∂xi
This is the short formula promised in Section 15. Cylindrical: √g = ρ gives Eq. (15.57). Spherical: √g = r² sin θ gives Exercise 15.8(a). The Laplacian: ∇²φ = (1/√g)∂i(√g gij∂jφ).

9. Intrinsic (absolute) derivative along a curve (Eqs. 57–64)

Let C be a curve xk = xk(τ) and A a tensor defined along it (57). Its intrinsic derivative is

DAi…j…/Dτ = Ai…j…;k dxk/dτ   (58)

a tensor of the same type (quotient law: a tensor times the vector dxk/dτ). Written out:

Dφ/Dτ = dφ/dτ  (59);    DAi/Dτ = dAi/dτ + ΓikjAjdxk/dτ  (60);    DAi/Dτ = dAi/dτ − ΓjikAjdxk/dτ  (61)

Differential form: DAi = dAi + ΓikjAjdxk,   DAi = dAi − ΓjikAjdxk (62). Higher rank as in (63). By Ricci's lemma, Dgij/Dτ = Dgij/Dτ = Dδij/Dτ = 0.  (64)

  • Physics example: with Ai = velocity dxi/dτ, (60) gives d²xi/dτ² + Γikj(dxk/dτ)(dxj/dτ), which is exactly the polar/spherical acceleration of Section 15 (e.g. ar = r̈ − rθ̇², with the −rθ̇² from Γrθθ = −r). Setting it to zero gives geodesics: Section 23.
  • Parallel transport: a vector is carried "without turning" along C if DAi/Dτ = 0. In a flat plane this keeps the arrow pointing the same way. On a curved surface it does not come back to itself around a closed loop. That failure measures curvature (Section 24).

10. 🔬 Parallel-transport lab on the sphere (drag to rotate the view)

A vector is carried around the circle of latitude θ = θ₀ by solving DAi/Dφ = 0 with the sphere's Γ's from Example 5:

dAθ/dφ = sin θ₀ cos θ₀ Aφ,    dAφ/dφ = −cot θ₀ Aθ

After one full turn the vector comes back rotated by 2π(1 − cos θ₀), which equals the solid angle of the cap enclosed (area/a²). On the equator (θ₀ = 90°, a great circle) there is no rotation. The orange arrow keeps constant length throughout, as Ricci's lemma requires.

11. The product (Leibniz) rule (Example 8, Eqs. 73–74)

(AijBkl);q = Aij;qBkl + AijBkl;q   (73)

Proof: write Ciklj = AijBkl and apply (27): C;q = C,q + (three +Γ terms for i, k, l) − (one −Γ term for j). The ordinary product rule splits C,q. Group the Γ terms belonging to A's indices with A,q B and those belonging to B's indices with A B,q: each group is exactly a covariant derivative. ∎  (74)   The same works for any product, outer or inner (Exercises 12, 13), because contraction commutes with ";" (δij;k = 0).

13. Book's Exercise 22: solved

1. Write the covariant derivatives of (a) Aijk, (b) Aijkl, (c) Aijkl, (d) Aijklm with respect to xq.
Show solution

Rule: +Γ for each upper, −Γ for each lower index (27).

(a) Aijk;q = Aijk,q + ApjkΓipq − AipkΓpjq − AijpΓpkq
(b) Aijkl;q = Aijkl,q + ApjklΓipq + AipklΓjpq − AijplΓpkq − AijkpΓplq
(c) Aijkl;q = Aijkl,q + ApjklΓipq + AipklΓjpq + AijplΓkpq − AijkpΓplq
(d) Aijklm;q = Aijklm,q + ApjklmΓipq + AipklmΓjpq − AijplmΓpkq − AijkpmΓplq − AijklpΓpmq
2. Write the intrinsic derivatives of the tensors of Exercise 1 along xq = xq(τ).
Show solution

Multiply each result of Exercise 1 by dxq/dτ (58). For example

DAijk/Dτ = dAijk/dτ + (ApjkΓipq − AipkΓpjq − AijpΓpkq) dxq/dτ

using A,qdxq/dτ = dA/dτ (chain rule). The others follow the same pattern.

3. If Ai is a covariant vector, show that Ai,j − Aj,i is a tensor.
Show solution

Ai;j − Aj;i = (Ai,j − ΓkijAk) − (Aj,i − ΓkjiAk) = Ai,j − Aj,i, because Γkij = Γkji. The left side is a difference of tensors, hence a tensor. ∎ This is the "curl" (e.g. Fij = Aj,i − Ai,j in Section 21 needs no Γ's even in curved coordinates).

4. If Aij is antisymmetric, show that Aij;k + Ajk;i + Aki;j is a tensor, antisymmetric in any pair of indices.
Show solution

It is a sum of covariant derivatives, so a tensor. Expand with (28a): the Γ terms are −ApjΓpik − AipΓpjk − ApkΓpji − AjpΓpki − ApiΓpkj − AkpΓpij. Pair them: ApjΓpik + AjpΓpki = 0 (antisymmetry of A, symmetry of Γ), and similarly for the other two pairs. So the expression equals Aij,k + Ajk,i + Aki,j.

Antisymmetry: swapping i ↔ j gives Aji;k + Aik;j + Akj;i = −(Aij;k + Aki;j + Ajk;i), the negative. Likewise for the other pairs. (This is the cyclic identity (21.34) for F.)

5. Compute the Christoffel symbols of both kinds for a plane in polar coordinates r, θ.
Show solution

g = diag(1, r²), only g₂₂,₁ = 2r ≠ 0. First kind: [22, 1] = −r, [12, 2] = [21, 2] = r, all others 0. Second kind: Γ122 = −r, Γ212 = Γ221 = 1/r, others 0 (Eq. 68).

6. Compute the nonvanishing Christoffel symbols of both kinds for cylindrical and spherical coordinates in 3D Euclidean space.
Show solution

Cylindrical (ρ, φ, z), g = diag(1, ρ², 1): [φφ, ρ] = −ρ, [ρφ, φ] = ρ; Γρφφ = −ρ, Γφρφ = 1/ρ.

Spherical (r, θ, φ), g = diag(1, r², r²sin²θ):

First kind: [θθ, r] = −r, [rθ, θ] = r, [φφ, r] = −r sin²θ, [rφ, φ] = r sin²θ, [φφ, θ] = −r² sin θ cos θ, [θφ, φ] = r² sin θ cos θ
Second kind: Γrθθ = −r, Γrφφ = −r sin²θ, Γθrθ = 1/r, Γθφφ = −sin θ cos θ, Γφrφ = 1/r, Γφθφ = cot θ

(Computed symbolically. Check with Example 6: Γiir = 2/r = ∂rln(r² sin θ) ✓, Γiiθ = cot θ ✓.)

7. Compute all the Christoffel symbols for the space-time ds² = −eλdr² − r²dθ² − r² sin²θ dφ² + eνdt², λ, ν functions of r and t.
Show solution

Coordinates (r, θ, φ, t), g = diag(−eλ, −r², −r²sin²θ, eν). Dots: ∂/∂t, primes: ∂/∂r. Nonzero second-kind symbols (computed symbolically):

Γrrr = λ′/2,   Γrrt = λ̇/2,   Γrθθ = −r e−λ,   Γrφφ = −r sin²θ e−λ,   Γrtt = ½ν′eν−λ
Γθrθ = 1/r,   Γθφφ = −sin θ cos θ
Γφrφ = 1/r,   Γφθφ = cot θ
Γtrr = ½λ̇eλ−ν,   Γtrt = ν′/2,   Γttt = ν̇/2

(plus the symmetric partners Γkji = Γkij). This is the general spherically symmetric metric; with eν = e−λ = 1 − 2GM/rc² it is the Schwarzschild metric of a star or black hole.

8. A fluid has vi = (x, 2y) in Cartesian coordinates. Find its covariant derivative in polar coordinates.
Show solution

Polar components: ur = cos θ·x + sin θ·2y = r(1 + sin²θ),   uθ = (−sin θ·x + cos θ·2y)/r = sin θ cos θ.

ur;r = ∂ur/∂r = 1 + sin²θ
ur;θ = ∂ur/∂θ + uθΓrθθ = 2r sin θ cos θ − r sin θ cos θ = r sin θ cos θ
uθ;r = ∂uθ/∂r + uθΓθθr = 0 + sin θ cos θ/r
uθ;θ = ∂uθ/∂θ + urΓθrθ = cos 2θ + 1 + sin²θ = 1 + cos²θ

Check: ui;i = 1 + sin²θ + 1 + cos²θ = 3 = ∂x/∂x + ∂(2y)/∂y ✓. (Load "Exercise 8" in the polar-field lab.)

9. Find the intrinsic derivative of the field of Exercise 8 along the spiral r = aθ + b.
Show solution

Parametrize by θ: dr/dθ = a, dθ/dθ = 1. DAi/Dθ = ui;r(dr/dθ) + ui;θ:

Dur/Dθ = a(1 + sin²θ) + r sin θ cos θ,    Duθ/Dθ = a sin θ cos θ/r + 1 + cos²θ,   with r = aθ + b

Check in Cartesian: v changes along the curve by dv/dθ = (dx/dθ, 2dy/dθ). Converting that vector to polar components gives the same two numbers, as it must (the intrinsic derivative is the true rate of change of the vector).

10. Christoffel symbols of both kinds for ds² = f(u, v)du² + h(u, v)dv².
Show solution

Diagonal, so use the short cut (subscripts u, v denote partial derivatives):

First kind: [uu, u] = ½fu, [uu, v] = −½fv, [uv, u] = ½fv, [uv, v] = ½hu, [vv, u] = −½hu, [vv, v] = ½hv

12. Prove (a) (AijBklm);q = Aij;qBklm + AijBklm;q; (b) (AijBjk);q (inner product) = Ai;qjBjk + AijBjk;q; (c) (AiBjCk);q = Ai;qBjCk + AiBj;qCk + AiBjCk;q.
Show solution

(a), (c): exactly as Example 8. Expand the covariant derivative of the outer product with (27); the ordinary product rule splits the partial derivative, and each Γ term belongs to the index of exactly one factor, so the terms regroup into the covariant derivatives of the factors.

(b): first form the outer product Dijlk = AijBlk, apply (a), then contract j with l. Contraction commutes with ";" because δjl;q = 0, i.e. the two Γ terms belonging to the contracted pair (+Γjpq and −Γplq) cancel after setting l = j. ∎

13. Prove in general that intrinsic differentiation of a product satisfies the distributive law.
Show solution

D(AB)/Dτ = (AB);kdxk/dτ = (A;kB + AB;k)dxk/dτ = (DA/Dτ)B + A(DB/Dτ), using Exercise 12 and multiplying by dxk/dτ. ∎ Consequence: D(gijAiBj)/Dτ = gij(DAi/Dτ)Bj + gijAi(DBj/Dτ). Under parallel transport (DA = DB = 0) the dot product, hence lengths and angles, stays constant.

14. If u is a 3D vector, show (curl u)i = εijkuk,j in Cartesian coordinates.

16. Interesting facts

Christoffel (1869) Elwin Bruno Christoffel introduced his symbols while asking when two quadratic forms gijdxidxj can be transformed into each other. Ricci and Levi-Civita turned his tools into the "absolute differential calculus" (1900), the name explained by the absolute derivative D/Dτ.
Gravity is a Γ In general relativity the geodesic equation ẍi + Γijkẋjẋk = 0 replaces Newton's law. For slow motion in weak gravity, Γi00 ≈ ∂iΦ/c², so the Christoffel symbols play the role of the gravitational field and the metric plays the role of the potential. Because Γ is not a tensor, gravity can be "switched off" at a point by free fall, which is Einstein's equivalence principle.
Foucault's pendulum The plane of a Foucault pendulum at latitude λ turns by 2π sin λ per day, exactly the parallel-transport rotation 2π(1 − cos θ₀) of the lab, with θ₀ = 90° − λ measured from the pole (after subtracting the trivial full turn of the Earth).
