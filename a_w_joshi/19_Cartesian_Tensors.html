0. Roadmap

General tensors (Sections 15–18) must transform correctly under every change of coordinates, including curvilinear ones. That is more than most physics needs. Crystals, elasticity, rigid bodies and electrostatics usually only need Cartesian axes, and the only freedom is to rotate (and shift) them. Restricting to these changes:

  • the transformation matrix aij is orthogonal (rows and columns are orthonormal),
  • so ∂x̄i/∂xj = ∂xj/∂x̄i = aij, and upper and lower indices become the same. Every index can be written as a subscript,
  • a Cartesian tensor of rank r has 3r components with Āα₁…αr = aα₁i₁…aαrirAi₁…ir.
direction cosines aij→AAᵀ = I→x̄ = Ax→Ā = AXAᵀ (rank 2)→physics: stress, strain, χij, dijk, Iij
Needed from earlier Orthogonal matrices (Section 5), rotation matrices and Euler angles (Section 14), real symmetric matrices diagonalized by rotations (Section 10, 11), εijk and δij (Section 16), quotient law (Section 17), gij = δij in Cartesian coordinates (Section 18).

1. Direction cosines (Eqs. 1–10)

The most general change of right-handed Cartesian axes is a rotation about an axis through O followed by a translation t to the new origin O′. The translation is simple: x̄ = x − t (1). The two operations commute. So the work is in the rotation.

The direction cosines (l₁, l₂, l₃) of a line OP are the cosines of the angles it makes with Ox₁, Ox₂, Ox₃. They are just the Cartesian components of a unit vector along OP, so:

lili = 1  (2, 3);    cos θ = lil′i  (6);    perpendicular ⇔ lil′i = 0  (7)

The projection of OP onto a line with direction cosines l′i is OR = x·n = l′ixi.  (10)

2. Orthogonal transformations (Eqs. 11–20)

Rotate the axes to Ox̄₁, Ox̄₂, Ox̄₃. Let aij = cosine of the angle between the new axis Ox̄i and the old axis Oxj. The new coordinate x̄i is the projection of OP on Ox̄i, so by (10):

x̄i = aijxj,    A = [aij]   (12, 13)
  • Rows of A = new axes written in old coordinates. They are orthonormal unit vectors: aijakj = δik (16), i.e. AAᵀ = I.
  • Columns of A = old axes in new coordinates: ajiajk = δik (17), i.e. AᵀA = I.
  • Inverse: multiply (12) by aik and sum: xi = ajix̄j (19), i.e. A⁻¹ = Aᵀ.
  • det A = ±1. +1: a proper rotation (right-handed stays right-handed). −1: an improper rotation (rotation combined with reflection or inversion; right-handed becomes left-handed).
  • Most general change of Cartesian axes: x̄i = aij(xj − tj), inverse xi = ajix̄j + ti.  (20)
Count the freedom 9 entries aij, 6 conditions in AAᵀ = I (it is symmetric), so 3 free parameters: the three Euler angles, or an axis (2 numbers) plus an angle (1 number).

3. Example 1: rotation through θ about an arbitrary axis (Eqs. 21–29)

Rotate a point P (position x) through θ, right-handedly, about the line OR with direction cosines λi (unit vector λ). It goes to Q. With S the foot of the perpendicular from P (and Q) on OR, and T the foot from Q on PS (Fig. 13):

  1. OQ = OP + PT + TQ.  (21)
  2. OS: the projection of OP on OR is λjxj, so OS⃗ = (λjxj)λi.  (22)   Then PS⃗ = (λjxj)λi − xi.  (23)
  3. PT: TS = QS cos θ = PS cos θ, so PT = (1 − cos θ)PS, and PT⃗ = (1 − cos θ)[(λjxj)λi − xi].  (24)
  4. TQ: perpendicular to both PS⃗ and λ, of length QS sin θ = PS sin θ. Using the cross product: TQ⃗i = εijkλjxk sin θ (after using εijkλjλk = 0).  (25–27)
  5. Add them:
    x̄i = xi + (1 − cos θ)(λjxjλi − xi) + εijkλjxk sin θ   (28)
  6. Read off the matrix:
    aij = cos θ δij + (1 − cos θ)λiλj + εiljλl sin θ   (29)

In vector form this is Rodrigues' rotation formula: x′ = x cos θ + λ(λ·x)(1 − cos θ) + (λ × x) sin θ.

Active, not passive Eq. (29) moves the point (active rotation), unlike Eq. (12), which turns the axes (passive). They are transposes of each other: turning the axes by +θ is the same as turning the point by −θ. Check with Exercise 3(a): (29) with λ = (0, 0, 1), θ = π/2 sends (1, 0, 0) to (0, 1, 0), which rotates the point anticlockwise. For Eq. (12) you would use the transpose.

Reading axis and angle from any rotation matrix

  • Trace: aii = 3cos θ + (1 − cos θ) = 1 + 2cos θ.
  • Antisymmetric part: aij − aji = 2εiljλl sin θ, so e.g. a₃₂ − a₂₃ = 2λ₁ sin θ (with the sign set by the active convention).
  • The axis λ is the eigenvector with eigenvalue 1. The other eigenvalues are e±iθ.

5. Cartesian tensors (Eqs. 30–32)

Definition A Cartesian tensor of rank r (in 3D) is a set of 3r components that obey (15.41) only under orthogonal transformations of Cartesian axes. This is a weaker condition than for a general tensor:
every general tensor is a Cartesian tensor, but not every Cartesian tensor is a general tensor.

From (20): ∂x̄i/∂xj = aij = ∂xj/∂x̄i.  (30)   The two kinds of Jacobian are equal, so covariant and contravariant laws coincide and all indices can be written as subscripts:

Āα₁α₂…αr = aα₁i₁aα₂i₂…aαrir Ai₁i₂…ir   (31)

For rank 2 in matrix form: Ā = A X Aᵀ, which is also a similarity transformation (Aᵀ = A⁻¹). So eigenvalues, trace and determinant of a Cartesian rank-2 tensor are invariants.

  • The position xi is a Cartesian vector, since (12) is linear, but not a general vector (Section 15).
  • δij is a Cartesian tensor: δ̄αβ = aαiaβjδij = aαiaβi = δαβ (32), by (16). Compare: lowered δ in general coordinates is gij (Exercise 18.8).
  • εijk is a Cartesian tensor under proper rotations: ε̄αβγ = aαiaβjaγkεijk = (det A)εαβγ. Under improper rotations it gets a factor −1: a pseudotensor.
  • The quotient law (Section 17) works for Cartesian tensors too.

6. Isotropic tensors (Eqs. 33–46, Example 2)

Definition A Cartesian tensor is isotropic if its components are unchanged by every rotation of the axes. A material property described by an isotropic tensor is the same in every direction.
RankAll isotropic tensors in 3D
0every scalar
1only the zero vector
2λδij
3cεijk (under proper rotations)
4aδijδkl + bδikδjl + cδilδjk  (46)

Rank 1 (Eqs. 33–34)

If u is isotropic, ū = Au = u for every rotation A, so (A − I)u = 0 for every A. A rotation about any axis not along u moves u, so u = 0.

Example 2: rank 2 ⇒ λδij, step by step

  1. Use an infinitesimal rotation aij = δij − cij, with C antisymmetric and |cij| ≪ 1.  (36, 37)   It is orthogonal to first order: aijaik ≈ δjk − ckj − cjk = δjk.  (38)
  2. Isotropy: Āij = aikajlAkl ≈ Aij − cjlAil − cikAkj must equal Aij. So cjlAil + cikAkj = 0 for all small antisymmetric C.  (39, 40)
  3. Take i = 1, j = 2: c₁₂(A₂₂ − A₁₁) + c₁₃A₃₂ + c₂₃A₁₃ = 0.  (41)   c₁₂, c₁₃, c₂₃ are independent, so A₁₁ = A₂₂ and A₃₂ = A₁₃ = 0.  (42)
  4. Other choices of (i, j) give A₁₁ = A₂₂ = A₃₃ and every off-diagonal Aij = 0.  (43)
  5. So Aij = λδij, λ = A₁₁.  (45) ∎
Physical meaning In an isotropic medium (glass, a liquid, a cubic crystal for rank-2 properties) conductivity, susceptibility and the like reduce to a single number: σij = σδij ⇒ j = σE. For an isotropic elastic solid, (46) plus the symmetries of stress and strain leaves 2 constants (the Lamé constants λ, μ): Xij = λ ekkδij + 2μ eij.

8. Stress, strain and Hooke's law (Eqs. 47–59)

Stress tensor

Stress = force per unit area, but its value depends on the orientation of the area. At a point P take three small area elements dσx, dσy, dσz normal to the x, y, z axes (Fig. 14). The force on dσx has three components, giving Xx = F1x/dσx, Yx = F1y/dσx, Zx = F1z/dσx (47). Similarly for dσy and dσz: 9 components Xij (force direction i, area normal j).

  • Force is a vector and area (its normal) is a vector, and the force is not necessarily along the normal. Linking two vectors in this way makes stress a rank-2 tensor (quotient law).
  • For an area with unit normal n, the force per area is ti = Xijnj. Diagonal entries are normal stresses (push or pull); off-diagonal entries are shear stresses.
  • Balance of torques on a tiny cube gives Xij = Xji (57): only 6 independent components.

Strain tensor

Before deformation: orthonormal x̂, ŷ, ẑ. After a small deformation the edges become (48):

x′ = (1 + εxx)x̂ + εxyŷ + εxzẑ,   etc.;   Δxi = Σj εijxj  (52)
  • Length: |x′| ≈ 1 + εxx, so the diagonal ε's are fractional changes of length.  (49)
  • Volume: V′ = x′·(y′ × z′) ≈ 1 + εxx + εyy + εzz, so the dilation δ = ΔV/V = εxx + εyy + εzz (50, 51), the trace, which is invariant.
  • Strain tensor (53): exx = εxx, …, exy = eyx = εxy + εyx, …. Symmetric (57). The antisymmetric part of ε is just a small rotation of the body, which produces no stress, so it is left out.
Factor-of-2 convention Joshi uses the engineering shear strain exy = εxy + εyx. Many physics books use ½(εxy + εyx) instead, so that eij transforms exactly as a tensor. Check which one a problem uses.

Hooke's law

Xij = Cijklekl  (54),    eij = SijklXkl  (55),    CijklSklmn = δimδjn  (56)

Cijkl = elastic stiffness constants (moduli), Sijkl = compliance constants. Both are rank-4 tensors and reciprocals of each other (conjugate, as in Section 17).

10. Dielectric susceptibility and piezoelectricity (Eqs. 60–62)

  • Dielectric susceptibility (rank 2): Pi = χijEj.  (60)   In an anisotropic crystal P need not be parallel to E. Isotropic: χij = χδij.
  • Piezoelectric strain coefficients (rank 3): stress alone polarizes crystals like quartz: Pi = dijkXjk.  (61)   Since Xjk is symmetric, dijk = dikj: 3 × 6 = 18 independent components rather than 27.
  • Both together (small fields, no interaction): Pi = dijkXjk + χijEj.  (62)
Why no piezoelectricity in centrosymmetric crystals Under inversion (aij = −δij, an improper rotation) a rank-3 tensor changes as d̄ijk = (−1)³dijk = −dijk. If the crystal looks the same after inversion, d̄ = d, so d = 0. Of the 32 crystal classes, the 21 without a centre of symmetry can be piezoelectric (all but one actually are). This is Neumann's principle at work.

11. Moment of inertia tensor (Eqs. 63–67)

For point masses mi at ri rotating with angular velocity ω (vi = ω × ri):

L = Σ mi ri × (ω × ri) = Σ mi[ω ri² − ri(ri·ω)]   (63, 64)

using a × (b × c) = (a·c)b − (a·b)c. In components, Li = Iijωj (67), with

Ixx = Σm(r² − x²) = Σm(y² + z²),   Ixy = Iyx = −Σm xy,   etc.   (66)    i.e.   Iij = Σm(r²δij − xixj)
  • L and ω are vectors, so by the quotient law Iij is a rank-2 tensor, the moment of inertia tensor. It is real and symmetric.
  • L is not necessarily parallel to ω. That is why an unbalanced wheel wobbles.
  • Diagonalize I by a rotation (Section 10): the eigenvectors are the principal axes and the eigenvalues I₁, I₂, I₃ are the principal moments. About a principal axis, L ∥ ω.
  • Kinetic energy T = ½ωiIijωj is a quadratic form (Section 11), and ωiIijωj = 1 is the inertia ellipsoid.
  • Moment about a unit axis n: In = niIijnj.

15. Practice set (CSIR-NET / GATE style)

P1. Is a = [[0, 1, 0], [0, 0, 1], [1, 0, 0]] a proper rotation? Find its axis and angle. rotation
Show solution

Orthogonal with det +1. Trace 0 ⇒ cos θ = −½ ⇒ θ = 120°. Axis (1, 1, 1)/√3 (eigenvalue 1). It is the transpose of Exercise 3(b): the same rotation in the opposite sense.

P2. How do the components of a rank-3 Cartesian tensor change under inversion x̄ = −x? parity
Show solution

aij = −δij ⇒ T̄ijk = (−1)³Tijk = −Tijk. Even-rank tensors are unchanged. So centrosymmetric crystals have zero odd-rank properties (no piezoelectricity, no pyroelectricity).

P3. Find the principal moments of four equal masses m at (±a, ±a, 0). inertia
Show solution

Ixx = Σm y² = 4ma², Iyy = 4ma², Izz = Σm(x² + y²) = 8ma², products Σxy = 0. Principal moments 4ma², 4ma², 8ma² (perpendicular-axis theorem: Izz = Ixx + Iyy for a flat body).

P4. A dumbbell (masses m at ±(a, a, 0)/√2) rotates with ω along the x axis. Find L and the angle between L and ω. L vs ω
Show solution

Positions ±(a/√2)(1, 1, 0). I = 2m(a²δij − xixj) with x = (a/√2)(1, 1, 0): I = m a² [[1, −1, 0], [−1, 1, 0], [0, 0, 2]]. ω = ω(1, 0, 0) ⇒ L = ma²ω(1, −1, 0). Angle between L and x̂: 45°. The rod lies along (1, 1, 0); L is perpendicular to the rod, as it must be.

P5. Show that the only isotropic vector is zero. isotropy
Show solution

A rotation by π about any axis perpendicular to u sends u to −u. Isotropy needs −u = u ⇒ u = 0.

P6. A strain has ε = [[0.01, 0.003], [0.003, −0.004]] (2D). Find the area change and the principal strains. strain
Show solution

ΔA/A = trace = 0.006. Eigenvalues: mean 0.003, radius √(0.007² + 0.003²) ≈ 0.00762 ⇒ 0.01062 and −0.00462.

P7. Count the independent components of a symmetric rank-2 Cartesian tensor and of an isotropic rank-4 tensor. count
Show solution

6 and 3 (a, b, c in Eq. 46). For elasticity the symmetries reduce the 3 to 2 (Lamé constants).

P8. Show that AijBij is invariant under orthogonal transformations. invariant

17. Interesting facts

The tennis-racket effect Spin a racket (or a phone) about its axis of intermediate moment I₂ and it flips over unexpectedly. Rotation about the largest and smallest principal axes is stable; about the middle one it is not. Astronaut Vladimir Dzhanibekov saw a wing nut doing this in orbit in 1985 (the "Dzhanibekov effect").
Quartz watches A tuning fork cut from quartz vibrates at 32 768 Hz = 2¹⁵ Hz. The piezoelectric tensor dijk converts its mechanical vibration into an electric signal and back. The crystal cut is chosen along special directions where the frequency hardly changes with temperature.
Cauchy and the stress tensor Augustin-Louis Cauchy introduced the stress tensor in 1822. It was the first tensor in physics, named "tension". Voigt later introduced the 6×6 matrix notation C11, C12, C44, … still used for elastic constants.
Rodrigues' formula (1840) Olinde Rodrigues found Eq. (29) and the composition law for rotations before quaternions were invented. Rotations about different axes do not commute, but Rodrigues' parameters tell you exactly how they combine.
