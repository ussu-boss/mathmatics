14.2 Analytic Functions

Goals

  • Compute $f'(z)$ from $u$ and $v$.

1. The derivative and the definition of "analytic"

Definition (2.1) $$f'(z)=\frac{df}{dz}=\lim_{\Delta z\to0}\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta z},\qquad \Delta f=f(z+\Delta z)-f(z),\quad\Delta z=\Delta x+i\Delta y .$$
Definition

$f(z)$ is analytic (also called regular, holomorphic or monogenic) in a region if it has a (unique) derivative at every point of the region. "$f$ is analytic at $z=a$" means $f$ has a derivative at every point inside some small circle about $a$. Isolated points and curves are not regions: a region must be two-dimensional.

Why this is so demanding

For $f(x)$ on the real line, $\Delta x\to0$ from only two directions (left and right). For $f(z)$, $\Delta z$ can approach $0$ from infinitely many directions, or along spirals, and the limit must be the same every time (Boas Figs. 2.1, 2.2).

Example 1 — $\frac{d}{dz}z^2=2z$
$$\frac{(z+\Delta z)^2-z^2}{\Delta z}=\frac{2z\,\Delta z+(\Delta z)^2}{\Delta z}=2z+\Delta z\ \xrightarrow{\ \Delta z\to0\ }\ 2z,$$

with no dependence on the direction of $\Delta z$, so $z^2$ is analytic. In the same way $(z^n)'=nz^{n-1}$ (Problem 30).

Example 2 — $|z|^2$ has no derivative (except at $0$)

$\dfrac{|z+\Delta z|^2-|z|^2}{\Delta z}$: the numerator is always real, but the denominator $\Delta z$ is real along a horizontal approach, imaginary along a vertical one, and complex in general. So the quotient depends on the direction.

Explicitly, $\dfrac{\Delta(z\bar z)}{\Delta z}=\bar z+z\dfrac{\overline{\Delta z}}{\Delta z}+\overline{\Delta z}\to\bar z+z\,e^{-2i\phi}$, where $\phi$ is the direction of approach. This takes a whole circle of values unless $z=0$.

H.K. Dass §22.8–22.9Differentiability: the same answer from every direction

$f$ is differentiable at $z$ if

$$f'(z)=\lim_{\delta z\to0}\frac{f(z+\delta z)-f(z)}{\delta z}$$

exists and is the same along every path by which $Q=z+\delta z$ approaches $P=z$.

Worked example (Dass §22.8) — $f=(4x+y)+i(-x+4y)$: three paths, one answer
  1. $\frac{\delta f}{\delta z}=\frac{4\delta x+\delta y+i(-\delta x+4\delta y)}{\delta x+i\delta y}$.
  2. Along the real axis ($\delta y=0$): $4-i$. Along the imaginary axis ($\delta x=0$): $\frac{1+4i}{i}=4-i$. Along $y=x$ ($\delta y=\delta x$): $\frac{5+3i}{1+i}=4-i$.
  3. All paths agree, and indeed $f=(4-i)(x+iy)=(4-i)z$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: if an expression in $x,y$ secretly equals $cz+d$, it is differentiable everywhere. Spotting this saves the whole Cauchy–Riemann check.

Worked example (Dass §22.8) — $f=\dfrac{x^3y(y-ix)}{x^6+y^2}$, $f(0)=0$: straight lines are not enough
  1. $\frac{f(z)-f(0)}{z}=\frac{-ix^3y}{x^6+y^2}$ (because $y-ix=-i(x+iy)$).
  2. Along $y=mx$: $\frac{-imx^4}{x^6+m^2x^2}=\frac{-imx^2}{x^4+m^2}\to0$ for every line.
  3. Along the curve $y=x^3$: $\frac{-ix^6}{2x^6}=-\frac i2\ne0$. So $f'(0)$ does not exist.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: "the limit is $0$ along every straight line" does not prove differentiability. A parabola- or cubic-type path is the standard counterexample.

Theorem (§22.8): differentiable ⇒ continuous, but not conversely

Proof. $f(z_0+\delta z)-f(z_0)=\delta z\cdot\frac{f(z_0+\delta z)-f(z_0)}{\delta z}\to0\cdot f'(z_0)=0$, so $f(z)\to f(z_0)$.

The converse fails: $f=|z|^2=x^2+y^2$ is continuous everywhere (it is a polynomial in $x,y$). But

$$\frac{|z+\delta z|^2-|z|^2}{\delta z}=\bar z+\overline{\delta z}+z\,\frac{\overline{\delta z}}{\delta z},\qquad\frac{\overline{\delta z}}{\delta z}=e^{-2i\theta},$$

which depends on the direction $\theta$ unless $z=0$. So $|z|^2$ is differentiable only at $z=0$, where $f'(0)=0$.

Analytic, entire, singular (§22.9)

Analytic at $z_0$ (also called holomorphic, regular, monogenic)differentiable at $z_0$ and at every point of some neighbourhood of $z_0$

The chain of implications is: entire ⇒ analytic ⇒ differentiable ⇒ continuous, and none of the arrows reverses. Example: $|z|^2$ is differentiable at $0$, but it is not analytic at $0$, because no neighbourhood of $0$ consists of points of differentiability. (H.K. Dass's Example 15 calls $z\bar z$ "analytic at $z=0$". Strictly it is only differentiable there.)

2. The Cauchy–Riemann equations

Theorem I (proved)

If $f(z)=u(x,y)+iv(x,y)$ is analytic in a region, then in that region

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial v}{\partial y},\qquad\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}=-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}}\qquad(2.2)$$

Proof. Since $f=f(z)$ with $z=x+iy$, the chain rule gives (2.3)

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=\frac{df}{dz}\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=\frac{df}{dz}\cdot1,\qquad\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=\frac{df}{dz}\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=\frac{df}{dz}\cdot i .$$

And from $f=u+iv$ (2.4): $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=u_x+iv_x$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=u_y+iv_y$. So

$$\frac{df}{dz}=u_x+iv_x\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{df}{dz}=\frac1i(u_y+iv_y)=v_y-iu_y .$$

The derivative is unique, so the two expressions are equal. Equating real and imaginary parts gives $u_x=v_y$ and $v_x=-u_y$. $\blacksquare$

Useful by-product: the derivative from $u,v$ $$f'(z)=u_x+iv_x=v_y-iu_y\ \ (=u_x-iu_y).$$
Theorem II (stated)

If $u$, $v$ and their first partial derivatives are continuous and satisfy the Cauchy–Riemann equations in a region, then $f=u+iv$ is analytic at all points inside the region (not necessarily on the boundary).

Theorem I says CR is necessary. Theorem II says CR plus continuity is sufficient.

Boas Example 3 — plausibility of Theorem II: approach along any straight line

Along a line of slope $m$ through $z_0$, $dy=m\,dx$:

$$\frac{df}{dz}=\frac{du+i\,dv}{dx+i\,dy}=\frac{u_x+u_ym+i(v_x+v_ym)}{1+im}\ \overset{\text{CR}}{=}\ \frac{u_x-v_xm+i(v_x+u_xm)}{1+im}=\frac{(u_x+iv_x)(1+im)}{1+im}=u_x+iv_x,$$

which does not depend on $m$. The theorem says the same holds for any curve.

How to use CR (and the traps)
  • If CR fails on a region, $f$ is not analytic there. If CR holds only on a line or at a point, $f$ is still not analytic anywhere, because analytic needs a 2-D region. Example: $z^2-\bar z^2$ satisfies CR only at $z=0$.
  • A quick screen: any honest dependence on $\bar z$ (including $|z|$, $\operatorname{Re}z$, $\operatorname{Im}z$) destroys analyticity. Analytic functions are functions of $z$ alone. (Precisely: $\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bar z}=0$ is the CR equations in one line.)
  • Rational functions, $e^z$, $\sin z$, … are analytic except where they blow up. $\sqrt z$ and $\ln z$ are analytic except at the branch point and on the cut.

H.K. Dass §22.10–22.11Cauchy–Riemann: the necessary condition and the sufficient condition, with proofs

§22.10 Necessary condition

If $f=u+iv$ is analytic in a region $R$, then $u_x$, $u_y$, $v_x$, $v_y$ exist and

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial v}{\partial y},\qquad\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=-\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}.$$

Proof (two paths). $\frac{\delta f}{\delta z}=\frac{\delta u}{\delta z}+i\frac{\delta v}{\delta z}$, and the limit must be the same along any path.

  • Along the real axis ($\delta z=\delta x$): $f'(z)=u_x+iv_x$.
  • Along the imaginary axis ($\delta z=i\delta y$): $f'(z)=\frac{u_y+iv_y}{i}=v_y-iu_y$.

Equating real and imaginary parts gives $u_x=v_y$ and $v_x=-u_y$. $\blacksquare$

§22.11 Sufficient condition

If (i) the Cauchy–Riemann equations hold in $R$ and (ii) $u_x$, $u_y$, $v_x$, $v_y$ are continuous in $R$, then $f$ is analytic in $R$.

Proof (Taylor's theorem in two variables). Continuity of the partials lets us write, to first order,

$$f(z+\delta z)-f(z)=(u_x+iv_x)\,\delta x+(u_y+iv_y)\,\delta y .$$

Replace $u_y$ by $-v_x$ and $v_y$ by $u_x$ (Cauchy–Riemann). The second bracket becomes $-v_x+iu_x=i(u_x+iv_x)$, so

$$f(z+\delta z)-f(z)=(u_x+iv_x)(\delta x+i\delta y)=(u_x+iv_x)\,\delta z .$$

Dividing by $\delta z$, the limit is $u_x+iv_x$, independent of the path. So $f'(z)$ exists, and

$$f'(z)=u_x+iv_x=v_y-iu_y .\qquad\blacksquare$$
Remember (Dass)
  1. Analytic ⇒ Cauchy–Riemann holds.
  2. Cauchy–Riemann alone is not sufficient (examples below).
  3. Cauchy–Riemann plus continuous partial derivatives is sufficient.

Worked example (Dass §22.11) — $\sin z$ is analytic and $\frac{d}{dz}\sin z=\cos z$
  1. $\sin z=\sin x\cosh y+i\cos x\sinh y$, so $u=\sin x\cosh y$ and $v=\cos x\sinh y$.
  2. $u_x=\cos x\cosh y=v_y$ and $u_y=\sin x\sinh y=-v_x$. These are continuous everywhere, so $\sin z$ is entire.
  3. $f'=u_x+iv_x=\cos x\cosh y-i\sin x\sinh y=\cos(x+iy)=\cos z$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: $f'=u_x+iv_x$ turns every Cauchy–Riemann check into a derivative for free. Use it to confirm $(e^z)'=e^z$, $(\log z)'=\frac1z$, $(\frac1z)'=-\frac{1}{z^2}$.

Worked example (Dass §22.11) — $\log z$ satisfies Cauchy–Riemann for $z\ne0$, and $(\log z)'=\frac1z$
  1. $u=\frac12\log(x^2+y^2)$ and $v=\tan^{-1}\frac yx$.
  2. $u_x=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}=v_y$ and $u_y=\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}=-v_x$, which are continuous except at $0$.
  3. $f'=u_x+iv_x=\frac{x-iy}{x^2+y^2}=\frac{1}{x+iy}=\frac1z$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: $\log z$ is the model "analytic except at a point (and on a cut)" function. Its derivative $\frac1z$ gives the $2\pi i$ in contour integrals.

Worked example (Dass §22.11) — Cauchy–Riemann holds at $0$ but $f$ is not differentiable: $f=\dfrac{x^3(1+i)-y^3(1-i)}{x^2+y^2}$, $f(0)=0$
  1. $u=\frac{x^3-y^3}{x^2+y^2}$ and $v=\frac{x^3+y^3}{x^2+y^2}$.
  2. From first principles at $0$: $u_x=\lim\frac{h^3/h^2}{h}=1$, $u_y=-1$, $v_x=1$, $v_y=1$. So $u_x=v_y$ and $u_y=-v_x$: Cauchy–Riemann holds.
  3. But $\frac{f(z)}{z}$ along $y=0$ is $1+i$, and along $y=x$ is $\frac{2ix^3/2x^2}{x(1+i)}=\frac{i}{1+i}=\frac{1+i}2$. They differ, so $f'(0)$ does not exist.
  4. The reason: the partial derivatives are not continuous at $0$, so the sufficient condition fails.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: multiple-select questions like "Cauchy–Riemann is satisfied at $0$, therefore $f$ is analytic at $0$ — true or false?" have answer false. Other standard counterexamples are $\sqrt{|xy|}$, $\frac{x^2y^5(x+iy)}{x^4+y^{10}}$ and $e^{-z^{-4}}$.

Worked example (Dass §22.11) — find $C_1,C_2$ so that $f=x^2+C_1y^2-2xy+i(C_2x^2-y^2+2xy)$ is analytic
  1. $u_x=2x-2y$ and $v_y=-2y+2x$: equal for all $C$.
  2. $u_y=2C_1y-2x$ must equal $-v_x=-2C_2x-2y$ for all $x,y$. So $2C_1=-2$ and $-2=-2C_2$, giving $C_1=-1$ and $C_2=1$.
  3. $f'=u_x+iv_x=(2x-2y)+i(2x+2y)=2(1+i)z$, so $f=(1+i)z^2$ (+ const).

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: "find the constants for which $f$ is analytic" is a staple question. Match the coefficients of $x$ and $y$ in the second Cauchy–Riemann equation.

3. Regular and singular points; Theorem III

  • A regular point of $f$ is a point where $f$ is analytic.
  • A singular point (singularity) is a point where $f$ is not analytic. It is isolated if $f$ is analytic everywhere else inside some small circle about it (e.g. $z=0$ for $1/z$; but not $z=0$ for $\ln z$, whose cut runs into it).
Theorem III (stated)

If $f(z)$ is analytic in a region $R$, then it has derivatives of all orders at points inside $R$, and can be expanded in a Taylor series about any point $z_0$ inside $R$. The series converges inside the circle about $z_0$ that extends to the nearest singular point.

This is astonishing compared with real functions: $f(x)=x|x|$ has a first derivative but no second. For $f(z)$, one derivative everywhere in a region means infinitely many, and a convergent Taylor series.

It also explains (Boas eqs. 2.5–2.6) why $\frac1{1+x^2}=1-x^2+x^4-\cdots$ converges only for $|x|\lt1$: the complex function $\frac{1}{1+z^2}$ blows up at $z=\pm i$, at distance 1 from the origin. See Interactive 1 of Chapter 2, §7 for a full visualisation.

H.K. Dass §25.6–25.7Taylor's theorem: proof, the three ways to get the coefficients, and the circle of convergence

Taylor's theorem

If $f$ is analytic at all points inside a circle $C$ with centre $a$, then at every $z$ inside $C$

$$f(z)=f(a)+f'(a)(z-a)+\frac{f''(a)}{2!}(z-a)^2+\cdots+\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(z-a)^n+\cdots$$

The series converges in the largest open disk about $a$ in which $f$ is analytic. Its radius is the distance from $a$ to the nearest singularity.

Dass's proof

  1. Fix $z$ inside $C$. Draw a circle $C_1$ about $a$ that encloses $z$, and let $w$ be on $C_1$. Then $|z-a|\lt|w-a|$.
  2. Expand the Cauchy kernel as a geometric series: $$\frac1{w-z}=\frac{1}{(w-a)-(z-a)}=\frac{1}{w-a}\left(1-\frac{z-a}{w-a}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(z-a)^n}{(w-a)^{n+1}} .$$ Since $\left|\frac{z-a}{w-a}\right|\lt1$ is fixed on $C_1$, the convergence is uniform (M-test), so we may integrate term by term.
  3. Multiply by $\frac{f(w)}{2\pi i}$ and integrate over $C_1$: $f(z)=\sum_n(z-a)^n\cdot\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_{C_1}\frac{f(w)\,dw}{(w-a)^{n+1}}$.
  4. By the derivative formula (Dass §24.6), the $n$-th integral is $\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}$. ∎

Corollaries. With $z=a+h$: $f(a+h)=\sum\frac{h^n}{n!}f^{(n)}(a)$. With $a=0$: the Maclaurin series $f(z)=\sum\frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}z^n$.

Three ways to get the coefficients (§25.6). Use the cheapest.

  1. Differentiate: $a_n=\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}$. Good for $e^z$, $\cosh z$, $\cos z$ and $\log z$ (Dass Ex 5, 7, 9, 16).
  2. Binomial/geometric series after a shift $z=a+t$ and partial fractions. Good for rational functions (Ex 6, 8, 10–15).
  3. Known series: substitute, multiply or divide known expansions, or integrate a known series term by term. Good for $\frac{\sin z}{z-\pi}$, $\sin^{-1}z$ and $\tan^{-1}z$ (Ex 17–19).

Worked example (Dass Ex 5–9) — expansions by differentiation and by binomial series

Ex 5: $e^z=e^a\left[1+(z-a)+\frac{(z-a)^2}{2!}+\cdots\right]$, valid for all $z$.

Ex 6: $\frac1z$ about $z=2$. $f^{(n)}(z)=\frac{(-1)^nn!}{z^{n+1}}$, so $\frac1z=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-1)^n}{2^{n+1}}(z-2)^n=\frac12-\frac{z-2}{4}+\frac{(z-2)^2}{8}-\cdots$. Alternatively $\frac{1}{2+(z-2)}=\frac12\left(1+\frac{z-2}2\right)^{-1}$. Both give $R=2$, the distance from $2$ to the pole at $0$.

Ex 7: $\cosh z$ about $\pi i$. $\cosh\pi i=\cos\pi=-1$ and $\sinh\pi i=i\sin\pi=0$. So $\cosh z=-\left[1+\frac{(z-\pi i)^2}{2!}+\frac{(z-\pi i)^4}{4!}+\cdots\right]=-\cosh(z-\pi i)$, which you can check directly.

Ex 8: $\frac{a}{bz+c}$ about $z_0$. $\frac{a}{bz_0+c}\sum_n\left(\frac{-b(z-z_0)}{bz_0+c}\right)^n$, with $R=\left|z_0+\frac cb\right|$, the distance to the pole $-\frac cb$.

Ex 9: $\log z=(z-1)-\frac{(z-1)^2}{2}+\frac{(z-1)^3}{3}-\cdots$ (principal branch, $|z-1|\lt1$). It comes from $f^{(n)}(1)=(-1)^{n-1}(n-1)!$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: "the radius of convergence of the Taylor series of $f$ about $a$" is answered by locating singularities, never by computing coefficients.

Worked example (Dass Ex 10, 11, 14) — the same function, but which series?

Ex 10: $\frac{1}{z^2-3z+2}=\frac1{z-2}-\frac1{z-1}$.

  • (a) For $|z|\lt1$, both terms are expanded in powers of $z$: $-\frac12\left(1-\frac z2\right)^{-1}+(1-z)^{-1}=\frac12+\frac34z+\frac78z^2+\frac{15}{16}z^3+\cdots$, i.e. $\sum\left(1-\frac1{2^{n+1}}\right)z^n$.
  • (b) For $|z|\gt2$, both are expanded in powers of $\frac1z$: $\frac1z\left(1-\frac2z\right)^{-1}-\frac1z\left(1-\frac1z\right)^{-1}=\frac1{z^2}+\frac3{z^3}+\frac7{z^4}+\cdots$. This is a Laurent series, not a Taylor series; see 14.4.

Ex 11: for $|z+1|\lt1$, $\frac1{z^2}=\left[1-(z+1)\right]^{-2}=\sum_{n\ge0}(n+1)(z+1)^n$.

Ex 14: "all Taylor series of $\frac{4z-1}{z^4-1}$ about $0$". There is only one Taylor series about a given centre. Quickest route: $\frac{1-4z}{1-z^4}=(1-4z)(1+z^4+z^8+\cdots)=1-4z+z^4-4z^5+z^8-\cdots$, valid for $|z|\lt1$ (the poles $\pm1,\pm i$ are all at distance $1$). For $|z|\gt1$ the expansion is a Laurent series.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: "factor out the bigger term" (Dass's phrase) is the whole algorithm. Inside a circle, divide by the constant. Outside it, divide by $z$.

Worked example (Dass Ex 12, 13, 15) — Taylor series about a point other than $0$, and the region of convergence

Ex 12: $\frac{z+1}{(z-3)(z-4)}$ about $z=2$. The singularities $3$ and $4$ are at distances $1$ and $2$, so $R=1$. With $\frac{-4}{z-3}+\frac{5}{z-4}=4\sum(z-2)^n-\frac52\sum\left(\frac{z-2}2\right)^n$: $$\frac32+\frac{11}4(z-2)+\frac{27}8(z-2)^2+\frac{59}{16}(z-2)^3+\cdots,\qquad|z-2|\lt1 .$$ Dass checks it with $f(2)=\frac32$, $f'(2)=\frac{11}4$, $f''(2)=\frac{27}4$, $f'''(2)=\frac{177}8$.

Ex 13: $\frac1{z^2+4}$ about $z=-i$. The poles $\pm2i$ are at distances $3$ and $1$, so $R=1$. The result is $\frac13+\frac{2i}9(z+i)-\frac{7}{27}(z+i)^2-\frac{20i}{81}(z+i)^3+\cdots$

Ex 15: $\frac{2z^3+1}{z^2+z}$ about $z=1$. First divide: $\frac{2z^3+1}{z(z+1)}=2z-2+\frac1z+\frac1{z+1}$. The poles $0$ and $-1$ are at distances $1$ and $2$, so $R=1$. Expanding $\frac1{1+(z-1)}$ and $\frac{1}{2+(z-1)}$: $$\frac32+\frac34(z-1)+\frac98(z-1)^2-\frac{17}{16}(z-1)^3+\cdots .$$ The linear coefficient $\frac34=2-1-\frac14$ is easy to slip on: remember to count $2z-2=2(z-1)$ when collecting it.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: "the radius of convergence about $z=a$ is the distance to the nearest pole" also works for complex centres such as $-i$. Draw the points.

Worked example (Dass Ex 16–19) — using known series

Ex 16: $\cos z=\frac1{\sqrt2}\left[1-\left(z-\frac\pi4\right)-\frac{(z-\pi/4)^2}{2!}+\frac{(z-\pi/4)^3}{3!}+\cdots\right]$. The signs follow the pattern $+,-,-,+$.

Ex 17: with $t=z-\pi$, $\frac{\sin z}{z-\pi}=\frac{-\sin t}{t}=-1+\frac{(z-\pi)^2}{3!}-\frac{(z-\pi)^4}{5!}+\cdots$. The point $z=\pi$ is a removable singularity.

Ex 18: $\frac{d}{dz}\sin^{-1}z=(1-z^2)^{-1/2}=1+\frac{z^2}2+\frac38z^4+\frac5{16}z^6+\cdots$. Integrating, with $\sin^{-1}0=0$: $\sin^{-1}z=z+\frac{z^3}{6}+\frac{3z^5}{40}+\frac{5z^7}{112}+\cdots$, for $|z|\lt1$.

Ex 19: $\frac{d}{dz}\tan^{-1}z=\frac1{1+z^2}=1-z^2+z^4-\cdots$, so $\tan^{-1}z=z-\frac{z^3}3+\frac{z^5}5-\cdots$, for $|z|\lt1$ (the singularities are $\pm i$).

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: "integrate the series of the derivative" is the fastest way to get $\sin^{-1}$, $\tan^{-1}$, $\log(1+z)$ and $\tanh^{-1}$. Each converges for $|z|\lt1$.

4. Harmonic functions (Theorem IV)

Definition

$\phi(x,y)$ is harmonic if it satisfies Laplace's equation $\nabla^2\phi=\phi_{xx}+\phi_{yy}=0$.

Theorem IV

Part 1 (Problem 44). If $f=u+iv$ is analytic in a region, then $u$ and $v$ are harmonic there.

Proof: $u_{xx}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}v_y=v_{yx}$ and $u_{yy}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(-v_x)=-v_{xy}$. Mixed partials are equal (Theorem III guarantees continuity), so $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0$. The same works for $v$.

Part 2 (stated). Any harmonic $u$ in a simply connected region is the real (or imaginary) part of some analytic $f(z)$.

So every analytic function hands you two solutions of Laplace's equation. This is the foundation of 2-D electrostatics, heat flow and fluid flow (Section 14.10). $u$ and $v$ are called conjugate harmonic functions.

Geometric meaning: the level curves are orthogonal

By CR, $\nabla u\cdot\nabla v=u_xv_x+u_yv_y=u_x(-u_y)+u_y(u_x)=0$. So the curves $u=$const and $v=$const cross at right angles wherever $f'\ne0$. In physics these are equipotentials and field lines, or isotherms and heat-flow lines.

Finding $v$ (and $f$) from $u$ — Boas Example 4

$u=x^2-y^2$
  1. Check that it is harmonic: $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=2-2=0$ ✔.
  2. CR: $v_y=u_x=2x$. Integrate in $y$: $v=2xy+g(x)$.
  3. CR: $v_x=2y+g'(x)$ must equal $-u_y=2y$. So $g'(x)=0$ and $g=$const.
  4. $f=u+iv=x^2-y^2+2ixy+\text{const}=z^2+\text{const}$.
Shortcut (Boas Problem 64, Struble 1979)

If $u$ is harmonic and defined at $z_0$, then $f(z)=2u\!\left(\dfrac{z+\bar z_0}{2},\ \dfrac{z-\bar z_0}{2i}\right)+\text{const}$. With $z_0=0$ (if $u(0,0)$ is defined): $\ f(z)=2u\!\left(\frac z2,\frac z{2i}\right)+\text{const}$.

Example: $u=x^2-y^2$ gives $2\left[\frac{z^2}4-\frac{z^2}{(2i)^2}\right]=2\left[\frac{z^2}{4}+\frac{z^2}{4}\right]=z^2$ ✔. A related, easier trick for most problems: put $y=0$ and look for $f(x)$ (the Milne-Thomson idea).

H.K. Dass §22.14–22.15Orthogonal curves and harmonic functions

§22.14 Orthogonal system

If $f=u+iv$ is analytic, the two families $u(x,y)=c_1$ and $v(x,y)=c_2$ cut each other at right angles.

Proof. Differentiating $u=c_1$: $u_x\,dx+u_y\,dy=0$, so the slope is $m_1=-\frac{u_x}{u_y}$. Likewise $m_2=-\frac{v_x}{v_y}$. By Cauchy–Riemann ($v_x=-u_y$, $v_y=u_x$):

$$m_1m_2=\left(-\frac{u_x}{u_y}\right)\left(\frac{u_y}{u_x}\right)=-1 .\qquad\blacksquare$$

(This holds wherever $f'\ne0$. At a critical point the curves may cross at other angles.)

§22.15 Harmonic functions

A function satisfying Laplace's equation $\phi_{xx}+\phi_{yy}=0$ is harmonic. If $f=u+iv$ is analytic (with continuous second partials), then $u$ and $v$ are both harmonic.

Proof. Differentiate $u_x=v_y$ with respect to $x$, and $u_y=-v_x$ with respect to $y$: $u_{xx}=v_{yx}$ and $u_{yy}=-v_{xy}$. Adding gives $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0$, since mixed partials are equal. The same argument works for $v$. $\blacksquare$

If $u+iv$ is analytic, $v$ is called a harmonic conjugate of $u$.

  1. $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0+0=0$, so $u$ is harmonic.

Worked example (Dass §22.15) — $u=\frac12\log(x^2+y^2)$ is harmonic, with conjugate $\tan^{-1}\frac yx$
  1. $u_{xx}=\frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}$ and $u_{yy}=\frac{x^2-y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}$. The sum is $0$.
  2. $dv=-\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}dx+\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}dy=d\left(\tan^{-1}\frac yx\right)$, so $v=\tan^{-1}\frac yx+C$. Together, $f=\log z$.

Worked example (Dass §22.15) — both harmonic, but not conjugates: $u=x^2-y^2$, $v=\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}$
  1. $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=2-2=0$ and $v=-\operatorname{Im}\frac1z$ is harmonic too.
  2. But $u_x=2x\ne v_y=\frac{x^2-y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}$, so $u+iv$ is not analytic.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: harmonic is necessary for a conjugate pair, but not sufficient. A pair must also satisfy Cauchy–Riemann. This is a common true/false item.

Worked example (Dass §22.15) — $f(x,y)=x^2+Axy+By^2$ is harmonic exactly when $B=-1$ ($A$ arbitrary)
  1. $f_{xx}+f_{yy}=2+2B$, which must be $0$, so $B=-1$.
  2. Then $x^2-y^2+Axy=\operatorname{Re}\left[\left(1-\frac{iA}2\right)z^2\right]$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: "for which constants is $u$ harmonic" needs only the Laplacian, and the $xy$ term never matters.

Worked example (Dass §22.15) — if $u$ and $v$ are harmonic, then $F=(u_y-v_x)+i(u_x+v_y)$ is analytic
  1. Let $R=u_y-v_x$ and $S=u_x+v_y$.
  2. $R_x=u_{xy}-v_{xx}$ and $S_y=u_{xy}+v_{yy}=u_{xy}-v_{xx}$ (using $\nabla^2v=0$). So $R_x=S_y$.
  3. $R_y=u_{yy}-v_{xy}=-u_{xx}-v_{xy}$ and $S_x=u_{xx}+v_{xy}$ (using $\nabla^2u=0$). So $R_y=-S_x$. Cauchy–Riemann holds. $\blacksquare$

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: it shows how Laplace's equation for two functions combines into a single analytic function. This is the idea behind complex potentials in 2-D electrostatics.

H.K. Dass §22.18–22.20Finding the conjugate: exact differentials, and Milne-Thomson (all four cases)

Method of exact differentials (§22.18)

Case I ($u$ given). By Cauchy–Riemann, $dv=v_x\,dx+v_y\,dy=-u_y\,dx+u_x\,dy=M\,dx+N\,dy$. This is exact, because $M_y=-u_{yy}=u_{xx}=N_x$ by Laplace. So

$$v=\int M\,dx\ (\text{treat }y\text{ as constant})+\int(\text{terms of }N\text{ free of }x)\,dy .$$

Case II ($v$ given). $du=u_x\,dx+u_y\,dy=v_y\,dx-v_x\,dy$, integrated the same way.

Milne-Thomson method (§22.19)

Since $x=\frac{z+\bar z}2$ and $y=\frac{z-\bar z}{2i}$, $f(z)=u\left(\frac{z+\bar z}2,\frac{z-\bar z}{2i}\right)+iv(\cdots)$ is a formal identity in $z$ and $\bar z$. Putting $\bar z=z$ (i.e. $x=z$, $y=0$) gives $f(z)=u(z,0)+iv(z,0)$. Applied to $f'=u_x-iu_y$, this gives a working rule that needs no integration of $v$.

5. Cauchy–Riemann in polar coordinates (Problems 46–47)

With $z=re^{i\theta}$ and $f=u(r,\theta)+iv(r,\theta)$: $\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}=f'(z)e^{i\theta}$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}=f'(z)\,ire^{i\theta}$. So $\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}=ir\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}$, i.e. $u_\theta+iv_\theta=ir(u_r+iv_r)$:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}=\frac1r\frac{\partial v}{\partial\theta},\qquad\frac{\partial v}{\partial r}=-\frac1r\frac{\partial u}{\partial\theta}}$$

These lead (Problem 47) to Laplace's equation in polar form: $u_{rr}+\frac1ru_r+\frac1{r^2}u_{\theta\theta}=0$.

H.K. Dass §22.12–22.13Cauchy–Riemann in polar form, and the two polar formulas for $\frac{dw}{dz}$

Proof of the polar Cauchy–Riemann equations (§22.12). Write $u+iv=f(z)=f(re^{i\theta})$ and differentiate partially:

$$u_r+iv_r=f'(re^{i\theta})\,e^{i\theta},\qquad u_\theta+iv_\theta=f'(re^{i\theta})\,ire^{i\theta}.$$

Comparing, the second equals $ir$ times the first: $u_\theta+iv_\theta=ir(u_r+iv_r)=-rv_r+iru_r$. Equating real and imaginary parts:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}=\frac1r\frac{\partial v}{\partial\theta},\qquad\frac{\partial v}{\partial r}=-\frac1r\frac{\partial u}{\partial\theta}}$$

Derivative in polar form (§22.13)

Use the chain rule $\frac{\partial}{\partial x}=\cos\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial r}-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}$ on $\frac{dw}{dz}=\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}$. Then replace the $\theta$-derivatives by $r$-derivatives (or the other way round) with the polar Cauchy–Riemann equations. This gives two equivalent forms:

$$\frac{dw}{dz}=(\cos\theta-i\sin\theta)\frac{\partial w}{\partial r}=e^{-i\theta}\frac{\partial w}{\partial r},\qquad\frac{dw}{dz}=-\frac ir\,e^{-i\theta}\frac{\partial w}{\partial\theta}.$$

A third, equivalent form (Dass Example 39) is $f'(z)=\frac rz\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}+i\frac{\partial v}{\partial r}\right)$. The polar equations also give Laplace's equation in polar form: $u_{rr}+\frac1ru_r+\frac1{r^2}u_{\theta\theta}=0$.

P2. Show that an analytic function with constant modulus in a region is constant. Hard
Show solution

$u^2+v^2=c$. Differentiate: $uu_x+vv_x=0$ and $uu_y+vv_y=0$. Use CR to replace $u_y=-v_x$ and $v_y=u_x$: $\ uu_x+vv_x=0$ and $-uv_x+vu_x=0$. This linear system in $(u_x,v_x)$ has determinant $u^2+v^2=c$. If $c\ne0$, then $u_x=v_x=0$, so $f'=u_x+iv_x=0$ and $f$ is constant. (If $c=0$, $f\equiv0$.)

9. Going deeper: Milne-Thomson's shortcut, and "differentiable" vs "analytic"

Finding $f$ from $u$ in two lines (Milne-Thomson)

If $f=u+iv$ is analytic, then $f'(z)=u_x+iv_x=u_x-iu_y$ by Cauchy–Riemann. The right side is a function of $z$ alone, so we may evaluate it on the real axis ($x=z$, $y=0$). The identity theorem then guarantees the result holds everywhere:

$$\boxed{f'(z)=u_x(z,0)-i\,u_y(z,0)}\qquad\text{and similarly}\qquad f'(z)=v_y(z,0)+i\,v_x(z,0).$$

Integrate once and you have $f$, up to an imaginary constant (or a real one, if you started from $v$). No partial integrations and no "$+g(y)$" bookkeeping.

Example — $u=x^3-3xy^2+3x^2-3y^2$
  1. $u_x=3x^2-3y^2+6x$ and $u_y=-6xy-6y$.
  2. On the real axis: $u_x(z,0)=3z^2+6z$ and $u_y(z,0)=0$.
  3. $f'(z)=3z^2+6z$, so $f=z^3+3z^2+iC$.

The trick for $u-v$ or $u+v$

If you are given $U=u-v$, look at $F=(1+i)f=(u-v)+i(u+v)$. $F$ is analytic with real part $U$. Find $F$ by Milne-Thomson, then $f=F/(1+i)$.

Differentiable at a point ≠ analytic

Analytic at $z_0$ means differentiable at every point of some disk around $z_0$. Cauchy–Riemann can hold on a curve but on no open set:

$f$Where CR holdsAnalytic where?
$|z|^2=x^2+y^2$only at $z=0$nowhere

(For these polynomial examples the partial derivatives are continuous, so CR at a point really does give differentiability there.)

The conjugate of the conjugate

If $v$ is a harmonic conjugate of $u$ (i.e. $u+iv$ is analytic), then $-u$ is a harmonic conjugate of $v$, because $v-iu=-i(u+iv)$ is analytic. And $u^2-v^2$ and $uv$ are harmonic too, as the real and imaginary parts of $\frac12f^2$.

13. Summary

  • $f'(z)=\lim\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta z}$ must be the same from every direction. Analytic means differentiable throughout a 2-D region.
  • CR: $u_x=v_y$, $u_y=-v_x$ (necessary; with continuity, sufficient). Then $f'=u_x+iv_x$.
  • Analytic implies infinitely differentiable and a Taylor series out to the nearest singularity (Theorem III).
  • $u$ and $v$ are harmonic, and their level curves are orthogonal. Recover $f$ from $u$ via CR or Struble's formula.
