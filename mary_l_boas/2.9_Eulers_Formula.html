2.9 Euler's Formula

Boas, Chapter 2, Section 9 · $e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$: three proofs, what it means geometrically, and how it turns multiplication and division into "multiply lengths, add angles".

Goals
  • Derive Euler's formula from power series, and understand two other proofs.
  • Picture $e^{i\theta}$ as a point moving around the unit circle.
  • Evaluate expressions like $3e^{-i\pi/2}$, $\left(\frac{1-i}{\sqrt2}\right)^{42}$, $|4e^{2i-1}|$ in your head.
  • Use $z_1z_2=r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}$ and $z_1/z_2=\frac{r_1}{r_2}e^{i(\theta_1-\theta_2)}$ fluently.
  • Solve all of Boas's Section 9 problems (1–38).

1. Derivation from power series (Boas)

For real $\theta$ we know (Chapter 1):

$$\sin\theta=\theta-\frac{\theta^3}{3!}+\frac{\theta^5}{5!}-\cdots,\qquad\cos\theta=1-\frac{\theta^2}{2!}+\frac{\theta^4}{4!}-\cdots\qquad(9.1)$$

Put $z=i\theta$ in the definition $e^z=\sum z^n/n!$ (Section 2.8). The powers of $i$ cycle $i,-1,-i,1,\dots$:

$$e^{i\theta}=1+i\theta+\frac{(i\theta)^2}{2!}+\frac{(i\theta)^3}{3!}+\frac{(i\theta)^4}{4!}+\frac{(i\theta)^5}{5!}+\cdots=1+i\theta-\frac{\theta^2}{2!}-\frac{i\theta^3}{3!}+\frac{\theta^4}{4!}+\frac{i\theta^5}{5!}-\cdots$$

Collect the terms without $i$ and the terms with $i$. This rearrangement is allowed because the series converges absolutely:

$$e^{i\theta}=\left(1-\frac{\theta^2}{2!}+\frac{\theta^4}{4!}-\cdots\right)+i\left(\theta-\frac{\theta^3}{3!}+\frac{\theta^5}{5!}-\cdots\right).$$

The two brackets are exactly the series (9.1). Hence Euler's formula:

$$\boxed{\,e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\,}\qquad(9.3)$$

and so every complex number can be written as (9.4) $\ z=x+iy=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)=re^{i\theta}$.

Proof 2: by calculus (no series)

Let $f(\theta)=e^{-i\theta}(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$. Differentiating (with $\frac{d}{d\theta}e^{-i\theta}=-ie^{-i\theta}$):

$$f'(\theta)=-ie^{-i\theta}(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)+e^{-i\theta}(-\sin\theta+i\cos\theta)=e^{-i\theta}\big(-i\cos\theta+\sin\theta-\sin\theta+i\cos\theta\big)=0.$$

So $f$ is constant, and $f(0)=1$. Therefore $\cos\theta+i\sin\theta=e^{i\theta}$. $\blacksquare$

Proof 3: compound interest with an imaginary rate

$$e^{i\theta}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{i\theta}{n}\right)^n .$$

Each factor $1+\frac{i\theta}{n}$ is a tiny step at right angles to the current position, a rotation by about $\theta/n$ with almost no stretching (its modulus is $\sqrt{1+\theta^2/n^2}\to1$). After $n$ such steps you have turned through the angle $\theta$ on the unit circle. Interactive 2 shows this.

Euler's identity

$$e^{i\pi}+1=0$$ links the five most important constants $e,i,\pi,1,0$ with one addition, one multiplication and one exponentiation. Richard Feynman called Euler's formula "our jewel" and "the most remarkable formula in mathematics".

3. Multiplying and dividing in polar form

From $e^{z_1}e^{z_2}=e^{z_1+z_2}$ (Section 2.8):

$$e^{i\theta_1}e^{i\theta_2}=e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)},\qquad\frac{e^{i\theta_1}}{e^{i\theta_2}}=e^{i(\theta_1-\theta_2)}\qquad(9.5)$$ $$\boxed{z_1z_2=r_1r_2\,e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)},\qquad\frac{z_1}{z_2}=\frac{r_1}{r_2}\,e^{i(\theta_1-\theta_2)}}\qquad(9.6)$$

To multiply, multiply the absolute values and add the angles. To divide, divide the absolute values and subtract the angles.

Boas example — $\dfrac{(1+i)^2}{1-i}$
  1. $1+i=\sqrt2e^{i\pi/4}$ and $1-i=\sqrt2e^{-i\pi/4}$ (Fig. 9.5).
  2. $\dfrac{(\sqrt2e^{i\pi/4})^2}{\sqrt2e^{-i\pi/4}}=\dfrac{2e^{i\pi/2}}{\sqrt2e^{-i\pi/4}}=\sqrt2e^{3\pi i/4}$.
  3. From the sketch (Fig. 9.6), $x=-1$ and $y=1$: the answer is $-1+i$.
  4. In degrees: the angle is $2(45^\circ)-(-45^\circ)=135^\circ$.

Things Euler's formula gives you for free

  • Addition formulas: $e^{i(a+b)}=e^{ia}e^{ib}$. Expanding and comparing real and imaginary parts gives $\cos(a+b)=\cos a\cos b-\sin a\sin b$ and $\sin(a+b)=\sin a\cos b+\cos a\sin b$.
  • Conjugate: $\overline{e^{i\theta}}=e^{-i\theta}=\cos\theta-i\sin\theta$.
  • Modulus: $|e^{i\theta}|=\sqrt{\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta}=1$, and $|e^z|=|e^xe^{iy}|=e^x$ (Problem 27).
  • Half-angle factoring (very useful later): $$1+e^{i\theta}=e^{i\theta/2}\left(e^{-i\theta/2}+e^{i\theta/2}\right)=2\cos\tfrac\theta2\,e^{i\theta/2},\qquad 1-e^{i\theta}=-2i\sin\tfrac\theta2\,e^{i\theta/2}.$$

5. More worked examples

Simplify $\dfrac{\cos\theta+i\sin\theta}{\cos\phi-i\sin\phi}$

$=\dfrac{e^{i\theta}}{e^{-i\phi}}=e^{i(\theta+\phi)}=\cos(\theta+\phi)+i\sin(\theta+\phi)$.

Find $|e^{i\alpha}+e^{i\beta}|$
  1. Factor out the average angle: $e^{i\alpha}+e^{i\beta}=e^{i(\alpha+\beta)/2}\left(e^{i(\alpha-\beta)/2}+e^{-i(\alpha-\beta)/2}\right)$.
  2. $=2\cos\frac{\alpha-\beta}2\,e^{i(\alpha+\beta)/2}$.
  3. So $|e^{i\alpha}+e^{i\beta}|=2\left|\cos\frac{\alpha-\beta}2\right|$. This is the interference of two equal waves: total brightness when in phase, zero when out of phase by $\pi$.
Compute $(1+\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n$
  1. $1+e^{i\theta}=2\cos\frac\theta2\,e^{i\theta/2}$.
  2. So $(1+e^{i\theta})^n=2^n\cos^n\frac\theta2\,e^{in\theta/2}=2^n\cos^n\frac\theta2\left(\cos\frac{n\theta}2+i\sin\frac{n\theta}2\right)$.

6. Practice problems

P1. Evaluate $e^{i\pi/3}\cdot e^{i\pi/6}$ and $\dfrac{e^{i\pi/3}}{e^{i\pi/6}}$. Easy
Show solution

$e^{i\pi/2}=i$ and $e^{i\pi/6}=\frac{\sqrt3}2+\frac i2$.

P2. Evaluate $(\sqrt3+i)^{9}$. Medium
Show solution

$(2e^{i\pi/6})^9=512e^{3\pi i/2}=-512i$.

P3. Prove $\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1$ using Euler's formula. Easy
Show solution

$1=e^{i\theta}e^{-i\theta}=(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)(\cos\theta-i\sin\theta)=\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta$.

P4. Find $|1-e^{i\theta}|$ and interpret it geometrically. Medium
Show solution

$|1-e^{i\theta}|=|{-2i}\sin\frac\theta2\,e^{i\theta/2}|=2|\sin\frac\theta2|$: the length of the chord from $1$ to $e^{i\theta}$ on the unit circle.

P5. Show that $i^i$ is real by writing $i=e^{i\pi/2}$. Medium
Show solution

$i^i=(e^{i\pi/2})^i=e^{i\cdot i\pi/2}=e^{-\pi/2}\approx0.2079$. This is the principal value. Using $i=e^{i(\pi/2+2n\pi)}$ gives infinitely many real values $e^{-\pi/2-2n\pi}$ (Section 2.14).

P6. (NET) The value of $\left(\dfrac{1+i}{\sqrt2}\right)^{2026}$ is: Medium
Show solution

$e^{2026\pi i/4}=e^{1013\pi i/2}$. Since $1013=4\cdot253+1$, this is $e^{i\pi/2}=i$.

P7. Show that $\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}e^{in\theta}\,d\theta=\begin{cases}2\pi&n=0\\0&n\ne0\end{cases}$ for integer $n$. Medium
Show solution

For $n\ne0$: $\left[\frac{e^{in\theta}}{in}\right]_0^{2\pi}=\frac{e^{2\pi in}-1}{in}=0$. For $n=0$ the integrand is 1. (This orthogonality is the basis of Fourier series, Boas Ch. 7.)

P8. Prove $\cos(a-b)=\cos a\cos b+\sin a\sin b$ using $e^{i(a-b)}=e^{ia}\overline{e^{ib}}$. Easy
Show solution

$(\cos a+i\sin a)(\cos b-i\sin b)=(\cos a\cos b+\sin a\sin b)+i(\sin a\cos b-\cos a\sin b)$. The real part gives the identity (and the imaginary part gives $\sin(a-b)$).

8. Going deeper: Euler's formula beyond numbers — phasors and matrices

Phasor addition (waves of the same frequency)

$A\cos(\omega t+\alpha)=\operatorname{Re}\left[Ae^{i\alpha}e^{i\omega t}\right]$. The time factor $e^{i\omega t}$ is common to all the waves, so adding them is just adding the complex amplitudes:

$$\sum_kA_k\cos(\omega t+\alpha_k)=\operatorname{Re}\Big[\underbrace{\Big(\sum_kA_ke^{i\alpha_k}\Big)}_{Ce^{i\gamma}}e^{i\omega t}\Big]=C\cos(\omega t+\gamma).$$

So $3\cos\omega t+4\sin\omega t$ has phasor $3-4i$ (since $\sin\omega t=\cos(\omega t-\pi/2)$). Its amplitude is $5$, and its phase is $-\arctan\frac43$. Three equal waves $120^\circ$ apart (three-phase power) add to zero, because $1+\omega+\omega^2=0$.

Euler's formula for matrices (used in quantum mechanics)

The series proof of $e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ used only one fact: $i^2=-1$. Any square matrix $A$ with $A^2=-I$ gives, by the same rearrangement,

$$e^{\theta A}=I\cos\theta+A\sin\theta .$$

For a Pauli matrix $\sigma$ (with $\sigma^2=I$), take $A=i\sigma$. Then $A^2=-I$, and

$$\boxed{e^{i\theta\sigma}=I\cos\theta+i\sigma\sin\theta}\qquad\text{e.g. }e^{i\theta\sigma_y}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.$$

This is the spin-rotation operator $e^{-i\theta\,\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma/2}$ that appears in CSIR Quantum Mechanics questions. A rotation by $2\pi$ gives $-I$, the famous sign change of spinors.

Sums of cosines in arithmetic progression

Let $S=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}e^{i(a+kd)}$. This is a geometric series, and the half-angle factoring from section 3 simplifies it:

$$S=e^{ia}\frac{e^{ind}-1}{e^{id}-1}=e^{i\left(a+\frac{(n-1)d}{2}\right)}\frac{\sin(nd/2)}{\sin(d/2)}.$$

Taking real and imaginary parts gives $\sum\cos(a+kd)$ and $\sum\sin(a+kd)$. This is the formula behind the $N$-slit pattern in 2.16.

12. Summary

  • $e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ (series, calculus, or a limit), and $z=re^{i\theta}$.
  • $e^{i\pi}=-1$, $e^{i\pi/2}=i$, $e^{2n\pi i}=1$, $|e^{i\theta}|=1$, $|e^z|=e^{\operatorname{Re}z}$.
  • Multiply: $r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}$. Divide: $\frac{r_1}{r_2}e^{i(\theta_1-\theta_2)}$. For powers, multiply the angle and reduce it mod $2\pi$.
  • Useful factoring: $1\pm e^{i\theta}=e^{i\theta/2}(\dots)$, which gives $2\cos\frac\theta2$ or $-2i\sin\frac\theta2$.
