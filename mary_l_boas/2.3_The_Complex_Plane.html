2.3 The Complex Plane

Boas, Chapter 2, Section 3 · the Argand diagram, rectangular and polar forms, finding the angle in the right quadrant, radians vs degrees, and geometry with complex numbers.

Goals
  • See every complex number as a point (and an arrow) in a plane.
  • Convert between $x+iy$ and $r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)=re^{i\theta}$ quickly and without quadrant errors.
  • Know exactly when degrees are allowed and when you must use radians.
  • Use complex numbers for plane geometry: distance, midpoint, area, rotation, collinearity, perpendicularity.

1. The complex plane (Argand diagram)

A complex number $x+iy$ is a pair of real numbers $(x,y)$ (Section 2.2). Plot it as the point $(x,y)$:

  • horizontal axis = real axis (holds $x=\operatorname{Re}z$),
  • vertical axis = imaginary axis (holds $y=\operatorname{Im}z$). We plot $y$, not $iy$.

The point $(5,3)$ may be labelled $(5,3)$ or $5+3i$. This plane is called the complex plane or Argand diagram. Real numbers sit on the horizontal axis and pure imaginary numbers on the vertical axis.

Picture it as an arrow too

Often it helps to think of $z$ as the arrow (vector) from the origin to the point $(x,y)$. Then adding complex numbers is exactly adding vectors (Section 6 below).

H.K. Dass §20.4, 20.7, 20.8, 20.10, 20.14Argand diagram: all four operations by geometry

Argand diagram (§20.4). The number $x+iy$ is the point $P(x,y)$. The $x$-axis is the real axis and the $y$-axis the imaginary axis. The length $OP=r$ is the modulus, and the angle that $OP$ makes with the positive $x$-axis is the argument (amplitude). H.K. Dass then shows that each of $+,-,\times,\div$ has a ruler-and-compass construction.

Addition (§20.7): the parallelogram law

Let $P$ be $z_1=x_1+iy_1$ and $Q$ be $z_2=x_2+iy_2$. Complete the parallelogram $OPRQ$ and drop perpendiculars $PK$, $RM$, $QL$ to the $x$-axis, with $PN\perp RM$.

  • $OM=OK+KM=OK+OL=x_1+x_2$, since $KM=OL$ (congruent triangles $OQL$ and $PRN$).
  • $RM=MN+NR=KP+LQ=y_1+y_2$.

So $R$ is the point $(x_1+x_2)+i(y_1+y_2)=z_1+z_2$: the sum is the far corner of the parallelogram, with $|z_1+z_2|=OR$ and $\arg(z_1+z_2)=\angle ROM$.

Subtraction (§20.8)

Write $z_1-z_2=z_1+(-z_2)$. The point $-z_2$ is $Q'$, found by extending $QO$ beyond $O$ to $OQ'=OQ$. Complete the parallelogram $OPRQ'$: then $OR$ represents $z_1-z_2$. It has the same length and direction as the arrow $QP$ from $z_2$ to $z_1$. So $|z_1-z_2|$ is the distance between the two points.

Multiplication (§20.10): the similar-triangle construction

With $z_1=r_1(\cos\theta_1+i\sin\theta_1)$ at $P$ and $z_2=r_2(\cos\theta_2+i\sin\theta_2)$ at $Q$, mark $A$ at $1$ on the real axis. On $OQ$, construct the triangle $OQR$ similar to $OAP$, turned the same way. Then

$$\frac{OR}{OQ}=\frac{OP}{OA}=\frac{r_1}{1}\Rightarrow OR=r_1r_2,\qquad\angle XOR=\angle XOQ+\angle QOR=\theta_2+\theta_1 .$$

So $R$ is $z_1z_2$: $|z_1z_2|=|z_1||z_2|$ and $\arg(z_1z_2)=\arg z_1+\arg z_2$. Algebraically this is the polar product

$$z_1z_2=r_1r_2\left[\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)+i\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\right],$$

which uses the addition formulas $\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2-\sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2=\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)$ and $\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2+\cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2=\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)$.

Division (§20.14)

Mark $A$ at $1$ again. On $OA$, construct the triangle $OAR$ similar to $OQP$. Then $\frac{OR}{OA}=\frac{OP}{OQ}$, so $OR=\frac{r_1}{r_2}$, and $\angle AOR=\angle QOP=\theta_1-\theta_2$. Hence $R$ is $\frac{z_1}{z_2}$:

$$\left|\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right|=\frac{|z_1|}{|z_2|},\qquad\arg\frac{z_1}{z_2}=\arg z_1-\arg z_2 .$$

Worked example — the fourth vertex of a parallelogram

$ABCD$ is a parallelogram with $A=2+i$, $B=5+2i$, $C=6+5i$. Find $D$.

  1. In a parallelogram the diagonals bisect each other, so $A+C=B+D$ (both equal twice the centre).
  2. $D=A+C-B=(2+6-5)+i(1+5-2)=3+4i$.
  3. Check with the parallelogram law: $\vec{AD}=D-A=1+3i$ and $\vec{BC}=C-B=1+3i$. They are equal. ✓

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: vector-addition geometry in the Argand plane turns coordinate-geometry questions into one-line additions.

Worked example — the triangle $z$, $iz$, $z+iz$
  1. $iz$ is $z$ turned by $90^\circ$ (§20.11), so $O$, $z$, $z+iz$, $iz$ is a square of side $|z|$ (the parallelogram on two perpendicular sides of equal length).
  2. The triangle $z$, $iz$, $z+iz$ is half of that square, so its area is $\frac12|z|^2$.
  3. For $z=3+4i$ the area is $\frac{25}{2}$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: combining the parallelogram law with the $i$-rotation rule gives areas without any coordinates.

2. Rectangular form and polar form

The point $P=(x,y)$ can also be located by its distance $r$ from the origin and the angle $\theta$ that $OP$ makes with the positive real axis (Boas Fig. 3.2):

$$x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta \qquad\qquad(3.1)$$ $$x+iy=r\cos\theta+ir\sin\theta=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\qquad(3.2)$$

and, using Euler's formula (proved in Section 2.9),

$$\boxed{\,z=x+iy=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)=re^{i\theta}\,}\qquad(3.3)$$

Converting

Polar → rectangular $$x=r\cos\theta,\quad y=r\sin\theta$$

Always straightforward: just evaluate the cosine and sine.

Rectangular → polar $$r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\ (\ge0)$$

Find $\theta$ from a sketch, in the quadrant where the point lies. $\tan\theta=y/x$ only narrows it down to two choices.

$r$ is always taken positive (Boas). A "negative $r$" would be the same as adding $\pi$ to $\theta$.

H.K. Dass §20.16–20.17Polar form and the three types of a complex number

Procedure for converting $x+iy$ to polar form (§20.16). Put

$$x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta .$$

Squaring and adding gives $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. Then pick the angle $\theta$ that satisfies both equations $\cos\theta=x/r$ and $\sin\theta=y/r$. Using $\tan\theta=y/x$ alone can put you in the wrong quadrant.

Worked example (Dass §20.17) — polar form of $(1-\sqrt2)+i$
  1. $r^2=(1-\sqrt2)^2+1=4-2\sqrt2$, so $r=\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2}\approx1.082$.
  2. $\cos\theta=\frac{1-\sqrt2}{r}\lt0$ and $\sin\theta=\frac1r\gt0$, so $\theta$ is in quadrant II.
  3. The reference angle is $\alpha=\tan^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt2-1}=\tan^{-1}(\sqrt2+1)=\frac{3\pi}8$, so $\theta=\pi-\frac{3\pi}8=\frac{5\pi}8$.
  4. Hence $(1-\sqrt2)+i=\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2}\left(\cos\frac{5\pi}8+i\sin\frac{5\pi}8\right)=\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2}\,e^{5\pi i/8}$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: recognising $\tan\frac{3\pi}8=\sqrt2+1$ (and $\tan\frac\pi8=\sqrt2-1$) turns an ugly-looking number into an exact angle.

Worked example (Dass §20.17) — smallest positive $n$ with $\left(\frac{1+i}{1-i}\right)^n=1$
  1. $\frac{1+i}{1-i}=\frac{(1+i)^2}{2}=\frac{2i}{2}=i$. In exponential form this is $e^{i\pi/2}$.
  2. $i^n=e^{in\pi/2}=1$ needs $\frac{n\pi}2$ to be a multiple of $2\pi$, so $n$ must be a multiple of 4. The smallest is $n=4$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: converting the base to $e^{i\theta}$ first is the standard way to handle any "find $n$ such that a power equals 1" question.

3. Getting the angle right (the arctan trap)

Your calculator's $\arctan$ only returns angles between $-\pi/2$ and $\pi/2$ (quadrants I and IV). But $(-1,-1)$ and $(1,1)$ have the same ratio $y/x=1$. So:

Point lies inSigns$\theta$ (with $\alpha=\arctan\lvert y/x\rvert$, the reference angle)
Quadrant I$x>0,y>0$$\theta=\alpha$
Quadrant II$x\lt0,y>0$$\theta=\pi-\alpha$
Quadrant III$x\lt0,y\lt0$$\theta=\pi+\alpha$ (or $-\pi+\alpha$)

Computer languages have a function $\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)$ that does this table automatically and returns $\theta\in(-\pi,\pi]$.

The angle is never unique: $\theta$, $\theta\pm2\pi$, $\theta\pm4\pi,\dots$ all describe the same point. Section 2.4 discusses the principal value.

4. Radians and degrees — when may you use degrees?

Boas explains this carefully. The rule:

Rule

You may use degrees to measure angles and to add and subtract angles, as long as the last step is taking sin, cos or tan of the result (with the calculator in degree mode).

In every formula where an angle stands alone as a number (calculus, series, $\theta=\arctan$ used as a number, exponents like $e^{i\theta}$ inside other algebra), you must use radians.

Why radians? Because of calculus

$\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin x=\cos x$ is true only when $x$ is in radians. The key limit is $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1$, which holds because the arc length on a unit circle equals the angle in radians. If $x$ is measured in degrees, then $\sin(x^\circ)=\sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{180}\right)$ and

$$\frac{d}{dx}\sin(x^\circ)=\frac{\pi}{180}\cos(x^\circ)\approx0.01745\cos(x^\circ).$$

Hence the small-angle approximation $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ works only in radians: $\sin(0.01)=0.0099998$, but $\sin(0.01^\circ)=0.000175\ne0.01$.

Boas's warning example

$\displaystyle\int_0^1\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=\arctan1=\frac\pi4=0.785.$ Here $\arctan1$ is not an angle; it is the numerical value of an integral. Answering "45" from degree mode is wrong.

Similarly, in Section 2.14 the expression $i^{-2i}=e^{\pi}$ uses $\pi$ as a number, so the angle of $i$ must be in radians.

Also, an angle is in radians unless the degree symbol is written: in $\sin2$, the 2 means 2 radians ($\approx114.6^\circ$).

6. Worked examples

Example 1 (Boas Fig. 3.3) — $1+i\sqrt3$ in all forms
  1. $x=1$, $y=\sqrt3$, so $r=\sqrt{1+3}=2$.
  2. The point is in quadrant I and $\tan\theta=\sqrt3$, so $\theta=\pi/3$ $(60^\circ)$.
  3. Labels: $(1,\sqrt3)$, $1+i\sqrt3$, $(r,\theta)=(2,\pi/3)$, $2\left(\cos\frac\pi3+i\sin\frac\pi3\right)$, $2e^{i\pi/3}$.
Example 2 — Quadrant II: $-\sqrt3+i$
  1. $r=\sqrt{3+1}=2$, and the reference angle is $\alpha=\arctan\frac1{\sqrt3}=\frac\pi6$.
  2. The point is in quadrant II, so $\theta=\pi-\frac\pi6=\frac{5\pi}6$ $(150^\circ)$.
  3. The naive calculator answer $\arctan\frac{1}{-\sqrt3}=-\frac\pi6$ points to quadrant IV, which is wrong.
  4. $-\sqrt3+i=2e^{5\pi i/6}$.
Example 3 — Non-special angle: $3-4i$
  1. $r=\sqrt{9+16}=5$.
  2. Quadrant IV, with reference angle $\arctan\frac43=0.9273$ rad $(53.13^\circ)$.
  3. $\theta=-0.9273$ rad (or $2\pi-0.9273=5.3559$ rad, i.e. $306.87^\circ$).
  4. $3-4i=5e^{-0.9273i}$.
Example 4 — Polar to rectangular: $4e^{5\pi i/6}$ and $3e^{2i}$
  1. $4\left(\cos\frac{5\pi}6+i\sin\frac{5\pi}6\right)=4\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}2+\frac i2\right)=-2\sqrt3+2i$.
  2. $3e^{2i}$: here the angle is 2 radians (about $114.6^\circ$, in quadrant II). $3\cos2+3i\sin2=3(-0.4161)+3i(0.9093)=-1.248+2.728i$.
Example 5 — Degrees allowed: $2(\cos20^\circ+i\sin20^\circ)$
  1. The last step is taking cos and sin, so degree mode is fine.
  2. $2(0.9397)+2i(0.3420)=1.879+0.684i$.
  3. The same in radians: $20^\circ=\pi/9$, and $2e^{i\pi/9}$ gives the same value.
Example 6 — Geometry: is the triangle $z_1=1+i$, $z_2=4+2i$, $z_3=2+4i$ right-angled at $z_1$?
  1. $z_2-z_1=3+i$ and $z_3-z_1=1+3i$.
  2. $\dfrac{z_3-z_1}{z_2-z_1}=\dfrac{1+3i}{3+i}=\dfrac{(1+3i)(3-i)}{10}=\dfrac{3-i+9i+3}{10}=\dfrac{6+8i}{10}$, which is not purely imaginary.
  3. So there is no right angle at $z_1$. The angle there is $\arg(0.6+0.8i)=53.13^\circ$.
  4. Area $=\tfrac12|\operatorname{Im}(\overline{(3+i)}(1+3i))|=\tfrac12|\operatorname{Im}((3-i)(1+3i))|=\tfrac12|\operatorname{Im}(6+8i)|=4$.

7. Practice problems

P1. Write in polar form ($r$ and $\theta$): (a) $-4$ (b) $3i$ (c) $-2+2i$ (d) $1-i\sqrt3$ (e) $-5-12i$. Easy
Show solution

(a) $4e^{i\pi}$. (b) $3e^{i\pi/2}$. (c) $r=2\sqrt2$, quadrant II, $\theta=3\pi/4$: $2\sqrt2e^{3\pi i/4}$. (d) $r=2$, quadrant IV, $\theta=-\pi/3$: $2e^{-i\pi/3}$.

(e) $r=13$, quadrant III, reference angle $\arctan\frac{12}5=1.1760$, so $\theta=\pi+1.1760=4.3176$ (or $-1.9656$): $13e^{-1.9656i}$.

P2. Write in $x+iy$ form: (a) $6e^{i\pi/4}$ (b) $2e^{-2\pi i/3}$ (c) $10(\cos210^\circ+i\sin210^\circ)$ (d) $3e^{i\pi}$ (e) $e^{i}$. Easy
Show solution

(a) $3\sqrt2+3\sqrt2\,i$. (b) $2(-\frac12-\frac{\sqrt3}2i)=-1-i\sqrt3$. (c) $10(-\frac{\sqrt3}2-\frac12i)=-5\sqrt3-5i$. (d) $-3$. (e) $\cos1+i\sin1=0.5403+0.8415i$ (1 radian!).

P3. Find the distance between $2+3i$ and $-1-i$, and their midpoint. Easy
Show solution

$|(2+3i)-(-1-i)|=|3+4i|=5$. Midpoint $=\frac{(2+3i)+(-1-i)}2=\frac12+i$.

P4. Find the area of the triangle with vertices $0$, $3+i$, $1+2i$. Medium
Show solution

$\bar z_1z_2=(3-i)(1+2i)=3+6i-i+2=5+5i$. Area $=\frac12|\operatorname{Im}|=\frac52$.

P5. $A=1+i$, $B=4+2i$, $C=5+5i$ are three vertices of a parallelogram $ABCD$. Find $D$. Medium
Show solution

The diagonals bisect each other: $\frac{A+C}2=\frac{B+D}2$, so $D=A+C-B=2+4i$.

P6. Rotate $3+i$ by $90^\circ$ counter-clockwise (a) about the origin, (b) about $1+i$. Medium
Show solution

(a) $i(3+i)=-1+3i$. (b) $c+i(z-c)=(1+i)+i(2)=1+3i$.

P7. Are $1+i$, $2+3i$, $4+7i$ collinear? Medium
Show solution

$\dfrac{(4+7i)-(1+i)}{(2+3i)-(1+i)}=\dfrac{3+6i}{1+2i}=3$, which is real. So yes, they are collinear.

P8. Find the third vertex of an equilateral triangle with two vertices at $0$ and $2$. Medium
Show solution

Rotate $2$ by $\pm60^\circ$ about $0$: $2e^{\pm i\pi/3}=1\pm i\sqrt3$.

P9. Two adjacent vertices of a square are $z_1=1$, $z_2=3+i$. Find the other two (both possibilities). Hard
Show solution

Side $v=z_2-z_1=2+i$. Turning it by $+90^\circ$ gives $iv=-1+2i$, so the vertices are $z_2+iv=2+3i$ and $z_1+iv=2i$. Turning by $-90^\circ$ gives $-iv=1-2i$, so the vertices are $4-i$ and $2-2i$.

P10. Show that the vectors $2+i$ and $-1+2i$ are perpendicular. Easy
Show solution

$\operatorname{Re}(\overline{(2+i)}(-1+2i))=\operatorname{Re}((2-i)(-1+2i))=\operatorname{Re}(-2+4i+i+2)=\operatorname{Re}(5i)=0$. Equivalently, $\frac{-1+2i}{2+i}=i$, which is purely imaginary. ✔

P11. If $z$ has $r=2$ and $\theta=3\pi/4$, give $z$, $\bar z$, $-z$, $iz$, $1/z$ in polar form. Medium
Show solution

$z=2e^{3\pi i/4}=-\sqrt2+\sqrt2i$. Then $\bar z=2e^{-3\pi i/4}$, $-z=2e^{i(3\pi/4+\pi)}=2e^{-i\pi/4}$, $iz=2e^{5\pi i/4}$, and $1/z=\frac12e^{-3\pi i/4}$.

P12. A student says $\int_0^{1}\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=45$. What went wrong? Easy
Show solution

They read $\arctan1$ in degree mode. The integral is a pure number, $\pi/4\approx0.785$. Never use degree mode when an inverse trig value is used as a number.

P13. The point $z$ moves so that $\theta$ increases from $0$ to $2\pi$ with $r=3$ fixed. Describe the path of $z$, of $\bar z$, and of $iz$. Medium
Show solution

$z$ goes once around the circle of radius 3 counter-clockwise, starting at $3$. $\bar z$ goes around the same circle clockwise. $iz$ goes around counter-clockwise but starts at $3i$ (a quarter turn ahead).

P14. Find the point dividing the segment from $z_1=-2+i$ to $z_2=4+7i$ in the ratio $1:2$. Medium
Show solution

$\dfrac{2z_1+1\cdot z_2}{3}=\dfrac{-4+2i+4+7i}{3}=3i$.

P15. Show that the points $z,\ iz,\ -z,\ -iz$ ($z\neq0$) are the vertices of a square, and find its area. Medium
Show solution

Each point is the previous one rotated by $90^\circ$ about $0$, and all are at distance $|z|$ from $0$. So they form a square inscribed in a circle of radius $|z|$. The diagonal is $2|z|$, so the area is $\frac{(2|z|)^2}{2}=2|z|^2$.

P16. If the angle of $z$ is $\alpha$, what are the angles of $\bar z,\ -z,\ z^2,\ 1/z,\ iz$? Medium
Show solution

$-\alpha$, $\ \alpha+\pi$, $\ 2\alpha$, $\ -\alpha$, $\ \alpha+\pi/2$ (each up to multiples of $2\pi$).

P17. (Challenge) Prove that a triangle $z_1z_2z_3$ is equilateral iff $z_1^2+z_2^2+z_3^2=z_1z_2+z_2z_3+z_3z_1$. Challenge
Show solution

Let $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$, so $1+\omega+\omega^2=0$. A triangle is equilateral iff $z_1+\omega z_2+\omega^2z_3=0$ or $z_1+\omega^2z_2+\omega z_3=0$ (one orientation each). The product of the two left-hand sides is

$$(z_1+\omega z_2+\omega^2z_3)(z_1+\omega^2z_2+\omega z_3)=z_1^2+z_2^2+z_3^2+(\omega+\omega^2)(z_1z_2+z_2z_3+z_3z_1)$$

which equals $z_1^2+z_2^2+z_3^2-(z_1z_2+z_2z_3+z_3z_1)$. It is zero iff one factor is zero, iff the triangle is equilateral.

9. Going deeper: one ratio that encodes all plane geometry

For three points $z_1,z_2,z_3$, look at the ratio

$$\frac{z_3-z_1}{z_2-z_1}=\frac{|z_3-z_1|}{|z_2-z_1|}\,e^{i\alpha},$$

where $\alpha$ is the angle at $z_1$ measured from the segment $z_1z_2$ to the segment $z_1z_3$. Why: division divides the lengths and subtracts the angles (Section 2.9), and $z_k-z_1$ is the arrow from $z_1$ to $z_k$. Almost every geometry question reduces to this one ratio:

Why multiplying by $e^{i\alpha}$ is a rotation: the matrix view

Multiplying $x+iy$ by $\cos\alpha+i\sin\alpha$ gives $(x\cos\alpha-y\sin\alpha)+i(x\sin\alpha+y\cos\alpha)$. As a map on $(x,y)$ that is

$$\begin{pmatrix}x'\\y'\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha\\\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix},$$

the standard rotation matrix. More generally, multiplying by $re^{i\alpha}$ is a rotation followed by a scaling, and these are the only linear maps of the plane that preserve angles. This is why complex numbers represent 2-D rotations and phasors so efficiently.

The angle as a single formula

For $z=x+iy\ne0$, the principal angle in $(-\pi,\pi]$ is the function programmers call $\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)$:

$$\operatorname{Arg}z=\begin{cases}\arctan(y/x)&x\gt0\\\arctan(y/x)+\pi&x\lt0,\ y\ge0\\\arctan(y/x)-\pi&x\lt0,\ y\lt0\\\pm\pi/2&x=0,\ y\gtrless0\end{cases}$$

Traps that cost marks
  • $\arctan(y/x)$ cannot tell $(1,1)$ from $(-1,-1)$. Always check the quadrant.
  • Know which principal-value convention the question uses, $(-\pi,\pi]$ or $[0,2\pi)$. MCQ distractors are often the other convention's value.
  • $\arg(z-a)=\alpha$ is a ray. $\arg(z-a)=\alpha$ or $\alpha+\pi$ together give the line.
  • Degrees are fine for $\cos$ and $\sin$ of an angle, but not inside $e^{i\theta}$ when you differentiate or integrate.
30-second shortcuts
  • Pythagorean triples hide in exam numbers: $3+4i\to5$, $5+12i\to13$, $8+15i\to17$, $7+24i\to25$.
  • Multiplying by $i$ rotates by $+90^\circ$, so $iz\perp z$. Multiplying by $-1$ reflects through the origin.

13. Summary

  • $z=x+iy$ ↔ point $(x,y)$ ↔ arrow from $0$. Real axis horizontal, imaginary axis vertical.
  • $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$, $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\ge0$, and $z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)=re^{i\theta}$.
  • Find $\theta$ from a sketch (quadrant!), not from $\arctan(y/x)$ alone.
  • Degrees are allowed only when the last step is sin, cos or tan. In formulas, use radians.
  • Geometry: distance $|z_1-z_2|$, rotation $e^{i\alpha}$, collinear iff the quotient is real, perpendicular iff the quotient is imaginary.
