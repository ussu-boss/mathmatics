2. Type I — $\int_0^{2\pi}R(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\,d\theta$

Put $z=e^{i\theta}$. As $\theta$ goes from $0$ to $2\pi$, $z$ goes once counter-clockwise around $|z|=1$ (Boas Fig. 7.1):

$$d\theta=\frac{dz}{iz},\qquad\cos\theta=\frac12\left(z+\frac1z\right),\qquad\sin\theta=\frac1{2i}\left(z-\frac1z\right).$$
Boas Example 1 — $I=\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{5+4\cos\theta}$
  1. $I=\oint\frac{dz/(iz)}{5+2(z+1/z)}=\frac1i\oint\frac{dz}{2z^2+5z+2}=\frac1i\oint\frac{dz}{(2z+1)(z+2)}$.
  2. Poles at $-\frac12$ (inside) and $-2$ (outside).
  3. $R\left(-\frac12\right)=\lim\left(z+\frac12\right)\frac{1}{(2z+1)(z+2)}=\frac{1}{2(z+2)}\Big|_{-1/2}=\frac13$.
  4. $I=\frac1i\cdot2\pi i\cdot\frac13=\frac{2\pi}3$.

This works for any rational function of $\sin\theta,\cos\theta$ whose denominator is never zero. For an even integrand, $\int_0^\pi=\frac12\int_0^{2\pi}$.

Useful general results (derived the same way) $$\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{a+b\cos\theta}=\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{a+b\sin\theta}=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{a^2-b^2}},\qquad\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\sin^2\theta\,d\theta}{a+b\cos\theta}=\frac{2\pi}{b^2}\left(a-\sqrt{a^2-b^2}\right)\qquad(a>|b|)$$

H.K. Dass §26.13–26.14Integrals round the unit circle: Dass's fourteen examples, sorted by trick

Substitution. $z=e^{i\theta}$, so $d\theta=\frac{dz}{iz}$, $\cos\theta=\frac12\left(z+\frac1z\right)$, $\sin\theta=\frac1{2i}\left(z-\frac1z\right)$, and $\cos n\theta=\operatorname{Re}z^n$. The integral over $[0,2\pi]$ becomes $\oint_{|z|=1}$, and only poles inside the unit circle count.

Trick 1 — denominators $a+b\cos\theta$ or $a+b\sin\theta$, with $a\gt|b|$ (Ex 38, 39, 40, 41, 44). The denominator becomes a quadratic $bz^2+2az+b$ (for $\cos$) or $bz^2+2iaz-b$ (for $\sin$). Its two roots have product of modulus $1$, so exactly one lies inside the unit circle. The result is always

$$\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{a+b\cos\theta}=\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{a+b\sin\theta}=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{a^2-b^2}} .$$

Ex 38 ($5-3\cos\theta$): $\frac{2\pi}{4}=\frac\pi2$. Ex 39 ($5-4\sin\theta$): $\frac{2\pi}3$. Ex 41 ($2+\cos\theta$): $\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt3}$. Ex 44: $1-2p\sin\theta+p^2$ is the case $a=1+p^2$, $b=-2p$, giving $\frac{2\pi}{1-p^2}$ for $p^2\lt1$.

Trick 2 — a numerator $\cos n\theta$: use $e^{in\theta}=z^n$ and take the real part (Ex 45, 47, 48). With $r=\frac{a-\sqrt{a^2-b^2}}{b}$ (the root inside the circle):

$$\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\cos n\theta\,d\theta}{a-b\cos\theta}=\frac{2\pi\,r^n}{\sqrt{a^2-b^2}},\qquad\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\cos n\theta\,d\theta}{1-2a\cos\theta+a^2}=\frac{2\pi a^n}{1-a^2}\ (|a|\lt1).$$
  • Ex 45 ($n=2$): $\frac{2\pi a^2}{1-a^2}$.
  • Ex 48 ($\frac{\cos3\theta}{5-4\cos\theta}$, $r=\frac12$): $\frac{2\pi}{3}\cdot\frac18=\frac{\pi}{12}$. With $5+4\cos\theta$ instead (Dass's "Question"), $r=-\frac12$, giving $-\frac{\pi}{12}$.
  • Ex 47 ($\frac{\cos2\theta}{5+4\cos\theta}$): $\frac{2\pi}{3}\cdot\frac14=\frac\pi6$.

Why not $\cos n\theta=\frac{z^n+z^{-n}}{2}$? That works too, but the $z^{-n}$ term creates a pole of order $n$ at $0$. The real-part trick avoids it.

Trick 3 — simplify first (Ex 42, 43, 46).

  • Ex 42: $\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\sin^2\theta}{5-4\cos\theta}d\theta$. Write $\sin^2\theta=\frac{1-\cos2\theta}{2}$ and use trick 2: $\frac12\left[\frac{2\pi}{3}-\frac{2\pi}{3}\cdot\frac14\right]=\frac\pi4$.
  • Ex 43: $\int_0^\pi\frac{1+2\cos\theta}{5+4\cos\theta}d\theta=0$. Since $1+2\cos\theta=\frac12(5+4\cos\theta)-\frac32$, the integral is $\frac\pi2-\frac32\cdot\frac{\pi}{3}=0$. In $z$ form the integrand is $\frac{1}{i(z+2)}$, whose only pole $-2$ is outside, so the answer is $0$.
  • Ex 46: $\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\sin^2\theta-2\cos\theta}{2+\cos\theta}d\theta$. Polynomial division gives $\frac{1-\cos^2\theta-2\cos\theta}{2+\cos\theta}=-\cos\theta+\frac{1}{2+\cos\theta}$, so the answer is $0+\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt3}$.

Trick 4 — differentiate with respect to a parameter, or rescale (Ex 49, 50, 51).

  • Ex 49: differentiating $\int\frac{d\theta}{a+b\cos\theta}=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{a^2-b^2}}$ with respect to $a$ gives $\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{(a+b\cos\theta)^2}=\frac{2\pi a}{(a^2-b^2)^{3/2}}$. Dass does it with a double pole; this route is faster.
  • Ex 50: $\sin^2\theta=\frac{1-\cos2\theta}2$, then $\phi=2\theta$ gives $\int_0^\pi\frac{a\,d\theta}{a^2+\sin^2\theta}=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{1+a^2}}$.
  • Ex 51: $e^{\cos\theta}\cos(\sin\theta-n\theta)=\operatorname{Re}\left[e^{e^{i\theta}}e^{-in\theta}\right]$, so the integral is $\operatorname{Re}\oint\frac{e^z}{z^{n+1}}\frac{dz}{i}=\frac{2\pi}{n!}$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: $\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{a+b\cos\theta}=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{a^2-b^2}}$ and its $a$-derivative answer most "evaluate using residues" MCQs on this type in under a minute.

3. Type II — $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}dx$

Boas Example 2 — $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=\pi$
  1. Close the real segment $[-\rho,\rho]$ with the upper semicircle $|z|=\rho$ (Fig. 7.2). For $\rho>1$ it encloses only $z=i$, with residue $\frac{1}{2i}$, so the contour integral is $2\pi i\cdot\frac1{2i}=\pi$ for every $\rho>1$.
  2. On the semicircle: $\left|\int\right|\le\pi\rho\cdot\frac{1}{\rho^2-1}\to0$ (ML estimate).
  3. So $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=\pi$.
Rule

If $P$ and $Q$ are polynomials, $\deg Q\ge\deg P+2$, and $Q$ has no real zeros, then

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}dx=2\pi i\sum\text{(residues of }P/Q\text{ in the upper half-plane)}.$$

H.K. Dass §26.15$\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{f_1(x)}{f_2(x)}dx$ and its Fourier cousins: Dass's examples and an important correction

Conditions (Dass). (i) $f_2$ has no real zeros. (ii) $\deg f_2\ge\deg f_1+2$. Close $[-R,R]$ with the upper semicircle $C_R$. On $C_R$, $|f|\lesssim\frac{K}{R^2}$ while its length is $\pi R$, so $\int_{C_R}\to0$. Hence

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\,dx=2\pi i\sum_{\operatorname{Im}a\gt0}\operatorname{Res}_af .$$

Fourier-type integrals: use $e^{imz}$, never $\cos mz$ or $\sin mz$

The trap (read this before Ex 52–54)

On the upper semicircle $z=Re^{i\theta}$, $|e^{imz}|=e^{-mR\sin\theta}\le1$ for $m\gt0$, so Jordan's lemma kills the arc. But $\sin mz=\frac{e^{imz}-e^{-imz}}{2i}$ contains $e^{-imz}$, which grows like $e^{mR\sin\theta}$ there. So $\oint\frac{\sin mz}{Q(z)}dz$ over the semicircle does not reduce to the real integral. The correct method is $\int\frac{\sin mx}{Q}dx=\operatorname{Im}\left[2\pi i\sum\operatorname{Res}_{\operatorname{Im}a\gt0}\frac{e^{imz}}{Q}\right]$, and similarly $\operatorname{Re}$ for $\cos$.

Worked example (Dass Ex 52, 53, 54, corrected) — $\cos$ and $\sin$ numerators

Ex 52: $\int_0^\infty\frac{\cos mx}{x^2+1}dx$ ($m\gt0$). $\operatorname{Res}_i\frac{e^{imz}}{z^2+1}=\frac{e^{-m}}{2i}$, so $\int_{-\infty}^\infty=\pi e^{-m}$ and $\int_0^\infty=\boxed{\frac\pi2e^{-m}}$.

Ex 53: $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{x\sin\pi x}{x^2+2x+5}dx$. The upper pole is $a=-1+2i$, and $\operatorname{Res}_a\frac{ze^{i\pi z}}{z^2+2z+5}=\frac{ae^{i\pi a}}{4i}=\frac{(-1+2i)(-1)e^{-2\pi}}{4i}$. Then $2\pi i\cdot\operatorname{Res}=\frac\pi2(1-2i)e^{-2\pi}$. Taking the imaginary part: $\boxed{-\pi e^{-2\pi}}\approx-0.00587$.

The book integrates $\frac{z\sin\pi z}{z^2+2z+5}$ itself around the semicircle and gets $\pi\sinh2\pi\approx841$. That is the trap above: the arc does not vanish for $\sin\pi z$. Numerical quadrature of the real integral gives $-0.0058667$, which matches $-\pi e^{-2\pi}$.

Ex 54: $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x^2+4x+5}dx$. The upper pole is $-2+i$, and $2\pi i\frac{e^{i(-2+i)}}{2i}=\pi e^{-1}(\cos2-i\sin2)$. Imaginary part: $\boxed{-\frac{\pi\sin2}{e}}\approx-1.0509$. The book's $-2\pi\sin2\cosh1\approx-8.82$ again comes from integrating $\sin z$ directly. Numerical quadrature gives $-1.05090$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: options are often built from exactly this mistake ($\sinh$ and $\cosh$ factors appear). If your answer grows with the frequency $m$, you have integrated $\sin$ instead of $e^{imz}$.

4. Type III — Fourier-type integrals ($\cos mx$, $\sin mx$)

Boas Example 3 — $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty\frac{\cos x}{1+x^2}dx=\frac{\pi}{2e}$
  1. Use $\oint\frac{e^{iz}}{1+z^2}dz$, not $\cos z$: $\cos z$ blows up like $e^{|y|}$ in the upper half-plane, but $|e^{iz}|=e^{-y}\le1$ there.
  2. The residue at $i$ is $\frac{e^{-1}}{2i}$, so the integral is $\frac\pi e$, and the semicircle part $\to0$.
  3. $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{e^{ix}}{1+x^2}dx=\frac\pi e$. The real part gives $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{\cos x}{1+x^2}=\frac\pi e$, and by evenness $\int_0^\infty=\frac{\pi}{2e}$.
Rule (with Jordan's lemma)

If $P,Q$ are polynomials, $Q$ has no real zeros, $\deg Q\ge\deg P+1$, and $m>0$:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}e^{imx}dx=2\pi i\sum\text{Res}\left(\frac{P(z)}{Q(z)}e^{imz}\right)_{\text{UHP}}.$$

Take real and imaginary parts to get the $\cos mx$ and $\sin mx$ integrals. (For $m\lt0$ close in the lower half-plane, or just use $\cos(-x)=\cos x$.) Standard results:

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{\cos mx}{x^2+a^2}dx=\frac{\pi}{a}e^{-ma},\qquad\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{x\sin mx}{x^2+a^2}dx=\pi e^{-ma},\qquad\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{\cos mx}{(x^2+a^2)^2}dx=\frac{\pi(1+ma)e^{-ma}}{2a^3}.$$

5. Type IV — poles on the real axis; principal values

Boas Example 4 — $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x}dx=\pi$
  1. Use $\oint\frac{e^{iz}}{z}dz$ around the contour of Fig. 7.3: the real axis with a small semicircle $C'$ of radius $r$ above the pole at $0$, closed by a large semicircle $C$.
  2. No pole is inside, so the total is $0$. The $C$ part $\to0$ (Jordan).
  3. On $C'$: $z=re^{i\theta}$, $\frac{dz}{z}=i\,d\theta$, and $e^{iz}\to1$, so the integral (traversed from $\theta=\pi$ to $0$) $\to\int_\pi^0i\,d\theta=-i\pi$.
  4. So $\mathrm{PV}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{e^{ix}}{x}dx=i\pi$. Real part: $\mathrm{PV}\int\frac{\cos x}{x}=0$. Imaginary part: $\int\frac{\sin x}{x}=\pi$, so $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x}dx=\frac\pi2$.
Half-residue rule (Problem 21)

For a closed contour that passes straight through simple poles on its boundary (a tangent exists at the pole):

$$\mathrm{PV}\oint=2\pi i\left(\sum\text{Res inside}+\frac12\sum\text{Res at simple poles on the boundary}\right).$$

A pole inside contributes $2\pi i\,R$, outside contributes $0$, and on a straight boundary exactly halfway, $\pi i\,R$. Warning: this does not hold for a multiple pole on the boundary.

Principal value

$\int_0^\infty\frac{\cos x}{x}dx$ diverges (the integrand $\approx\frac1x$ near $0$). The "$0$" found above is a Cauchy principal value: cut out a symmetric interval $(-r,r)$ about the singular point and let $r\to0$. Example: $\int_0^5\frac{dx}{x-3}$. Here $\int_0^{3-r}+\int_{3+r}^5=(\ln r-\ln3)+(\ln2-\ln r)=\ln\frac23$, so $\mathrm{PV}\int_0^5\frac{dx}{x-3}=\ln\frac23$. The infinite areas on the two sides cancel.

H.K. Dass §26.17Indented semicircles: poles on the real axis (Ex 65–67, Exercise 26.7)

When the integrand has a simple pole on the real axis, Dass removes it from the region with a small semicircle $\gamma_\varepsilon$ of radius $\varepsilon$ in the upper half-plane. The key lemma: going over the pole clockwise on a small semicircle gives

$$\lim_{\varepsilon\to0}\int_{\gamma_\varepsilon}f\,dz=-\pi i\operatorname{Res}_{x_0}f\qquad\text{(half of a full turn, with the sign of a clockwise turn).}$$

So the contour (big arc, real axis with the dent, small arc) gives

$$\text{PV}\int_{-\infty}^\infty f\,dx=2\pi i\sum_{\operatorname{Im}a\gt0}\operatorname{Res}+\pi i\sum_{\text{real }x_0}\operatorname{Res} .$$
Worked example (Dass Ex 65) — $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin mx}{x}dx=\frac\pi2$ ($m\gt0$)
  1. Take $f=\frac{e^{imz}}{z}$. It has no poles in the upper half-plane and a simple pole at $0$ with residue $1$.
  2. The big arc vanishes by Jordan's lemma. The small arc gives $-\pi i$. So $\text{PV}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{e^{imx}}{x}dx-\pi i=0$, i.e. $\text{PV}\int\frac{e^{imx}}{x}dx=\pi i$.
  3. Imaginary part: $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{\sin mx}{x}dx=\pi$, so $\int_0^\infty=\frac\pi2$. The real part gives $\text{PV}\int\frac{\cos mx}{x}dx=0$ (Exercise 26.7 Q1).
  4. Variations: for $m\lt0$ the answer is $-\frac\pi2$, and for $m=0$ it is $0$. In short, $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin mx}{x}dx=\frac\pi2\operatorname{sgn}m$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: this is the Dirichlet integral. It appears directly, and also inside Fourier transforms of the step and box functions.

Worked example (Dass Ex 66) — $\int_0^\infty\frac{\cos2ax-\cos2bx}{x^2}dx=\pi(b-a)$ ($a\ge b\ge0$)
  1. Take $f=\frac{e^{2iaz}-e^{2ibz}}{z^2}$. Near $0$: $\frac{(1+2iaz)-(1+2ibz)+O(z^2)}{z^2}$, so the pole is simple (the numerator vanishes at $0$), with residue $2i(a-b)$.
  2. $\text{PV}\int_{-\infty}^\infty f\,dx=\pi i\cdot2i(a-b)=-2\pi(a-b)$. The integrand's real part is even, so $\int_0^\infty=\frac12(-2\pi)(a-b)=\pi(b-a)$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: pairing two oscillating terms so that the $\frac1{z^2}$ parts cancel leaves only a simple pole. This idea also gives $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin^2x}{x^2}dx=\frac\pi2$.

7. The argument principle

Argument principle (7.8)

If $f$ is analytic on and inside a simple closed curve $C$ except for finitely many poles, and $f\ne0$ on $C$, then

$$N-P=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}dz=\frac{1}{2\pi}\big(\text{change in the angle of }f(z)\text{ around }C\big),$$

where $N$ and $P$ count the zeros and poles inside $C$, with multiplicity.

Why: $\frac{f'}{f}$ has residue $n$ at a zero of order $n$ and $-p$ at a pole of order $p$ (Problem 42). Also $\oint\frac{f'}{f}dz=[\ln f]_C=[\operatorname{Ln}R+i\Theta]_C=i\Delta\Theta$, since $\operatorname{Ln}R$ returns to its starting value but the angle $\Theta$ need not.

Picture: follow the image curve $w=f(z)$ as $z$ goes around $C$. The number of times it winds around $w=0$ is $N-P$.

Boas Example 6 — $z^3+4z+1$ has exactly one zero in the first quadrant
  1. Contour: $O\to P$ along the $x$-axis, a big quarter circle $PQ$, then back down the $y$-axis $QO$.
  2. On $OP$: $f(x)>0$, so the angle $\Theta=0$ throughout.
  3. On $PQ$: $f\approx z^3=\rho^3e^{3i\theta}$, so $\Theta$ goes from $0$ to $\frac{3\pi}2$.
  4. On $QO$: $f=1+i(4y-y^3)$ and $\tan\Theta=4y-y^3$, negative and then positive, with no crossing of $\pm\infty$. So $\Theta$ goes from $\frac{3\pi}2$ to $2\pi$, wiggles, and returns to $2\pi$.
  5. Total change $2\pi$, so $N=1$. With conjugate pairs, one zero is in Q4 and the third is on the negative real axis.

8. Bromwich integral — inverse Laplace transforms

With complex $p=x+iy$, the Laplace transform $F(p)=\int_0^\infty e^{-pt}f(t)\,dt$ is a Fourier transform of $e^{-xt}f(t)$ (7.11–7.12). Inverting it gives the Bromwich integral (7.15):

$$f(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}F(z)e^{zt}\,dz,\qquad t>0,$$

along a vertical line $x=c$ to the right of all singularities of $F$. Closing it with a large semicircle to the left (the integral there $\to0$ when $F=P/Q$ with $\deg Q>\deg P$):

$$\boxed{f(t)=\sum\text{residues of }F(z)e^{zt}\text{ at all its poles}}\qquad(7.16)$$
Boas Example 7 — $F(p)=\dfrac{5}{(p+2)(p^2+1)}$

Residues of $\frac{5e^{zt}}{(z+2)(z+i)(z-i)}$: at $-2$, $e^{-2t}$; at $i$, $\frac{5e^{it}}{(2+i)(2i)}$; at $-i$, $\frac{5e^{-it}}{(2-i)(-2i)}$. Adding: $f(t)=e^{-2t}+2\sin t-\cos t$.

9. Dispersion relations (Kramers–Kronig)

Let $f(z)$ be analytic for $y\ge0$ and vanish fast enough at $\infty$. Integrating $\frac{f(z)}{z-a}$ ($a$ real) around the upper half-plane, with the half-residue rule at $z=a$:

$$\mathrm{PV}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{f(x)}{x-a}dx=i\pi f(a).\qquad(7.17)$$

With $f=u+iv$, the real and imaginary parts give (7.18):

$$\mathrm{PV}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{u(x)}{x-a}dx=-\pi v(a),\qquad\mathrm{PV}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{v(x)}{x-a}dx=\pi u(a).$$

$u$ and $v$ are Hilbert transforms of each other. In optics, $u$ and $v$ are the refractive and absorptive parts of the complex index of refraction. Causality (analyticity in the upper half-plane) forces each to determine the other.

12. Going deeper: the three lemmas behind every contour, and the rectangle trick

1. The big-arc estimate (for Type II)

If $|f(z)|\le\frac{M}{R^{k}}$ on the arc $|z|=R$ with $k\gt1$, then $\left|\int_{\text{arc}}f\,dz\right|\le\pi R\cdot\frac{M}{R^k}\to0$. For $\frac{P}{Q}$ this needs $\deg Q\ge\deg P+2$.

2. Jordan's lemma (for Type III)

For $m\gt0$ and $|g|\le M_R$ on the upper semicircle $C_R$:

$$\left|\int_{C_R}e^{imz}g(z)\,dz\right|\le\frac{\pi}{m}M_R .$$

Why: $|e^{imz}|=e^{-mR\sin\theta}$, and $\sin\theta\ge\frac{2\theta}\pi$ on $[0,\frac\pi2]$ (Jordan's inequality). This gives $\int_0^\pi e^{-mR\sin\theta}R\,d\theta\le\frac\pi m$. So it is enough that $g\to0$, i.e. $\deg Q\ge\deg P+1$. That is one degree less than the crude estimate needs, and it is why $\int\frac{x\sin x}{x^2+1}dx$ works.

Which half-plane? $e^{imz}$ decays in the upper half-plane when $m\gt0$, and in the lower half-plane when $m\lt0$. Closing in the lower half-plane traverses the contour clockwise, so a minus sign appears.

3. The small-arc lemma (for Type IV)

If $f$ has a simple pole at $a$, a small arc of angle $\alpha$ around $a$, traversed counter-clockwise, contributes

$$\lim_{\epsilon\to0}\int_{\text{arc}}f\,dz=i\alpha\operatorname{Res}_af .$$

A clockwise semicircle indentation gives $-i\pi\operatorname{Res}$. Hence the principal-value rule:

$$\mathrm{PV}\!\int_{-\infty}^\infty f\,dx=2\pi i\sum_{\text{upper}}\operatorname{Res}+\pi i\sum_{\text{real axis}}\operatorname{Res}.$$

The rectangle trick for $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{e^{ax}}{1+e^x}dx$, $0\lt a\lt1$

  1. The integrand $f(z)=\frac{e^{az}}{1+e^z}$ has period-like behaviour: $f(z+2\pi i)=e^{2\pi ia}f(z)$.
  2. Integrate around the rectangle $-R\to R\to R+2\pi i\to-R+2\pi i\to-R$. The vertical sides vanish as $R\to\infty$ because $0\lt a\lt1$.
  3. The top side gives $-e^{2\pi ia}I$. The only pole inside is $z=i\pi$, with residue $\frac{e^{ia\pi}}{e^{i\pi}}=-e^{ia\pi}$.
  4. $(1-e^{2\pi ia})I=-2\pi ie^{ia\pi}$, so $I=\frac{2\pi i}{e^{ia\pi}-e^{-ia\pi}}=\dfrac{\pi}{\sin\pi a}$.

Substituting $x=\ln t$ shows this is the same as the keyhole result $\int_0^\infty\frac{t^{a-1}}{1+t}dt=\frac{\pi}{\sin\pi a}$.
