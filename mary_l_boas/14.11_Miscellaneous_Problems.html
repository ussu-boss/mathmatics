1. Formula sheet (Chapter 14 on one screen)

Analytic functions

$f=u+iv$ is analytic iff (CR) $u_x=v_y$, $u_y=-v_x$ (with continuous partials), and then $f'=u_x+iv_x$.
Polar CR: $u_r=\frac1rv_\theta$, $v_r=-\frac1ru_\theta$.
$u$, $v$ harmonic; level curves orthogonal. $f$ from $u$: $f(z)=2u\left(\frac z2,\frac z{2i}\right)+$const.
Taylor radius = distance to the nearest singularity.

Integrals

$\oint(z-a)^ndz=2\pi i\,\delta_{n,-1}$; Cauchy: $\oint f\,dz=0$.
$f^{(n)}(a)=\frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f\,dz}{(z-a)^{n+1}}$.
$\oint_Cf\,dz=2\pi i\sum\text{Res}_{\text{inside}}=-2\pi iR(\infty)$ (if all are inside).
ML: $|\int_Cf|\le ML$.

Residues

Simple pole: $\lim(z-a)f=\frac{g(a)}{h'(a)}$.
Order $\le m$: $\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\frac{d^{m-1}}{dz^{m-1}}[(z-a)^mf]_a$.
$R(\infty)=-b_1=-\lim zf$ ($f\to0$) $=\lim z^2f'$ ($f\to c$).
$\frac{f'}{f}$: $+n$ at a zero of order $n$, $-p$ at a pole of order $p$.

Real integrals

$\int_0^{2\pi}R(\cos,\sin)d\theta$: $z=e^{i\theta}$.   $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac PQ$: semicircle.
$\int\frac PQe^{imx}$: Jordan (deg diff $\ge1$).   Axis poles: $+\pi i\,R$ each.
$\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{p-1}}{1+x}=\frac{\pi}{\sin\pi p}$ (keyhole).   $N-P=\frac{\Delta\arg f}{2\pi}$.
Inverse Laplace: $f(t)=\sum\text{Res}\,F(z)e^{zt}$.

3. Boas Problems, Section 11 — solutions

All numerical values were checked: residues by contour integration, integrals with mpmath/scipy (the principal values with a dedicated Cauchy-weight routine), and roots with a polynomial solver.

Show solutions 1–4

1. $\ln\sqrt{(1+x)^2+y^2}=\ln|1+z|=\operatorname{Re}\ln(1+z)$. It is harmonic (the real part of an analytic function). $f=\ln(1+z)$.

2. $\arctan\frac{y}{x+1}=\arg(1+z)=\operatorname{Im}\ln(1+z)=\operatorname{Re}[-i\ln(1+z)]$. So $f=-i\ln(1+z)$ (+ real const).

3. Liouville. $f'(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f(w)\,dw}{(w-z)^2}$ on $w=z+Re^{i\theta}$. Then $|f'(z)|\le\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{M}{R^2}\cdot2\pi R=\frac MR\to0$ as $R\to\infty$. So $f'\equiv0$ and $f$ is constant.

4. If the polynomial $P$ ($\deg\ge1$) had no zeros, $\frac1P$ would be entire. It is also bounded, since $|P|\to\infty$ as $|z|\to\infty$ and $\frac1P$ is continuous on any disk. By Liouville $\frac1P$ is constant, a contradiction. So $P$ has a zero.

21. $\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{a+b\cos\theta}=\frac1i\oint\frac{2\,dz}{bz^2+2az+b}$. The roots are $z_\pm=\frac{-a\pm\sqrt{a^2-b^2}}{b}$; $z_+$ is inside, with residue $\frac{2}{2bz_++2a}=\frac1{\sqrt{a^2-b^2}}$. Answer $\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{a^2-b^2}}$. The $\sin$ version is the same (shift $\theta$).

22. $-\frac{\partial}{\partial a}$ of 21: $\int\frac{d\theta}{(a+b\cos\theta)^2}=\frac{2\pi a}{(a^2-b^2)^{3/2}}$.

23. $\int\frac{\cos\theta}{a+b\cos\theta}=\frac1b\int\left(1-\frac{a}{a+b\cos\theta}\right)=\frac{2\pi}{b}\left(1-\frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2-b^2}}\right)$.

28. $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sqrt x\ln x}{(1+x)^2}dx=\frac{d}{dp}\left[\frac{(1-p)\pi}{\sin\pi p}\right]_{p=3/2}=\left[-\frac{\pi}{\sin\pi p}-\frac{(1-p)\pi^2\cos\pi p}{\sin^2\pi p}\right]_{3/2}=\pi$. (Keyhole: along $DE$, $\ln z=\ln r+2\pi i$.)

29. $\oint\frac{(\ln z)^2}{1+z^2}$ on the indented upper semicircle. On the negative axis $\ln z=\ln r+i\pi$, so: $2\int_0^\infty\frac{\ln^2x}{1+x^2}+2\pi i\int_0^\infty\frac{\ln x}{1+x^2}-\pi^2\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=2\pi i\frac{(i\pi/2)^2}{2i}=-\frac{\pi^3}4$. Show solutions 35–41

35. The directional derivatives along the two orthogonal coordinate directions are $\frac1{h_1}\partial_{x_1}$ and $\frac1{h_2}\partial_{x_2}$. Applying $f'=\frac{\partial f}{\partial s_1}=\frac1i\frac{\partial f}{\partial s_2}$ (as in Problem 2.46) gives $\frac{1}{h_1}u_{x_1}=\frac1{h_2}v_{x_2}$ and $\frac1{h_1}v_{x_1}=-\frac1{h_2}u_{x_2}$.

36. Mean value: $f(a)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f\,dz}{z-a}=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(a+re^{i\theta})d\theta$. Its real part gives $u(a)=$ the average of $u$ on the circle.

37. If $u$ had an interior maximum at $a$, then the average of $u$ on any small circle about $a$ would equal $u(a)$ while every value is $\le u(a)$. That forces $u\equiv u(a)$ on the circle, hence nearby, hence everywhere (connectedness), i.e. $u$ is constant. The same holds for minima (apply it to $-u$).

38. $u_2-u_1$ is harmonic and zero on the boundary. By 37 its max and min are on the boundary, i.e. both are 0. So $u_1=u_2$.

39. $z'=-\cos z$ maps the half-strip to the upper half $z'$-plane (the base goes to $-1\le x'\le1$). Then $w=\frac{z'-1}{z'+1}$ maps it to $v\ge0$, with the base going to the negative $u$-axis. There $T=\frac{100}{\pi}\arctan\frac vu$ (Fig. 10.1 type). Composing and simplifying gives $T=\frac{100}{\pi}\arctan\frac{2\sin x\sinh y}{\sinh^2y-\sin^2x}$. With $\tan\alpha=\frac{\sin x}{\sinh y}$ this is $\tan2\alpha$, so $T=\frac{200}{\pi}\arctan\frac{\sin x}{\sinh y}$.

40. $\mathcal L\{\sin at\sinh at\}=\dfrac{2a^2p}{p^4+4a^4}$ (checked with sympy). Bromwich: the poles are $p=a(\pm1\pm i)$, each with residue $\frac{2a^2p\,e^{pt}}{4p^3}=\frac{a^2e^{pt}}{2p^2}$. Their sum is $\sin at\sinh at$ ✔.

41. Use $\oint\frac{1+e^{i\pi z}}{(z-1)^2(z+1)^2}dz$ over the upper half-plane. The poles at $\pm1$ are really simple (the numerator vanishes there), with residues $\frac{-i\pi}{4}$ each. So $\mathrm{PV}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{1+e^{i\pi x}}{(1-x^2)^2}dx=\pi i\left(-\frac{i\pi}2\right)=\frac{\pi^2}2$. Real part: $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{2\cos^2(\pi x/2)}{(1-x^2)^2}dx=\frac{\pi^2}2$, so

$$\int_0^\infty\frac{\cos^2(\alpha\pi/2)}{(1-\alpha^2)^2}d\alpha=\frac{\pi^2}{8}\approx1.2337\quad(\text{numerically confirmed}).$$

4. Exam-level problems (CSIR NET / GATE / JAM style)

E1. $\oint_{|z|=1}\dfrac{e^z}{z^n}dz$ for $n\ge1$. Easy
Show solution

$\frac{2\pi i}{(n-1)!}$.

E2. Show $\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}e^{\cos\theta}\cos(n\theta-\sin\theta)\,d\theta=\frac{2\pi}{n!}$. Hard
Show solution

$\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{e^z}{z^{n+1}}dz=\frac{2\pi i}{n!}$. With $z=e^{i\theta}$: $i\int_0^{2\pi}e^{e^{i\theta}}e^{-in\theta}d\theta$. The real part of $e^{e^{i\theta}-in\theta}$ is $e^{\cos\theta}\cos(\sin\theta-n\theta)$. So $\int_0^{2\pi}e^{\cos\theta}\cos(n\theta-\sin\theta)d\theta=\frac{2\pi}{n!}$ (numerically confirmed for $n=1,2,3$).

E3. $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{dx}{(x^2+1)^3}$ and $\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{x^3+1}$. Medium
Show solution

$\frac{3\pi}8$. For the second, use a wedge of angle $\frac{2\pi}3$ (or $u=x^3$): $\frac13\cdot\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi/3)}=\frac{2\pi}{3\sqrt3}$.

E4. $\int_0^\infty\frac{\ln x}{x^2+a^2}dx$ ($a>0$). Hard
Show solution

$\frac{\pi\ln a}{2a}$ (indented semicircle with $\ln z$; the residue at $ia$ is $\frac{\ln a+i\pi/2}{2ia}$). For $a=1$ it is $0$, as in Problem 29.

E5. Residue of $\frac{1}{z\sin z}$ at $0$; residue of $ze^{1/z}$ at $0$. Easy
Show solution

$0$ (an even function: double pole, no $\frac1z$ term). And $z\left(1+\frac1z+\frac{1}{2z^2}+\cdots\right)$ gives residue $\frac12$.

E6. How many zeros does $z^5+3z+1$ have in $|z|\lt1$? Medium
Show solution

Rouché: on $|z|=1$, $|3z|=3>|z^5+1|\le2$. So it has as many zeros as $3z$: one (numerically confirmed).

E7. Find the analytic $f$ with $\operatorname{Re}f=x^2-y^2+2x$ and $f(0)=0$. Easy
Show solution

$f=z^2+2z$.

E8. Under $w=\frac{1+z}{1-z}$, what is the image of the unit disk? Medium
Show solution

The right half-plane $\operatorname{Re}w>0$: $\operatorname{Re}w=\frac{1-|z|^2}{|1-z|^2}>0$ iff $|z|\lt1$.

E9. The radius of convergence of the Taylor series of $\frac{1}{\cos z}$ about $z=1$. Medium
Show solution

The nearest zero of $\cos z$ to $1$ is $\frac\pi2$, at distance $\frac\pi2-1\approx0.571$.

E10. $\oint_{|z|=5}\dfrac{z^9}{z^{10}-1}dz$. Easy
Show solution

$R(\infty)=-\lim zf=-1$, so the integral is $2\pi i$.

5. Going deeper: the logical chain of Chapter 14 on one screen

Every big result in this chapter follows from the one before it. If you can rebuild this chain, you can rebuild the chapter.

  1. Cauchy–Riemann ($u_x=v_y$, $u_y=-v_x$) $\Rightarrow$ $f\,dz$ is a closed form $\Rightarrow$ (Green's theorem) Cauchy's theorem: $\oint f\,dz=0$. (14.2, 14.3)
  2. Cauchy's theorem applied to $\frac{f(z)}{z-a}$ minus a tiny circle $\Rightarrow$ Cauchy's integral formula. Differentiating under the integral sign $\Rightarrow$ analytic functions have derivatives of every order. (14.3)
  3. Expanding $\frac{1}{z-a}$ geometrically inside the formula $\Rightarrow$ Taylor series converging out to the nearest singularity. The ML estimate gives Cauchy's estimates $\Rightarrow$ Liouville $\Rightarrow$ fundamental theorem of algebra. (14.3, 2.7)
  4. The same expansion on two circles $\Rightarrow$ Laurent series in annuli $\Rightarrow$ the classification of singularities. (14.4)
  5. Integrating a Laurent series term by term, only $a_{-1}$ survives $\Rightarrow$ the residue theorem. (14.5, 14.6, 14.8)
  6. Residues plus estimates on arcs $\Rightarrow$ real integrals, the argument principle and Rouché. (14.7, 14.5)
  7. $f'\ne0$ $\Rightarrow$ the map is conformal; real and imaginary parts are harmonic $\Rightarrow$ 2-D potential problems are solved by mapping. (14.9, 14.10)

The ten traps of Chapter 14
  1. CR at a point or on a line does not mean analytic.
  2. $\oint\bar z\,dz\ne0$, because $\bar z$ is not analytic.
  3. $\frac{2\pi i}{n!}$, not $2\pi i$, in the derivative formula.
  4. Count only the poles inside the contour.
  5. $g/h'$ only at simple zeros of $h$.
  6. Laurent series in an outer annulus do not classify $z_0$.
  7. $\operatorname{Res}_\infty\frac1z=-1$.
  8. Use $e^{imz}$, never $\cos mz$, on a big arc; close up for $m\gt0$ and down for $m\lt0$.
  9. A real-axis pole contributes $\pi i\operatorname{Res}$ to a principal value.
  10. Under $1/z$, circles through $0$ become lines.

T10. Using a sector contour of angle $\frac{2\pi}{n}$, show that $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^n}=\frac{\pi}{n\sin(\pi/n)}$ ($n\ge2$), and evaluate it for $n=6$. Part CHard

  1. On the ray $z=re^{2\pi i/n}$, the integrand is unchanged ($z^n=r^n$) and $dz=e^{2\pi i/n}dr$. That side contributes $-e^{2\pi i/n}I$.
  2. The arc vanishes ($\sim R^{1-n}$). The only pole inside is $a=e^{i\pi/n}$, with residue $\frac{1}{na^{n-1}}=-\frac{a}{n}$.
  3. $(1-e^{2\pi i/n})I=-\frac{2\pi i}{n}e^{i\pi/n}$, so $I=\frac{2\pi i}{n(e^{i\pi/n}-e^{-i\pi/n})}=\frac{\pi}{n\sin(\pi/n)}$.
  4. $n=6$: $\frac{\pi}{6\cdot\frac12}=\frac\pi3\approx1.047$.
