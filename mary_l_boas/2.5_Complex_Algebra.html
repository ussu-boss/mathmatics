2.5 Complex Algebra

Boas, Chapter 2, Section 5 · A. simplifying to $x+iy$ · B. conjugate of an expression · C. absolute value · D. complex equations · E. graphs (loci) · F. physical applications.

Goals
  • Simplify any algebraic complex expression to $x+iy$ or $re^{i\theta}$, picking the faster route.
  • Take conjugates and absolute values of whole expressions without simplifying them first.
  • Solve complex equations by splitting them into two real equations, including square roots of complex numbers and quadratics with complex coefficients.
  • Recognise every standard locus: circle, disk, line, bisector, Apollonius circle, ellipse, hyperbola, ray, arc, half-plane.
  • Use $dz/dt$ to get speed and acceleration of a moving particle.

A. Simplifying to $x+iy$ form

Complex numbers obey the ordinary rules of algebra plus $i^2=-1$.

Division: multiply by the conjugate of the denominator

Because $(c+id)(c-id)=c^2+d^2$ is real, multiplying the top and bottom by $c-id$ makes the denominator real. Then just divide each part.

Boas Example 1 & 2
$$(1+i)^2=1+2i+i^2=2i,\qquad \frac{2+i}{3-i}=\frac{2+i}{3-i}\cdot\frac{3+i}{3+i}=\frac{6+5i+i^2}{9-i^2}=\frac{5+5i}{10}=\frac12+\frac12 i .$$
Boas Example 3 — polar is sometimes faster

$1+i=\sqrt2e^{i\pi/4}$, so $(1+i)^2=(\sqrt2)^2e^{i\pi/2}=2i$. For high powers like $(1+i)^{20}$ the polar route is much faster.

Boas Example 4 — mixed: degrees allowed when the last step is cos/sin
$$\frac{1}{2(\cos20^\circ+i\sin20^\circ)}=\frac{1}{2e^{i\pi/9}}=0.5e^{-i\pi/9}=0.5(\cos20^\circ-i\sin20^\circ)=0.47-0.17i .$$
Which route?
  • Sums and differences: rectangular.
  • Products, quotients and powers of numbers with "nice" angles: polar.
  • A single quotient: multiply by the conjugate.
  • Mixed expressions: simplify each block in its best form, then combine.

B. Complex conjugate of a complex expression

Theorem $$\overline{z_1\pm z_2}=\bar z_1\pm\bar z_2,\qquad\overline{z_1z_2}=\bar z_1\,\bar z_2,\qquad\overline{\left(\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right)}=\frac{\bar z_1}{\bar z_2},\qquad\overline{z^n}=\bar z^{\,n}.$$

Practical rule: to conjugate an expression, change the sign of every $i$ in it.

Proofs (Boas Problem 25):

  • Sum and difference (rectangular): $\overline{(x_1+x_2)+i(y_1+y_2)}=(x_1+x_2)-i(y_1+y_2)=(x_1-iy_1)+(x_2-iy_2)$. The same works for the difference.
  • Product (polar): $\overline{r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}}=r_1r_2e^{-i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}=r_1e^{-i\theta_1}\,r_2e^{-i\theta_2}=\bar z_1\bar z_2$.
  • Quotient (polar): $\overline{\tfrac{r_1}{r_2}e^{i(\theta_1-\theta_2)}}=\tfrac{r_1}{r_2}e^{-i(\theta_1-\theta_2)}=\dfrac{r_1e^{-i\theta_1}}{r_2e^{-i\theta_2}}$.
Boas example
$$z=\frac{2-3i}{i+4}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\bar z=\frac{2+3i}{-i+4}.$$
Hidden $i$'s

If $z=f+ig$ where $f$ and $g$ are themselves complex, then $\bar z=\bar f-i\bar g$, not $f-ig$. Examples of hidden $i$'s: $e^{i\theta}$, $\sqrt{-3}=i\sqrt3$, $\ln(-1)$, a symbol such as $a$ that is complex, $\sin(2+i)$.

For example, $\overline{e^{3i}+5i}=e^{-3i}-5i$, and if $a=1+i$, then $\overline{a+2i}=\bar a-2i=1-3i$ (not $a-2i=1-i$).

Important consequence: real polynomials have conjugate roots

If $P(z)=a_nz^n+\dots+a_1z+a_0$ with all $a_k$ real, then $\overline{P(z)}=P(\bar z)$. So if $P(z_0)=0$, then $P(\bar z_0)=\overline{0}=0$. Non-real roots come in conjugate pairs. In particular, a real polynomial of odd degree always has at least one real root.

More generally, $\overline{f(z)}=f(\bar z)$ for any $f$ built from real constants and $z$, e.g. $\overline{\cos z}=\cos\bar z$ (Boas Problem 17.25).

C. Finding the absolute value $|z|$

$$|z|=r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=\sqrt{z\bar z}\qquad(5.1)$$

$z\bar z$ is always real and $\ge0$. Combined with B, the absolute value of a product or quotient is the product or quotient of the absolute values, so you never need to simplify first:

$$\left|\frac{\sqrt5+3i}{1-i}\right|=\frac{|\sqrt5+3i|}{|1-i|}=\frac{\sqrt{14}}{\sqrt2}=\sqrt7.\qquad(\text{Boas example})$$
Common mistakes
  • $|a+ib|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ only if $a,b$ are real. $|3+i\cdot(2i)|=|3-2|=1$, not $\sqrt{9+4}$.
  • $|z|^2=z\bar z$, not $z^2$.
  • For sums there is no such shortcut: $|z_1+z_2|\ne|z_1|+|z_2|$ in general.

D. Complex equations

A complex number is a pair of real numbers, and two complex numbers are equal iff their real parts are equal and their imaginary parts are equal. So every complex equation is two real equations.

Boas example — find real $x,y$ with $(x+iy)^2=2i$
  1. Expand: $x^2-y^2+2ixy=0+2i$.
  2. Real parts: $x^2-y^2=0$. Imaginary parts: $2xy=2$.
  3. The first gives $y=\pm x$. Substituting into the second: $2x^2=2$ or $-2x^2=2$.
  4. $x$ is real, so $-2x^2=2$ is impossible. Hence $x^2=1$ and $y=x$.
  5. Solutions: $x=y=1$ and $x=y=-1$. That is, $\sqrt{2i}=\pm(1+i)$.

General method: square root of any complex number

To solve $(x+iy)^2=a+ib$: equate parts, $x^2-y^2=a$ and $2xy=b$. Also $|z|^2=|w|$, so $x^2+y^2=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$. Adding and subtracting:

$$\boxed{x^2=\frac{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}+a}{2},\qquad y^2=\frac{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}-a}{2},\qquad \operatorname{sign}(xy)=\operatorname{sign}(b)}$$

Example: $\sqrt{3-4i}$. Here $|w|=5$, so $x^2=4$ and $y^2=1$. Since $b\lt0$, $x$ and $y$ have opposite signs: $\pm(2-i)$.

Quadratics with complex coefficients

Use the quadratic formula, and find $\sqrt{d}$ by the boxed formula. Example: $z^2-(5+i)z+(8+i)=0$.

$$d=(5+i)^2-4(8+i)=24+10i-32-4i=-8+6i,\qquad\sqrt d=\pm(1+3i)\ \ (x^2=1,\ y^2=9,\ xy>0)$$ $$z=\frac{5+i\pm(1+3i)}{2}=3+2i\ \ \text{or}\ \ 2-i .$$
Watch the word "real"

When the unknowns $x,y$ are real, any real-valued expression must be real. For example, in $|1-(x+iy)|=x+iy$ (Boas 5.49), the left side is real and $\ge0$, so the right side must be too: $y=0$ and $x\ge0$. Forgetting this gives false solutions.

H.K. Dass §20.18Square root of a complex number: the formula and every sign case

Dass's method. Suppose $\sqrt{a+ib}=x+iy$ with $x,y$ real. Square both sides:

$$a+ib=(x^2-y^2)+i\,2xy\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2-y^2=a,\qquad 2xy=b .$$

A third equation comes from the identity $(x^2+y^2)^2=(x^2-y^2)^2+4x^2y^2=a^2+b^2$. Since $x^2+y^2\ge0$,

$$x^2+y^2=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}.$$

Add and subtract this from $x^2-y^2=a$:

$$x^2=\frac{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}+a}{2},\qquad y^2=\frac{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}-a}{2}.$$

That fixes $|x|$ and $|y|$. The signs come from $2xy=b$, and this is where students lose marks. All the cases:

Compact form: $\sqrt{a+ib}=\pm\left[\sqrt{\dfrac{|w|+a}{2}}+i\,\operatorname{sgn}(b)\sqrt{\dfrac{|w|-a}{2}}\right]$, where $|w|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ (take $\operatorname{sgn}0=+1$).

Worked example (Dass §20.18) — $\sqrt{7-24i}$
  1. $x^2-y^2=7$ and $x^2+y^2=\sqrt{49+576}=25$, so $x^2=16$ and $y^2=9$.
  2. $b=-24\lt0$, so $x$ and $y$ have opposite signs: $\sqrt{7-24i}=\pm(4-3i)$.
  3. Check: $(4-3i)^2=16-24i-9=7-24i$. ✓

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: the integer answer comes from the Pythagorean triple $7,24,25$. Spotting such triples gives exact square roots in seconds.

Worked example (Dass §20.18) — $\sqrt{-5+12i}$ and $\sqrt{-9}$
  1. $x^2+y^2=\sqrt{25+144}=13$ and $x^2-y^2=-5$, so $x^2=4$ and $y^2=9$.
  2. $b=12\gt0$ means the same sign: $\pm(2+3i)$. Check: $4+12i-9=-5+12i$. ✓
  3. For $\sqrt{-9}$ ($b=0$, $a\lt0$): $x=0$ and $y^2=9$, so the roots are $\pm3i$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: you need these square roots inside the quadratic formula for equations with complex coefficients, a frequent multi-step Part C ingredient.

Worked example — solve $z^2-(3+i)z+(4+3i)=0$
  1. Discriminant: $(3+i)^2-4(4+3i)=8+6i-16-12i=-8-6i$.
  2. $\sqrt{-8-6i}$: $x^2+y^2=10$ and $x^2-y^2=-8$, so $x^2=1$ and $y^2=9$. Since $b\lt0$, the signs are opposite: $\pm(1-3i)$.
  3. $z=\frac{(3+i)\pm(1-3i)}{2}$, giving $z=2-i$ or $z=1+2i$.
  4. Check with Vieta: the sum is $3+i$ ✓ and the product is $(2-i)(1+2i)=4+3i$ ✓.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: the complex quadratic formula is the same as the real one, and the only new skill is this square root.

E. Graphs: equations and inequalities as regions

Since $z\leftrightarrow(x,y)$, an equation in $z$ is a curve and an inequality is a region. The key tool is

Distance (Boas Problem 65)

$|z_1-z_2|$ is the distance between the points $z_1$ and $z_2$, because $|z_1-z_2|=\sqrt{(x_1-x_2)^2+(y_1-y_2)^2}$. So $|z-a|$ is the distance from $z$ to the fixed point $a$.

Note on words: "circle" means the curve, and "disk" means the area inside it.

Derivations of the less obvious loci

Apollonius circle: $|z-1|=2|z+1|$
  1. Square: $(x-1)^2+y^2=4[(x+1)^2+y^2]$.
  2. Expand: $x^2-2x+1+y^2=4x^2+8x+4+4y^2$, so $3x^2+10x+3+3y^2=0$.
  3. Divide by 3 and complete the square: $\left(x+\tfrac53\right)^2+y^2=\tfrac{25}9-1=\tfrac{16}9$.
  4. Circle with centre $-\tfrac53$ and radius $\tfrac43$.
Boas 5.62: $|z+1|+|z-1|=8$
  1. The sum of distances to the two foci $\pm1$ is constant, so this is an ellipse with $2a=8$, $a=4$, focal distance $c=1$.
  2. $b^2=a^2-c^2=15$, so the equation is $\dfrac{x^2}{16}+\dfrac{y^2}{15}=1$.
  3. If the constant were exactly $2=|a-b|$, the "ellipse" would collapse to the segment $[-1,1]$. If it were less than 2, there would be no points at all.
Arc: $\arg\dfrac{z-1}{z+1}=\dfrac\pi2$
  1. The quotient must be purely imaginary with positive imaginary part.
  2. From Section 2.2: $\dfrac{z-1}{z+1}=\dfrac{(x^2+y^2-1)+2iy}{(x+1)^2+y^2}$.
  3. Real part zero gives $x^2+y^2=1$. Imaginary part positive gives $y>0$.
  4. The locus is the upper half of the unit circle: from it, the segment $[-1,1]$ is seen at a right angle (Thales' theorem).
Boas 5.63, 5.64: $z^2=\bar z^2$ and $z^2=-\bar z^2$

$z^2-\bar z^2=2i\operatorname{Im}(z^2)=4ixy$, so $z^2=\bar z^2\iff xy=0$: both axes.

$z^2+\bar z^2=2\operatorname{Re}(z^2)=2(x^2-y^2)$, so $z^2=-\bar z^2\iff y=\pm x$: the two diagonal lines.

F. Physical applications: velocity and acceleration

If a particle's position is $z(t)=x(t)+iy(t)$, define the complex velocity and acceleration:

$$\frac{dz}{dt}=\frac{dx}{dt}+i\frac{dy}{dt},\qquad\frac{d^2z}{dt^2}=\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}+i\frac{d^2y}{dt^2}.$$

Speed $v=\left|\dfrac{dz}{dt}\right|$ and magnitude of acceleration $a=\left|\dfrac{d^2z}{dt^2}\right|$. You can differentiate with respect to $t$ treating $i$ as a constant, and you never need to separate $x$ and $y$.

Boas example — $z=\dfrac{i+2t}{t-i}$
  1. Quotient rule: $\dfrac{dz}{dt}=\dfrac{2(t-i)-(i+2t)}{(t-i)^2}=\dfrac{-3i}{(t-i)^2}$.
  2. $v=\dfrac{|-3i|}{|t-i|^2}=\dfrac{3}{t^2+1}$.
  3. $\dfrac{d^2z}{dt^2}=\dfrac{(-3i)(-2)}{(t-i)^3}=\dfrac{6i}{(t-i)^3}$, so $a=\dfrac{6}{(t^2+1)^{3/2}}$.
  4. Check (Boas 5.66): $z=\dfrac{(i+2t)(t+i)}{t^2+1}=\dfrac{2t^2-1}{t^2+1}+i\dfrac{3t}{t^2+1}$. Then $\dot x=\dfrac{6t}{(t^2+1)^2}$, $\dot y=\dfrac{3(1-t^2)}{(t^2+1)^2}$, and $\dot x^2+\dot y^2=\dfrac{9(t^2+1)^2}{(t^2+1)^4}$, so $v=\dfrac{3}{t^2+1}$ ✔.

All physical quantities ($x$, $y$, $v$, $a$) are real. The complex expressions are just a convenient way to compute them.

Problems 66–68: motion

Show solutions 66–68

66. Done in the example above: $x=\frac{2t^2-1}{t^2+1}$, $y=\frac{3t}{t^2+1}$, $v=\frac3{t^2+1}$. For the acceleration: $\ddot x=\frac{6(1-3t^2)}{(t^2+1)^3}$ and $\ddot y=\frac{6t(t^2-3)}{(t^2+1)^3}$, so $\ddot x^2+\ddot y^2=\frac{36[(1-3t^2)^2+t^2(t^2-3)^2]}{(t^2+1)^6}=\frac{36(t^2+1)^3}{(t^2+1)^6}$, giving $a=\frac{6}{(t^2+1)^{3/2}}$ ✔.

Bonus: the path is a circle. $x-\tfrac12=\frac{3(t^2-1)}{2(t^2+1)}$, so $(x-\tfrac12)^2+y^2=\frac{9(t^2-1)^2+36t^2}{4(t^2+1)^2}=\frac94$: the circle $|z-\tfrac12|=\tfrac32$.

67. $z=\frac{1-it}{2t+i}$. Then $\frac{dz}{dt}=\frac{-i(2t+i)-2(1-it)}{(2t+i)^2}=\frac{-1}{(2t+i)^2}$, so $v=\frac{1}{4t^2+1}$. Next, $\frac{d^2z}{dt^2}=\frac{4}{(2t+i)^3}$, so $a=\frac{4}{(4t^2+1)^{3/2}}$.

68. $z=\cos2t+i\sin2t=e^{2it}$, so $\dot z=2ie^{2it}$ and $v=2$, and $\ddot z=-4e^{2it}$, so $a=4$. Uniform circular motion on the unit circle, counter-clockwise, with angular velocity 2. The acceleration $-4z$ points to the centre (centripetal, $v^2/r=4$).

More worked examples (exam level)

Example — Solve $z^2+|z|=0$
  1. $x^2-y^2+\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=0$ (real part) and $2xy=0$ (imaginary part).
  2. If $x=0$: $-y^2+|y|=0$, so $|y|=0$ or $1$, giving $z=0,\ \pm i$.
  3. If $y=0$: $x^2+|x|=0$, so $x=0$.
  4. Solutions: $z=0,\ i,\ -i$.
Example — Largest $|z|$ if $\left|z-\dfrac4z\right|=2$
  1. Reverse triangle inequality: $|z|-\dfrac4{|z|}\le\left|z-\dfrac4z\right|=2$.
  2. With $r=|z|$: $r^2-2r-4\le0$, so $r\le1+\sqrt5$.
  3. When is the bound reached? $\big|z+(-4/z)\big|=|z|-|4/z|$ needs $z$ and $-4/z$ to point in opposite directions, i.e. $4/z=kz$ with $k>0$, so $z^2>0$ and $z$ is real.
  4. Check $z=1+\sqrt5$: $\ \dfrac4z=\dfrac{4}{1+\sqrt5}=\sqrt5-1$, so $z-\dfrac4z=2$ ✔.
  5. Maximum $|z|=1+\sqrt5$, attained at $z=\pm(1+\sqrt5)$. (By the same method, $\frac4{|z|}-|z|\le2$ gives the minimum $|z|=\sqrt5-1$, also attained at real $z=\pm(\sqrt5-1)$: $(\sqrt5-1)-(\sqrt5+1)=-2$ ✔.)

Lesson: always check where equality in a triangle inequality is actually attained. A purely imaginary $z=iR$ gives $|z-4/z|=R+4/R\ge4$, which is never 2, so the maximum cannot occur there.

Example — Solve $\bar z=iz^2$
  1. Moduli: $|z|=|z|^2$, so $|z|=0$ or $1$.
  2. If $|z|=1$: $\bar z=1/z$, so $1/z=iz^2$, i.e. $z^3=1/i=-i$.
  3. $z^3=-i=e^{-i\pi/2}$ gives $z=e^{i(-\pi/2+2\pi k)/3}=e^{-i\pi/6},\ e^{i\pi/2},\ e^{7i\pi/6}$.
  4. Solutions: $0,\ i,\ \pm\frac{\sqrt3}2-\frac12i$.

Going deeper: lines, Apollonius circles and conics in complex form

Lines

The perpendicular bisector of $a$ and $b$ is $|z-a|=|z-b|$. Squaring and cancelling $z\bar z$ gives

$$(\bar b-\bar a)z+(b-a)\bar z+|a|^2-|b|^2=0 .$$

Every line has the form $\bar\beta z+\beta\bar z+c=0$ with $c$ real. The vector $\beta$ is normal to the line.

Apollonius circles: $|z-a|=k|z-b|$, $k\ne1$

Square both sides: $(z-a)(\bar z-\bar a)=k^2(z-b)(\bar z-\bar b)$. Collect terms and divide by $1-k^2$:

$$z\bar z-\frac{\bar a-k^2\bar b}{1-k^2}z-\frac{a-k^2b}{1-k^2}\bar z+\frac{|a|^2-k^2|b|^2}{1-k^2}=0 .$$

Completing the square $|z-c|^2=\rho^2$ gives

$$\boxed{c=\frac{a-k^2b}{1-k^2},\qquad\rho=\frac{k\,|a-b|}{|1-k^2|}}$$

Check with the worked example $|z-1|=2|z+1|$: $c=\frac{1+4}{-3}=-\frac53$ and $\rho=\frac{2\cdot2}{3}=\frac43$. ✓ Physically, these circles are the equipotentials of two opposite line charges in 2-D. That is why they reappear in the electrostatics of 14.10.

Summary

  • Divide: multiply by the conjugate of the denominator. Multiply or power: polar is often faster.
  • Conjugate of an expression: change every $i$ (including hidden ones).
  • $|z|=\sqrt{z\bar z}$. The modulus of a product or quotient is the product or quotient of the moduli.
  • A complex equation is two real equations. $\sqrt{a+ib}$: $x^2=\frac{|w|+a}2$, $y^2=\frac{|w|-a}2$.
  • $|z-a|$ is a distance, and this gives circles, bisectors, ellipses, hyperbolas and Apollonius circles.
  • Motion: $v=|\dot z|$ and $a=|\ddot z|$.
