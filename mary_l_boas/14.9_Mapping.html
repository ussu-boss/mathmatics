14.9 Mapping

Boas, Chapter 14, Section 9 · picturing $w=f(z)$ as a transformation of the plane, images of grids and regions, Riemann surfaces, and conformal mapping: analytic functions preserve angles and the shapes of tiny figures.

Goals
  • Find and sketch the images of lines, circles and regions under a given $w=f(z)$.
  • Understand why analytic maps are conformal (angle-preserving), with magnification $|f'|$ and rotation $\arg f'$.
  • Know the standard maps ($az+b$, $1/z$, Möbius, $z^n$, $e^z$, $\ln z$, $\sin z$, Joukowski) and what each does to strips, sectors, disks and half-planes.
  • Describe Riemann surfaces of $z^3$, $\sqrt z$, $\ln z$ (Problems 9–11), and solve all of Problems 1–15.

1. A "graph" of a complex function needs two planes

For $y=f(x)$ we draw a curve in the $(x,y)$-plane. For $w=f(z)$ we would need 4 dimensions. Instead (Boas Fig. 9.1 shows the 1-D analogue) we use a $z$-plane and a $w$-plane: a point $z$ and its value $w$ are images of each other. It is most informative to map curves and regions. The correspondence is called a mapping or transformation.

Boas Example 1 — $w=i+ze^{i\pi/4}$
  1. $ze^{i\pi/4}=re^{i(\theta+\pi/4)}$ is a rotation by $45^\circ$, and adding $i$ is a translation. So the grid is rotated and shifted (Fig. 9.2).
  2. Algebraically: $w=i+(x+iy)\frac{1+i}{\sqrt2}$, so $u=\frac{x-y}{\sqrt2}$ and $v=1+\frac{x+y}{\sqrt2}$ (9.1).
  3. Eliminating: $u-v=-1-y\sqrt2$ and $u+v=1+x\sqrt2$ (9.2). So the $x$-axis ($y=0$) maps to the line $u-v=-1$, and the $y$-axis to $u+v=1$.

When elimination is hard, use (9.1) as parametric equations: e.g. for the image of $y=0$, $u=x/\sqrt2$, $v=1+x/\sqrt2$, with $x$ as the parameter.

Boas Example 2 — $w=z^2$: hyperbolas and parabolas

$u=x^2-y^2$, $v=2xy$ (9.3). The images in the $z$-plane of the lines $u=$const are the hyperbolas $x^2-y^2=$const, and of $v=$const the hyperbolas $xy=$const (Fig. 9.3). They cross at right angles.

Going the other way (Problem 1), the lines $x=a$ map to $u=a^2-\frac{v^2}{4a^2}$ (parabolas opening left), and $y=b$ to $u=\frac{v^2}{4b^2}-b^2$ (parabolas opening right).

Boas Example 3 — $w=z^2$ in polar form: two sheets

$z=re^{i\theta}$ gives $w=r^2e^{2i\theta}$: angles double. The upper half of the unit disk ($0\le\theta\le\pi$) already covers the whole unit disk in the $w$-plane. The lower half needs a second copy of the $w$-plane (sheet II, Fig. 9.4). The two sheets joined along a branch cut (here the positive real axis) form a Riemann surface; the origin is the branch point. The surface is in the $w$-plane because the inverse $z=\sqrt w$ is two-valued.

H.K. Dass §23.1–23.2Two planes, and how to "transform" a curve

Why two planes (§23.1). One Argand diagram is enough to plot $z=x+iy$. But $w=f(z)$, that is $u+iv=f(x+iy)$, involves four real variables $x,y,u,v$, and we cannot draw a 4-D graph. So we keep two separate planes: the $z$-plane for the input and the $w$-plane for the output.

Transformation (§23.2). Each point $(x,y)$ gets an image $(u,v)$, where $u=u(x,y)$ and $v=v(x,y)$ are the real and imaginary parts of $f$. As $P(x,y)$ moves along a curve $C$, its image $P'(u,v)$ moves along a curve $C'$. We say $C$ is mapped onto $C'$.

Recipe for the image of a curve. There are two equivalent routes. Use whichever is less algebra.

  1. Forward (parametric): parametrise $C$ as $x(t),y(t)$, then plot $u(x(t),y(t))$ and $v(x(t),y(t))$.
  2. Backward (eliminate): solve $w=f(z)$ for $z$, i.e. $x=x(u,v)$ and $y=y(u,v)$. Substitute into the equation of $C$. The result is the equation of $C'$ in $u,v$.
  3. Regions: map the boundary curves, then test one interior point to decide which side is the image.
Worked example (Dass Ex 1) — the rectangle $1\le x\le3$, $0\le y\le3$ under $w=z+(2+i)$
  1. $u+iv=(x+2)+i(y+1)$, so $u=x+2$ and $v=y+1$.
  2. The lines $x=1$ and $x=3$ go to $u=3$ and $u=5$. The lines $y=0$ and $y=3$ go to $v=1$ and $v=4$.
  3. The image is the rectangle $3\le u\le5$, $1\le v\le4$: the same shape, just shifted by the vector $(2,1)$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: adding a constant is the simplest mapping there is. Spotting it inside a bigger map (like $az+b$) saves a lot of algebra.

Worked example (Dass Ex 2) — the hyperbola $x^2-y^2=4$ under $w=z^2$
  1. $u+iv=(x+iy)^2=(x^2-y^2)+i\,2xy$.
  2. On the curve, $u=x^2-y^2=4$ at every point. Meanwhile $v=2xy$ runs over all real values: e.g. $x=2.5,\ y=\pm1.5$ gives $v=\pm7.5$.
  3. So the whole hyperbola maps onto the vertical line $u=4$. The right branch alone already covers the line once, and the left branch ($x\lt0$) covers it a second time, because $z$ and $-z$ have the same square.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: the level curves of $\operatorname{Re}z^2$ and $\operatorname{Im}z^2$ are the hyperbolas $x^2-y^2=c$ and $xy=c$. They straighten into grid lines under $z^2$, which is the key to the "flow in a corner" problems.

2. Conformal mapping

Write $dz=dx+i\,dy$, $dw=du+i\,dv$, so $|dz|^2=dx^2+dy^2$ and $|dw|^2=du^2+dv^2$ (9.5). For analytic $w(z)$, $dw=\frac{dw}{dz}dz$, so (9.6)

$$ds^2=dx^2+dy^2=|dz|^2=\left|\frac{dz}{dw}\right|^2(du^2+dv^2).$$

There is no $du\,dv$ term, so the curves $u=$const and $v=$const form an orthogonal coordinate system (Chapter 10), and its two scale factors are equal.

Conformal property

Near a point where $f$ is analytic and $f'(z_0)\ne0$, every tiny arc is magnified by $|f'(z_0)|$ and rotated by $\arg f'(z_0)$ (Problem 14), since $dw=f'(z_0)\,dz$. So

$$\frac{A'B'}{AB}=\frac{B'C'}{BC}=\cdots=\frac{dS}{ds}=\left|\frac{dw}{dz}\right|\qquad(9.7)$$

Small figures map to similar small figures, and angles between curves are preserved, both in size and in sense. Such a map is called conformal ("same form").

  • The property is local. $f'$ varies from point to point, so large figures do get distorted.
  • It fails where $f'=0$ (a critical point). There a zero of order $k$ of $f-f(z_0)$ multiplies angles by $k$. E.g. $w=z^2$ at $0$ turns a right angle (quarter disk) into a straight angle (half disk), as in Fig. 9.4.
  • Jacobian (Problem 12): $\dfrac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}=u_xv_y-u_yv_x=u_x^2+v_x^2=|f'(z)|^2$. So areas scale by $|f'|^2$.

Theorem (§23.4)

If $f$ is analytic at $z_0$ and $f'(z_0)\ne0$, then $w=f(z)$ is conformal at $z_0$. Every curve through $z_0$ has its tangent turned by the same angle $\lambda=\arg f'(z_0)$. Tiny lengths are stretched by $|f'(z_0)|$.

  1. Let curves $C_1$ and $C_2$ leave $z_0$ with tangent angles $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$. Take nearby points $z_1-z_0=re^{i\theta_1}$ on $C_1$, so $\theta_1\to\alpha_1$ as $r\to0$. Similarly $z_2-z_0=re^{i\theta_2}$ on $C_2$.
  2. Write the images as $w_1-w_0=\rho_1e^{i\phi_1}$ and $f'(z_0)=Re^{i\lambda}$, with $R\ne0$.
  3. $f'(z_0)=\lim\frac{w_1-w_0}{z_1-z_0}$ gives $Re^{i\lambda}=\lim\frac{\rho_1}{r}e^{i(\phi_1-\theta_1)}$. Hence $\lim\frac{\rho_1}{r}=R$ and $\lim\phi_1=\alpha_1+\lambda$.
  4. The image curve $C_1'$ therefore has a tangent at angle $\beta_1=\alpha_1+\lambda$. In the same way, $C_2'$ has $\beta_2=\alpha_2+\lambda$.
  5. So $\beta_1-\beta_2=\alpha_1-\alpha_2$: the angle between the curves is unchanged in size and sense. ∎

Consequences (Dass's notes).

  • Rotation of every tangent: $\arg f'(z_0)=\tan^{-1}\frac{v_x}{u_x}$ (taking the quadrant of $(u_x,v_x)$). Coefficient of magnification: $|f'(z_0)|$.
  • Areas scale by the Jacobian $J=\dfrac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}=u_xv_y-u_yv_x=u_x^2+v_x^2=|f'(z)|^2$, using Cauchy–Riemann.
  • Points with $f'(z)=0$ are critical points. All other points are ordinary points.
  • Harmonic functions stay harmonic, and conjugate pairs stay conjugate, after a conformal change of variables. This is why the method solves Laplace problems (14.10).
Theorem (§23.5) — critical points

If $f'$ has a zero of order $n-1$ at $z_0$ (so $f'(z_0)=\dots=f^{(n-1)}(z_0)=0$ but $f^{(n)}(z_0)\ne0$), then angles at $z_0$ are multiplied by $n$ and the local magnification is $0$.

  1. Taylor: $f(z)-f(z_0)=a_n(z-z_0)^n+\cdots$ with $a_n=\frac{f^{(n)}(z_0)}{n!}\ne0$. Let $\lambda=\arg a_n$.
  2. Then $\rho_1e^{i\phi_1}\approx|a_n|r^ne^{i(n\theta_1+\lambda)}$, so $\lim\phi_1=n\alpha_1+\lambda$, and likewise $\lim\phi_2=n\alpha_2+\lambda$.
  3. So the angle between the images is $n(\alpha_2-\alpha_1)$, which is not preserved. Also $\rho_1/r\approx|a_n|r^{n-1}\to0$. ∎

Worked example (Dass Ex 3) — $u=2x^2+y^2$ and $v=\dfrac{y^2}{x}$: the level curves are orthogonal, yet $w=u+iv$ is not conformal
  1. On $u=k_1$: $4x+2yy'=0$, so the slope is $m_1=-\frac{2x}{y}$.
  2. On $v=k_2$, i.e. $y^2=k_2x$: $2yy'=k_2=\frac{y^2}{x}$, so the slope is $m_2=\frac{y}{2x}$.
  3. $m_1m_2=-1$, so the two families cut orthogonally everywhere.
  4. But Cauchy–Riemann fails: $u_x=4x$ while $v_y=\frac{2y}{x}$. So $u+iv$ is not analytic, and angles between other pairs of curves are not preserved. In the widget ("Dass Ex 3"), choose $\alpha\ne90^\circ$ and the image angle changes.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: "the level curves are orthogonal" is necessary but not sufficient for conformality. This is a favourite trap among the options.

Worked example (Dass Ex 4) — $w=z^2$: rotation and magnification at $z=2+i$ and at $z=1+i$
  1. $f'(z)=2z$.
  2. At $z=2+i$: $f'=4+2i$. Magnification $|f'|=\sqrt{20}=2\sqrt5\approx4.47$. Rotation $\arg f'=\tan^{-1}\frac12\approx26.6^\circ$.
  3. At $z=1+i$: $f'=2+2i$. Magnification $2\sqrt2$. Rotation $\frac\pi4$.
  4. At $z=0$: $f'=0$, a critical point. Here $n=2$, so angles double: a right angle becomes a straight angle.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: "magnification and angle of rotation at $z_0$" is simply $|f'(z_0)|$ and $\arg f'(z_0)$. That is a 20-second MCQ once you know it.

Inversion sends circles to circles (Dass §23.10 proof)

Take a general circle (or line) in the $z$-plane: $A(x^2+y^2)+2gx+2fy+c=0$. Here $A=1$ for a circle and $A=0$ for a line. Substitute $x=\frac{u}{u^2+v^2}$ and $y=\frac{-v}{u^2+v^2}$, then multiply by $u^2+v^2$:

$$c\,(u^2+v^2)+2gu-2fv+A=0 .$$

All four cases. They follow from which of $A$ and $c$ vanish:

  • circle not through $0$ ($A=1$, $c\ne0$) → circle not through $0$;
  • circle through $0$ ($A=1$, $c=0$) → straight line $2gu-2fv+1=0$, not through $0$;
  • line not through $0$ ($A=0$, $c\ne0$) → circle through $0$;
  • line through $0$ ($A=0$, $c=0$) → line through $0$ (reflected: $\phi=-\theta$).

Inside the unit circle maps to outside it, and vice versa. The unit circle itself maps to itself, reflected: $e^{i\theta}\to e^{-i\theta}$.

Worked example (Dass Ex 10) — the hyperbola $x^2-y^2=1$ under $w=1/z$ becomes the lemniscate $R^2=\cos2\phi$
  1. In polar form the hyperbola is $r^2(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta)=1$, i.e. $r^2\cos2\theta=1$.

H.K. Dass §23.11–23.17Bilinear (Möbius) transformations: fixed points, cross-ratio, three-point method, inverse points

Definition (§23.11). $w=\dfrac{az+b}{cz+d}$ with $ad-bc\ne0$. The name comes from the equivalent form $cwz+dw-az-b=0$, which is linear in $w$ and linear in $z$. If $ad-bc=0$ the map is a constant, which is useless.

  • Inverse: $z=\dfrac{-dw+b}{cw-a}$, again bilinear. This is the form you substitute when finding the image of a curve.
  • Derivative: $\dfrac{dw}{dz}=\dfrac{ad-bc}{(cz+d)^2}\ne0$. So the map is conformal everywhere except at the pole $z=-d/c$, which goes to $w=\infty$. Also $z=\infty$ goes to $w=a/c$.
  • Decomposition ($c\ne0$): $w=\dfrac ac+\dfrac{bc-ad}{c}\cdot\dfrac{1}{cz+d}$. Read it as: $z\to cz+d$ (rotate, magnify, translate), then $\to\frac1{(\cdot)}$ (inversion), then $\to\times\frac{bc-ad}{c}$, then $\to+\frac ac$. Every step maps circles and lines to circles and lines (§23.10), so a bilinear map sends circles to circles, with lines counted as circles through $\infty$.

Cross-ratio (§23.13–23.14)

Dass defines the cross-ratio of four points as $(z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4)=\dfrac{(z_1-z_2)(z_3-z_4)}{(z_2-z_3)(z_4-z_1)}$.

Theorem (§23.14)

A bilinear transformation preserves the cross-ratio: $(w_1,w_2,w_3,w_4)=(z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4)$.

  1. $w_i-w_j=\dfrac{az_i+b}{cz_i+d}-\dfrac{az_j+b}{cz_j+d}=\dfrac{(ad-bc)(z_i-z_j)}{(cz_i+d)(cz_j+d)}$.
  2. In $\frac{(w_1-w_2)(w_3-w_4)}{(w_2-w_3)(w_4-w_1)}$, the factor $(ad-bc)^2$ cancels. Each of $(cz_1+d),\dots,(cz_4+d)$ appears exactly once on top and once below, so they cancel too. What remains is $(z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4)$. ∎

Properties (§23.15) and the method (§23.16)

  • Circles and lines go to circles and lines. Whether you get a line depends only on whether the curve passes through the pole $-d/c$.
  • The composition of two bilinear maps is bilinear, and so is the inverse. They form a group.
  • There is exactly one bilinear map sending three distinct points $z_1,z_2,z_3$ to three distinct points $w_1,w_2,w_3$.
  • Pairs of inverse points with respect to a circle go to pairs of inverse points with respect to the image circle (the symmetry principle, used in Dass Ex 21).

Method (§23.16). Write the cross-ratio equation with the unknown $w$ (and $z$) as the 4th point, then solve for $w$:

$$\frac{(w-w_1)(w_2-w_3)}{(w_1-w_2)(w_3-w)}=\frac{(z-z_1)(z_2-z_3)}{(z_1-z_2)(z_3-z)}.$$

If one given point is $\infty$: drop every factor that contains it. For example, with $w_3=\infty$, $\frac{w_2-w_3}{w_3-w}\to-1$.

Alternative: put $w=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}$, substitute the three pairs, and solve for the ratios $a:b:c:d$ (Dass uses this in Ex 21).

Worked example (Dass Ex 11) — send $z=1,\,i,\,-1$ to $w=i,\,0,\,-i$; then where does $|z|\lt1$ go?
  1. The cross-ratio equation is $\dfrac{(w-i)(0+i)}{(i-0)(-i-w)}=\dfrac{(z-1)(i+1)}{(1-i)(-1-z)}$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: "three points to three points, then which side goes where" is the standard bilinear question. A single test point decides the region.

Worked example (Dass Ex 12, 13, 14) — the three-point method when $\infty$ is involved

Ex 12: $i,\,-i,\,1\ \to\ 0,\,1,\,\infty$. Since $w_3=\infty$, the left side becomes $-\frac{w-0}{0-1}=w$. Hence $w=\frac{(z-i)(-i-1)}{(i+i)(1-z)}$, i.e. $\boxed{w=\dfrac{(1-i)(z-i)}{2(z-1)}}$. Check: $z=-i$ gives $\frac{(1-i)(-2i)}{2(-i-1)}=1$ ✓.

Ex 13: $1,\,-i,\,-1\ \to\ i,\,0,\,-i$. The same algebra gives $\boxed{w=\dfrac{iz-1}{iz+1}}$. Check: $z=1$ gives $\frac{i-1}{i+1}=i$ ✓.

Ex 14: $0,\,-1,\,i\ \to\ i,\,0,\,\infty$. The image $w=\infty$ at $z=i$ forces the factor $(z-i)$ in the denominator. The image $w=0$ at $z=-1$ forces $(z+1)$ on top. So $w=k\frac{z+1}{z-i}$, and $z=0\mapsto i$ gives $k\cdot\frac{1}{-i}=i$, so $k=1$: $\boxed{w=\dfrac{z+1}{z-i}}$.

The unit circle passes through the pole $z=i$, so its image is a line through $w(1)=\frac{2}{1-i}=1+i$ and $w(-1)=0$: the line $\boxed{v=u}$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: reading off "zero → factor on top, pole → factor below" is much faster than the full cross-ratio when $0$ or $\infty$ is among the images.

Worked example (Dass Ex 15, 16, Exercise 23.2 Q15) — fixed points and normal forms: all three types

Parabolic: $w=\frac{3z-4}{z-1}$. $z^2-z=3z-4$ gives $(z-2)^2=0$, a double fixed point at $z=2$. With $k=\frac{2c}{a+d}=\frac{2}{3-1}=1$, the normal form is $\boxed{\dfrac1{w-2}=\dfrac1{z-2}+1}$. Check at $z=0$: $w=4$, so $\frac12=-\frac12+1$ ✓.

Elliptic: $w=\frac{z-1}{z+1}$. $z^2+z=z-1$ gives $z=\pm i$. Then $K=\frac{a-c\alpha}{a-c\beta}=\frac{1-i}{1+i}=-i$, so $\boxed{\dfrac{w-i}{w+i}=-i\,\dfrac{z-i}{z+i}}$. Since $|K|=1$, the map is elliptic: points rotate around the fixed points. (Also $w=\frac{2z-5}{z+4}$ has fixed points $-1\pm2i$ and $|K|=1$, so it too is elliptic.)

Hyperbolic: $w=\frac{5z+4}{z+5}$. $z^2+5z=5z+4$ gives $z=\pm2$. Then $K=\frac{5-2}{5+2}=\frac37$, which is real and positive. Points flow along circles from the repelling fixed point $-2$ toward the attracting fixed point $2$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: "the fixed points of $\frac{az+b}{cz+d}$" means solving one quadratic, $cz^2+(d-a)z-b=0$. A repeated root (discriminant $(a-d)^2+4bc=0$) is the parabolic case, often asked as "the map has only one fixed point".

Worked example (Dass Ex 17, 18, 19) — which region goes where

Ex 17: $w=i\frac{1-z}{1+z}$. Expanding, $u=\frac{2y}{(1+x)^2+y^2}$ and $v=\frac{1-x^2-y^2}{(1+x)^2+y^2}$. On $|z|=1$, $v=0$: the real axis. Inside, $x^2+y^2\lt1$ gives $v\gt0$: the upper half-plane. Test point: $z=0\mapsto i$ ✓.

Ex 18: $w=\frac{i-z}{i+z}$. $|w|^2=\frac{x^2+(1-y)^2}{x^2+(1+y)^2}$. On $y=0$ this is $1$, and it is $\lt1$ exactly when $y\gt0$. So the real axis maps to $|w|=1$, and the upper half-plane to the inside of the unit disk. (It is just $-1$ times the Cayley map $\frac{z-i}{z+i}$.)

Ex 19: $w=\frac{3-z}{z-2}$ and the circle centre $\frac52$, radius $\frac12$. Dass eliminates $x,y$ to get $u\left[(u+1)^2+v^2\right]=0$, i.e. $u=0$. Shortcut: the circle passes through the pole $z=2$, so its image is a line. It passes through $w(3)=0$ and $w\left(\frac52+\frac i2\right)=\frac{1-i}{1+i}=-i$, so it is the imaginary axis. The centre $z=\frac52$ maps to $w=1$, so the inside goes to $u\gt0$, the right half-plane.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: it shows both routes, full elimination and the "pole on the circle, two image points, one test point" shortcut. Use the shortcut in the exam.

Inverse points (§23.17)

$P$ and $Q$ are inverse points with respect to a circle with centre $C$ and radius $r$ if $C,P,Q$ lie on one ray from $C$ (same side) and $CP\cdot CQ=r^2$. Every circle through $P$ and $Q$ cuts the given circle at right angles. That is why bilinear maps, which preserve circles and angles, carry inverse points to inverse points.

Worked example (Dass Ex 20) — the inverse of $a$ in the circle $|z-c|=R$ is $c+\dfrac{R^2}{\overline{a-c}}$
  1. Let $b$ be the inverse. Collinear, same side: $\arg(b-c)=\arg(a-c)=-\arg\overline{(a-c)}$. So $(b-c)\overline{(a-c)}$ has argument $0$: it is real and positive.
  2. Distance condition: $|b-c|\,|a-c|=R^2$, so $|(b-c)\overline{(a-c)}|=R^2$.
  3. A positive real number with modulus $R^2$ is $R^2$ itself. So $(b-c)\overline{(a-c)}=R^2$, i.e. $\boxed{b=c+\dfrac{R^2}{\overline{a-c}}}$. ∎

Special cases: for the unit circle, $b=\frac1{\bar a}$. The centre's inverse is $\infty$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: this formula plus the symmetry principle gives "the disk-to-disk" and "half-plane-to-disk" maps $e^{i\lambda}\frac{z-z_0}{z-\bar z_0}$ without any three-point algebra.

Worked example (Dass Ex 21) — a map from the disk $|w|\le1$ onto $|z-1|\lt1$ with $w=0\mapsto z=\frac12$ and $w=1\mapsto z=0$
  1. Take $w=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}$. Then $w=0$ at $z=\frac12$ gives $\frac a2+b=0$, and $w=1$ at $z=0$ gives $b=d$.
  2. Symmetry: $w=0$ and $w=\infty$ are inverse points of $|w|=1$. So their $z$-images, $\frac12$ and $1+\frac{1^2}{\overline{(1/2-1)}}=-1$, are inverse points of $|z-1|=1$. Hence $w=\infty$ at $z=-1$: $-c+d=0$.
  3. So $a=-2b$ and $c=d=b$, giving $\boxed{w=\dfrac{-2z+1}{z+1}}$. Check: $z=2$ (on the circle) gives $w=-1$, and $|w|=1$ ✓.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: when the problem gives only two point-pairs plus "circle to circle", the third condition comes from inverse points.

Worked example (Dass Ex 22) — two bilinear maps with fixed points $1$ and $2$
  1. The fixed-point equation $cz^2-(a-d)z-b=0$ must be a multiple of $(z-1)(z-2)=z^2-3z+2$. So $\frac{a-d}{c}=3$ and $-\frac bc=2$, i.e. $b=-2c$ and $d=a-3c$.
  2. So $w=\dfrac{az-2c}{cz+a-3c}$. The choices $(a,c)=(1,-1)$ and $(2,-1)$ give $\boxed{w=\dfrac{z+2}{4-z}}$ and $\boxed{w=\dfrac{2(z+1)}{5-z}}$. Check: $\frac{1+2}{4-1}=1$ ✓ and $\frac{2+2}{4-2}=2$ ✓.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: two fixed points leave a one-parameter family of maps (the multiplier $K$), so the answer is never unique. Expect "which of these has fixed points …" instead.

Worked example (Dass Ex 23, 24, 25) — the image of a circle by substituting the inverse map

Ex 23: $w=\frac{2z+3}{z-4}$, circle $x^2+y^2-4x=0$. Write the circle as $z\bar z-2(z+\bar z)=0$ and substitute $z=\frac{4w+3}{w-2}$. After clearing denominators, $22(w+\bar w)+33=0$, i.e. $\boxed{4u+3=0}$. It is a line because the circle passes through the pole $z=4$.

Ex 24: $w=\frac{5-4z}{4z-2}$, circle $|z|=1$. $z=\frac{2w+5}{4w+4}$, so $|z|=1$ becomes $|2w+5|=|4w+4|$. Squaring: $12(u^2+v^2)+12u-9=0$, i.e. $u^2+v^2+u-\frac34=0$. This is the circle with centre $-\frac12$ and radius $1$. Check: $z=\pm1$ go to $\frac12$ and $-\frac32$ ✓.

Ex 25: $w=\frac{z-i}{iz-1}$, circle $x^2+y^2-4y+2=0$ (centre $2i$, radius $\sqrt2$). From $z=\frac{w-i}{iw-1}$: $x^2+y^2=\frac{u^2+(v-1)^2}{u^2+(v+1)^2}$ and $y=-\frac{u^2+v^2-1}{u^2+(v+1)^2}$. Substituting gives $\boxed{7(u^2+v^2)+2v-1=0}$: the circle with centre $-\frac i7$ and radius $\frac{2\sqrt2}{7}$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: the $z\bar z$ form of a circle, $z\bar z+\bar\beta z+\beta\bar z+\gamma=0$, makes these substitutions mechanical.

Worked example (Dass Ex 27) — the circle $|z-1|=1$ under $w=z^2$ becomes the cardioid $R=2(1+\cos\phi)$
  1. Put the pole at the circle's centre: $z=1+e^{i\theta}$.

Worked example (Dass Exercise 23.3, Q1) — the triangle bounded by $x=1$, $y=1$, $x+y=1$ under $w=z^2$
  1. $x=1$ ↦ $v^2=-4(u-1)$, i.e. $4u+v^2=4$.
  2. $y=1$ ↦ $v^2=4(u+1)$, i.e. $4u-v^2=-4$.
  3. $x+y=1$: $u=(x-y)(x+y)=x-y=2x-1$ and $v=2x(1-x)$. Eliminating $x=\frac{u+1}2$ gives $v=\frac{1-u^2}{2}$, i.e. $\boxed{u^2+2v=1}$. The printed answer reads $u^2-2v=1$, but the midpoint $z=\frac{1+i}2\mapsto\frac i2$ satisfies $u^2+2v=1$, so the sign must be $+$.

Worked example (Dass §23.20, Exercises 23.3 Q6–Q8) — the Joukowski map $w=z+\frac1z$ (or $z+\frac{a^2}{z}$)
  1. $\frac{dw}{dz}=1-\frac1{z^2}$ vanishes at $z=\pm1$, the critical points. These are exactly where the circle $|z|=1$ folds flat onto $[-2,2]$.
  2. For $|z|=c$: $u=(c+\frac1c)\cos\theta$ and $v=(c-\frac1c)\sin\theta$, an ellipse. Its foci are at $\pm\sqrt{(c+\frac1c)^2-(c-\frac1c)^2}=\pm2$ for every $c$: the ellipses are confocal.
  3. For a ray $\arg z=\alpha$: $\frac{u^2}{4\cos^2\alpha}-\frac{v^2}{4\sin^2\alpha}=1$. Its eccentricity is $\sqrt{1+\tan^2\alpha}=\sec\alpha$ (Q7).
  4. For $w=z+\frac{a^2}{z}$ (Q8): the circle $|z|=a$ ↦ the segment from $-2a$ to $2a$, of length $4a$. The circle $|z|=b\gt a$ ↦ an ellipse with semi-axes $b\pm\frac{a^2}{b}$ and foci $\pm2a$, the ends of that segment.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: this is flow past a cylinder (14.10) and the aerofoil map. "The circle maps to a segment of length $4a$" is a frequent one-liner.

Worked example (Dass Ex 28) — the rectangle $1\le x\le2$, $2\le y\le3$ under $w=e^z$
  1. $Re^{i\phi}=e^xe^{iy}$, so $R=e^x$ and $\phi=y$.
  2. $x=1$ ↦ circle $R=e\approx2.72$; $x=2$ ↦ circle $R=e^2\approx7.39$.
  3. $y=2$ ↦ ray $\phi=2$ rad ($\approx114.6^\circ$); $y=3$ ↦ ray $\phi=3$ rad ($\approx171.9^\circ$).
  4. The image is the piece of the annulus $e\le R\le e^2$ between these two rays: an annular sector in the second quadrant.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: $e^z$ turns rectangles into annular sectors, and its inverse $\log z$ turns annular sectors into rectangles. This is the standard trick for problems between two circles or two rays.

Worked example (Dass Ex 29) — the strip $-\frac\pi2\lt x\lt\frac\pi2$, $1\lt y\lt2$ under $w=\sin z$
  1. $u=\sin x\cosh y$ and $v=\cos x\sinh y$. So $\frac{u^2}{\cosh^2y}+\frac{v^2}{\sinh^2y}=1$.
  2. $y=1$ ↦ the ellipse with semi-axes $\cosh1\approx1.543$ and $\sinh1\approx1.175$. $y=2$ ↦ the ellipse with semi-axes $\cosh2\approx3.762$ and $\sinh2\approx3.627$, i.e. $\frac{u^2}{14.15}+\frac{v^2}{13.15}=1$.

  4. The image is the upper half of the region between the two confocal ellipses (foci $\pm1$), cut off by the $u$-axis.

4. Riemann surfaces (Problems 9–11)

When the inverse of a map is multivalued, the plane holding its values must be replaced by several sheets glued along a branch cut:

  • $w=z^3$: each $w\ne0$ has three preimages, so the Riemann surface (in the $w$-plane) has 3 sheets. Each sheet is the image of a $120^\circ$ sector of the $z$-plane. They are joined cyclically along the cut (I→II→III→I), with branch point $w=0$.
  • $w=\sqrt z$: two-valued as a function of $z$, so the surface is in the $z$-plane with 2 sheets (the half-planes $\operatorname{Re}w>0$ and $\operatorname{Re}w\lt0$ correspond to them).
  • $w=\ln z$: infinitely many sheets (one for each strip $2\pi n\le v\lt2\pi(n+1)$ of the $w$-plane), forming a spiral staircase (see Chapter 2, §13).

Show solutions 12–15 (Jacobian, magnification, rotation)

12. $J=\begin{vmatrix}u_x&u_y\\v_x&v_y\end{vmatrix}=u_xv_y-u_yv_x\overset{CR}{=}u_x^2+v_x^2=|u_x+iv_x|^2=|f'(z)|^2$.

13. $\begin{pmatrix}du\\dv\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}dx\\dy\end{pmatrix}$ with $a=u_x$, $b=v_x$ (using CR). Then $du^2+dv^2=(dx,dy)J^TJ(dx,dy)^T=(a^2+b^2)(dx^2+dy^2)$, so $\frac{dS}{ds}=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}=\left|\frac{dw}{dz}\right|$. (And $J$ is $|f'|$ times a rotation matrix.)

14. $dw=f'(z)\,dz$, so $\arg dw=\arg dz+\arg f'(z)$: every arc through $z$ is rotated by the same angle $\arg f'(z)$.

15. $\frac{dT}{ds}=\frac{dT}{dS}\frac{dS}{ds}=\left|\frac{dw}{dz}\right|\frac{dT}{dS}$. So rates of change in corresponding directions are proportional, with factor $|dw/dz|$. In particular, zero normal derivative (an insulated boundary) maps to zero normal derivative, which is what makes 14.10 work.

P2. Show $w=\frac{z-i}{z+i}$ maps the upper half-plane onto the unit disk. Medium
Show solution

$|w|=\frac{|z-i|}{|z+i|}$ is less than 1 iff $z$ is closer to $i$ than to $-i$, i.e. iff $y>0$. The real axis (equidistant) maps to $|w|=1$.

P3. Where is $w=z^3-3z$ not conformal? Easy
Show solution

$w'=3z^2-3=0$ at $z=\pm1$. There angles are doubled.

P4. Under $w=\frac1z$, find the image of the line $x=1$. Medium
Show solution

$u=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}=\frac{1}{1+y^2}$ and $v=\frac{-y}{1+y^2}$. Then $u^2+v^2=\frac{1}{1+y^2}=u$: the circle $\left|w-\frac12\right|=\frac12$, without $w=0$ (which corresponds to $z=\infty$).

P5. Find the magnification and rotation of $w=z^2$ at $z=1+i$. Easy
Show solution

$f'=2z=2+2i$: magnification $2\sqrt2$, rotation $45^\circ$. Area magnification $|f'|^2=8$.

7. Going deeper: Möbius maps, the Joukowski map, and area scaling

Möbius (bilinear) transformations $w=\dfrac{az+b}{cz+d}$, $ad-bc\ne0$

Each one is a composition of four simple maps: translate $z\to z+\frac dc$, invert $z\to\frac1z$, rotate and scale, translate. Translations, rotations and scalings send circles to circles. Inversion sends "circles and lines" to "circles and lines", with lines counted as circles through $\infty$. Therefore:

Properties
  • Circles and lines go to circles and lines. A circle through the pole $z=-d/c$ becomes a line.
  • The map is fixed uniquely by the images of three points. It preserves the cross-ratio $\frac{(z-z_1)(z_2-z_3)}{(z-z_3)(z_2-z_1)}$.
  • It has at most two fixed points (solve $cz^2+(d-a)z-b=0$), unless it is the identity.
  • It is conformal everywhere except at the pole, since $w'=\frac{ad-bc}{(cz+d)^2}\ne0$.

Which side goes where? Test one interior point. For example, $w=\frac{z-i}{z+i}$ sends $i\to0$, so the upper half-plane goes to the inside of the unit disk.

The Joukowski map $w=z+\dfrac1z$ (aerofoils, flow past a cylinder)

Put $z=re^{i\theta}$:

$$u=\left(r+\frac1r\right)\cos\theta,\qquad v=\left(r-\frac1r\right)\sin\theta .$$

A circle $|z|=r\ne1$ becomes an ellipse with semi-axes $r+\frac1r$ and $\left|r-\frac1r\right|$, and foci at $\pm2$. The unit circle collapses onto the segment $[-2,2]$, traversed twice. Rays $\theta=$ const become hyperbolas with the same foci. Shifting the circle off-centre gives the Joukowski aerofoil.

How lengths and areas scale

Near $z_0$, $w-w_0\approx f'(z_0)(z-z_0)$: a rotation by $\arg f'$ and a scaling by $|f'|$. So lengths scale by $|f'(z_0)|$ and areas by $|f'(z_0)|^2$. Indeed the Jacobian of $(x,y)\to(u,v)$ is $u_xv_y-u_yv_x=u_x^2+v_x^2=|f'|^2$ by Cauchy–Riemann.

11. Summary

  • Map curves and regions between a $z$-plane and a $w$-plane, using either elimination or parametric equations.
  • Analytic and $f'\ne0$ means conformal: magnification $|f'|$, rotation $\arg f'$, angles kept, and the Jacobian is $|f'|^2$.
  • The $u=$const and $v=$const curves form an orthogonal net with equal scale factors.
  • Multivalued inverses lead to Riemann surfaces: 2 sheets ($z^2$ / $\sqrt z$), $n$ sheets ($z^n$), $\infty$ sheets ($\ln$).
