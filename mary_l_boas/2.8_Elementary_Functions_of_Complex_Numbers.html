2.8 Elementary Functions of Complex Numbers

Boas, Chapter 2, Section 8 · evaluating polynomials and rational functions at complex points, defining $e^z$ by its power series, the law $e^{z_1}e^{z_2}=e^{z_1+z_2}$, and $\frac{d}{dz}e^z=e^z$.

Goals
  • Understand why we define $e^z$ by a power series, and what rules such a definition must respect.
  • Evaluate polynomials and rational functions at complex numbers quickly.
  • Prove $e^{z_1}e^{z_2}=e^{z_1+z_2}$ and $(e^z)'=e^z$ from the series.
  • Get $e^{x+iy}=e^x(\cos y+i\sin y)$ and use it: modulus, angle, periodicity, zeros.
  • See functions of $z$ as mappings of the plane (grids, landscapes).

1. What is an "elementary function" of $z$?

The elementary functions are powers and roots, the trigonometric functions and their inverses, logarithms and exponentials, and combinations of these. For real arguments you know how to compute them. Now we want things like $i^i$, $\sin(1+i)$ and $\ln i$. These are not curiosities: they appear in physics calculations whose final answers ($\operatorname{Re}z$, $\operatorname{Im}z$, $|z|$, angle) are real and measurable.

Two requirements for any definition
  1. Consistency: when $z=x$ is real ($y=0$), the new definition must give the old real function.
  2. Keep the laws: familiar identities and calculus rules should still hold, e.g. $\sin2z=2\sin z\cos z$ and $\frac{d}{dz}e^z=e^z$.

2. Polynomials and rational functions: just substitute

These use only $+,-,\times,\div$, which we already know how to do with complex numbers.

Boas example — $f(z)=\dfrac{z^2+1}{z-3}$ at $z=i-2$
  1. Numerator: $(i-2)^2+1=i^2-4i+4+1=4-4i$.
  2. Denominator: $i-2-3=i-5$.
  3. Divide, multiplying by the conjugate $-i-5$: $\dfrac{(4-4i)(-i-5)}{(i-5)(-i-5)}=\dfrac{-4i-20+4i^2+20i}{1+25}=\dfrac{-24+16i}{26}$.
  4. $f(i-2)=\dfrac{8i-12}{13}$.
Speed tip: Horner's rule

For $p(z)=z^3-2z+1$ write $p(z)=((z)z-2)z+1$, which needs only two multiplications. At $z=1+i$: $(1+i)^2=2i$, then $2i-2$, then $(2i-2)(1+i)=2i-2-2-2i=-4$, and $-4+1=-3$. So $p(1+i)=-3$.

Facts carried over from algebra: a polynomial of degree $n$ has exactly $n$ complex zeros, and if its coefficients are real, $p(\bar z)=\overline{p(z)}$, so its zeros come in conjugate pairs (Section 2.5B).

3. Defining $e^z$

We cannot "multiply $e$ by itself $z$ times" when $z=2+3i$. So we use the power series, which only needs $+$ and $\times$:

$$\boxed{\,e^z=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{z^n}{n!}=1+z+\frac{z^2}{2!}+\frac{z^3}{3!}+\cdots\,}\qquad(8.1)$$
  • It converges for every $z$, since $\rho=\lim\frac{|z|}{n+1}=0$ (Boas Problem 7.1).
  • For $z=x$ real it is the familiar series of $e^x$: requirement 1 ✔.
  • It satisfies the law of exponents and the derivative rule (below): requirement 2 ✔.

It can be shown that (8.1) is the only definition with these properties (uniqueness of analytic continuation, Ch. 14).

Proof of the law of exponents $e^{z_1}e^{z_2}=e^{z_1+z_2}$ (Boas Problem 8.1)

Both series converge absolutely, so we may multiply them and collect the terms of total degree $n$ (Cauchy product):

$$e^{z_1}e^{z_2}=\sum_{j=0}^\infty\frac{z_1^j}{j!}\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{z_2^k}{k!}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\ \sum_{j+k=n}\frac{z_1^jz_2^k}{j!\,k!}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac1{n!}\sum_{j=0}^n\binom nj z_1^jz_2^{n-j}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(z_1+z_2)^n}{n!}.$$

The last step is the binomial theorem, which holds for complex numbers because $z_1z_2=z_2z_1$. $\blacksquare$

Proof that $\frac{d}{dz}e^z=e^z$ (Boas Problem 8.2)

Differentiate term by term (allowed inside the disk of convergence, which here is the whole plane), using $\frac{d}{dz}z^n=nz^{n-1}$:

$$\frac{d}{dz}\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{z^n}{n!}=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{nz^{n-1}}{n!}=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{z^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}=\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{z^m}{m!}=e^z.\ \blacksquare$$

More generally, all the differentiation and integration formulas of elementary calculus hold with $x$ replaced by $z$ (Boas Ch. 14).

4. The key consequence: $e^{x+iy}=e^x(\cos y+i\sin y)$

By the law of exponents, $e^{x+iy}=e^xe^{iy}$. Write out the series for $e^{iy}$ and group the even and odd powers (Section 2.9 does this in detail):

$$e^{iy}=\left(1-\frac{y^2}{2!}+\frac{y^4}{4!}-\dots\right)+i\left(y-\frac{y^3}{3!}+\dots\right)=\cos y+i\sin y.$$ $$\boxed{e^{z}=e^{x}(\cos y+i\sin y)}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \operatorname{Re}e^z=e^x\cos y,\quad\operatorname{Im}e^z=e^x\sin y .$$

Things that change from real to complex
  • $e^x$ is one-to-one, but $e^z$ is not: $e^{0}=e^{2\pi i}=e^{4\pi i}=1$. That is why $\ln z$ will be multivalued (Section 2.13).
  • $e^x>0$, but $e^z$ takes every nonzero complex value, including negative reals.

Boas Problem 8.3: series for $e^x\cos x$ and $e^x\sin x$

Put $y=x$ in $e^{x+iy}=e^x(\cos y+i\sin y)$: $\ e^{x(1+i)}=e^x\cos x+ie^x\sin x$. Now expand the left side and use $(1+i)^n=(\sqrt2)^ne^{in\pi/4}$:

$$e^{x(1+i)}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(1+i)^nx^n}{n!}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{2^{n/2}x^n}{n!}\left(\cos\frac{n\pi}4+i\sin\frac{n\pi}4\right).$$ $$e^x\cos x=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{2^{n/2}\cos\frac{n\pi}{4}}{n!}x^n=1+x-\frac{x^3}{3}-\frac{x^4}{6}-\frac{x^5}{30}+\frac{x^7}{630}+\cdots$$ $$e^x\sin x=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{2^{n/2}\sin\frac{n\pi}{4}}{n!}x^n=x+x^2+\frac{x^3}{3}-\frac{x^5}{30}-\frac{x^6}{90}-\frac{x^7}{630}+\cdots$$

Since $\cos\frac{n\pi}4=0$ for $n=2,6,10,\dots$, the $e^x\cos x$ series has no $x^2$, $x^6$, $x^{10}$ terms. Similarly $e^x\sin x$ has no $x^4,x^8,\dots$ terms (Boas Problem 17.30).

5. Seeing a complex function: mapping and landscapes

A function $w=f(z)$ needs 4 real dimensions to graph ($x,y$ in and $u,v$ out), so we use two pictures:

  • Mapping picture: draw a grid in the $z$-plane and draw its image in the $w$-plane. For $e^z$, vertical lines $x=c$ become circles of radius $e^c$, and horizontal lines $y=c$ become rays at angle $c$. The horizontal strip $-\pi\lt y\le\pi$ covers the entire $w$-plane except $0$ exactly once.
  • Landscape picture: plot the height $|f(z)|$ over the $z$-plane and colour each point by $\arg f(z)$. For $e^z$ the height is $e^x$ (a ramp that does not depend on $y$), and the colour cycles with period $2\pi$ in $y$. For polynomials, the zeros are where the landscape touches the floor.

6. Worked examples

Example 1 — $e^{1+i\pi/2}$ and $e^{2-i\pi/3}$
  1. $e^{1+i\pi/2}=e\left(\cos\frac\pi2+i\sin\frac\pi2\right)=ei\approx2.718i$.
  2. $e^{2-i\pi/3}=e^2\left(\frac12-\frac{\sqrt3}2i\right)\approx3.695-6.399i$.
Example 2 — Re, Im and modulus of $e^{z^2}$
  1. $z^2=(x^2-y^2)+2ixy$.
  2. $e^{z^2}=e^{x^2-y^2}\big(\cos2xy+i\sin2xy\big)$.
  3. $|e^{z^2}|=e^{x^2-y^2}$: it grows along the real axis and decays along the imaginary axis (a Gaussian there: $e^{-y^2}$).
Example 3 — Where is $|e^{iz}|\gt1$?
  1. $iz=i(x+iy)=-y+ix$.
  2. $|e^{iz}|=e^{-y}\gt1\iff y\lt0$: the lower half-plane.
  3. This matters for waves: $e^{i(kz-\omega t)}$ with complex $k$ grows or decays depending on the sign of $\operatorname{Im}k$.
Example 4 — Solve $e^z=1$ and $e^z=-1$
  1. $e^z=e^xe^{iy}=1=1\cdot e^{i0}$. Compare moduli: $e^x=1$, so $x=0$. Compare angles: $y=0+2\pi n$.
  2. So $z=2\pi in$, $n\in\mathbb Z$: infinitely many solutions.
  3. $e^z=-1=e^{i\pi}$ gives $x=0$ and $y=\pi+2\pi n$, so $z=(2n+1)\pi i$.

7. Practice problems

P1. Evaluate $p(z)=z^3-2z+1$ at $z=1+i$ and $z=i$. Easy
Show solution

$p(1+i)=-3$ (see the Horner example). $p(i)=i^3-2i+1=-i-2i+1=1-3i$.

P2. Evaluate $f(z)=\dfrac{z-1}{z^2+1}$ at $z=2i$. Easy
Show solution

$\dfrac{2i-1}{-4+1}=\dfrac{-1+2i}{-3}=\dfrac13-\dfrac23i$.

P3. Show from the series that $\overline{e^z}=e^{\bar z}$. Medium
Show solution

Conjugate term by term: $\overline{\sum z^n/n!}=\sum\bar z^n/n!$ (the coefficients $1/n!$ are real, and conjugation is continuous, so it passes through the limit).

P4. Find Re and Im of $e^{1/z}$. Medium
Show solution

$\frac1z=\frac{x-iy}{x^2+y^2}$, so $e^{1/z}=e^{x/(x^2+y^2)}\left[\cos\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}-i\sin\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}\right]$.

P5. Show $e^z\ne0$ for all $z$, and find all $z$ with $e^z=i$. Medium
Show solution

$|e^z|=e^x>0$. For $e^z=i=e^{i\pi/2}$: $x=0$ and $y=\frac\pi2+2\pi n$, so $z=i\left(\frac\pi2+2\pi n\right)$.

P6. Using $e^{z_1}e^{z_2}=e^{z_1+z_2}$, prove the addition formulas for $\cos(a+b)$ and $\sin(a+b)$ (with $a,b$ real). Medium
Show solution

$e^{i(a+b)}=e^{ia}e^{ib}=(\cos a+i\sin a)(\cos b+i\sin b)=(\cos a\cos b-\sin a\sin b)+i(\sin a\cos b+\cos a\sin b)$. Equating real and imaginary parts with $\cos(a+b)+i\sin(a+b)$ gives both formulas at once.

P7. Find the $x^8$ coefficient of $e^x\cos x$. Hard
Show solution

$\dfrac{2^4\cos2\pi}{8!}=\dfrac{16}{40320}=\dfrac{1}{2520}$.

P8. Show that $\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(1+i)^n}{n!}=e\cos1+ie\sin1$, and use this to show $\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{2^{n/2}\cos(n\pi/4)}{n!}=e\cos1$. Medium
Show solution

The series is $e^{1+i}=e(\cos1+i\sin1)$. Writing $(1+i)^n=2^{n/2}e^{in\pi/4}$ and taking real parts gives the second identity.

P9. Where in the plane is $|e^{z^2}|\lt1$? Medium
Show solution

$e^{x^2-y^2}\lt1\iff y^2\gt x^2\iff|y|\gt|x|$: the two "vertical" quarter-wedges between the lines $y=\pm x$.

9. Summary

  • Polynomials and rational functions: substitute and simplify.
  • $e^z=\sum z^n/n!$ converges everywhere, reduces to $e^x$, and keeps $e^{z_1}e^{z_2}=e^{z_1+z_2}$ and $(e^z)'=e^z$.
  • $e^{x+iy}=e^x(\cos y+i\sin y)$: $|e^z|=e^x$, $\arg e^z=y$, never zero, period $2\pi i$.
  • A complex function is best "seen" as a mapping, or as a landscape coloured by phase.
