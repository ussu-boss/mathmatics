14.6 Methods of Finding Residues

Boas, Chapter 14, Section 6 · A. from the Laurent series · B. at a simple pole: $\lim(z-z_0)f$ and $g(z_0)/h'(z_0)$ · C. at a multiple pole: the derivative rule · plus faster tricks, a decision guide, a residue calculator, and unlimited practice.

Goals
  • Choose the fastest method for any residue, and recognise the order of a pole before you start.
  • Never make the classic mistakes: multiplying by $(2z+1)$ instead of $(z+\frac12)$, or using a series about the wrong point.
  • Solve all of Boas's Section 6 problems: 1–9, 10–13 (proofs), 14–35 (residues), 14′–35′ (contour integrals), 36, 37.

A. Laurent series

If the Laurent series about $z_0$ (valid near $z_0$) is easy to write, the residue is the coefficient $b_1$ of $\frac1{z-z_0}$.

Caution

The series you know for $e^z$, $\sin z$, … are about $z=0$, so they give residues only at the origin directly. For another point, rewrite in powers of $(z-z_0)$ first.

Boas example — residue of $\dfrac{e^z}{z-1}$ at $z=1$
$$\frac{e^z}{z-1}=\frac{e\cdot e^{z-1}}{z-1}=\frac{e}{z-1}\left[1+(z-1)+\frac{(z-1)^2}{2!}+\cdots\right]=\frac{e}{z-1}+e+\cdots\quad\Rightarrow\quad R(1)=e .$$

B. Simple poles

Rule B $$R(z_0)=\lim_{z\to z_0}(z-z_0)f(z)\qquad\text{(simple pole)}\qquad(6.1)$$

Why (Problem 10): $(z-z_0)f=b_1+a_0(z-z_0)+a_1(z-z_0)^2+\cdots\to b_1$.

Boas Example 1 — $f(z)=\dfrac{z}{(2z+1)(5-z)}$: $R(-\frac12)$ and $R(5)$
  1. Multiply by $\left(z+\frac12\right)$, not by $(2z+1)$: $\left(z+\frac12\right)f=\dfrac{z}{2(5-z)}$, so $R\left(-\frac12\right)=\dfrac{-\frac12}{2\cdot\frac{11}2}=-\dfrac1{22}$.
  2. $(z-5)f=\dfrac{-z}{2z+1}$, so $R(5)=-\dfrac5{11}$.
Boas Examples 2, 3 — $\frac{\cos z}{z}$ and $\cot z$ at $0$

$R(0)=\cos0=1$. For $\cot z$: $\lim\frac{z\cos z}{\sin z}=\cos0\cdot\lim\frac{z}{\sin z}=1$.

The $g/h'$ formula (6.2) — usually the fastest

If $f=\dfrac{g(z)}{h(z)}$ with $g(z_0)$ finite and nonzero, $h(z_0)=0$, $h'(z_0)\ne0$:

$$\boxed{R(z_0)=\frac{g(z_0)}{h'(z_0)}}$$

Proof (Problem 11): $\lim\frac{(z-z_0)g}{h}=g(z_0)\lim\frac{z-z_0}{h(z)-h(z_0)}=\frac{g(z_0)}{h'(z_0)}$, by the definition of $h'$.

Boas Example 4 — $\dfrac{\sin z}{1-z^4}$ at $z=i$
$$R(i)=\frac{\sin z}{-4z^3}\bigg|_{z=i}=\frac{\sin i}{-4i^3}=\frac{\sin i}{4i}=\frac{i\sinh1}{4i}=\frac14\sinh1 .$$

How do you know the pole is simple?

  • If the limit in (6.1) is a finite nonzero constant, it was a simple pole and that constant is the residue. If the limit is $0$, the point is regular (residue $0$). If it is infinite, the pole has higher order.
  • Think of $g$ and $h$ as power series in $(z-z_0)$. If the denominator has one more power of $(z-z_0)$ than the numerator, it is a simple pole. Example: $z\cot^2z=\frac{z\cos^2z}{\sin^2z}=\frac{z(1+\cdots)}{z^2(1+\cdots)}$ has a simple pole at $0$.

C. Multiple poles

Rule C

If $f$ has a pole of order $\le m$ at $z_0$:

$$\boxed{R(z_0)=\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\left.\frac{d^{\,m-1}}{dz^{m-1}}\Big[(z-z_0)^mf(z)\Big]\right|_{z=z_0}}$$

Multiply by $(z-z_0)^m$ (any $m$ at least the order), differentiate $m-1$ times, divide by $(m-1)!$, and evaluate at $z_0$.

Proof (Problem 12): $(z-z_0)^mf=b_m+\dots+b_1(z-z_0)^{m-1}+a_0(z-z_0)^m+\cdots$. The $(m-1)$-th derivative at $z_0$ picks out $(m-1)!\,b_1$. (Problem 13 gives the same result from Cauchy's derivative formula.)

Boas Example 5 — $\dfrac{z\sin z}{(z-\pi)^3}$ at $z=\pi$
  1. Take $m=3$ (allowed: the order is at most 3; it is actually 2, since $\sin\pi=0$).
  2. $R(\pi)=\frac1{2!}\frac{d^2}{dz^2}(z\sin z)\big|_\pi=\frac12[-z\sin z+2\cos z]_\pi=\frac12(-2)=-1$.

Tip: use Leibniz's rule for derivatives of products.

Do not use rule C when a series is quicker

For $\frac{\cosh z-1}{z^7}$ at $0$, rule C needs six derivatives. The series $\cosh z-1=\frac{z^2}{2!}+\frac{z^4}{4!}+\frac{z^6}{6!}+\cdots$ gives the residue $\frac1{720}$ at once.

D. More tricks worth knowing

Decision guide

  1. Is $z_0$ really singular? Check the zero orders of the numerator and denominator. If it is removable, $R=0$.
  2. Is it a simple pole of $g/h$ with $h'(z_0)\ne0$? Use $g(z_0)/h'(z_0)$.
  3. Is it a multiple pole with a clean factor $(z-z_0)^n$? Use rule C or the Taylor coefficient of $g$.
  4. Is it at $0$ with standard functions, or an essential singularity? Write the Laurent series.

H. Summary

  • Simple pole: $R=\lim(z-z_0)f=\dfrac{g(z_0)}{h'(z_0)}$.
  • Pole of order $\le m$: $R=\frac1{(m-1)!}\frac{d^{m-1}}{dz^{m-1}}[(z-z_0)^mf]_{z_0}$.
  • At $0$ with standard functions, or at essential singularities: read $b_1$ from the series.
  • Removable points and even functions give residue $0$. Always expand about the right point.
