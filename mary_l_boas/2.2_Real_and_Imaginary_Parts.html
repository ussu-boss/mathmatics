2.2 Real and Imaginary Parts of a Complex Number

Boas, Chapter 2, Section 2 · $\operatorname{Re}z$, $\operatorname{Im}z$, complex numbers as ordered pairs $(x,y)$, and how to split any expression into its real and imaginary parts.

Goals
  • Read off $\operatorname{Re}$ and $\operatorname{Im}$ of any complex number or expression, including ones containing $x,y$.
  • Understand a complex number as a pair of real numbers with a special multiplication rule.
  • Know which rules $\operatorname{Re}$ and $\operatorname{Im}$ obey (linear) and which they do not (multiplicative).
  • Use "equal complex numbers ⇒ equal real parts and equal imaginary parts" to turn one complex equation into two real equations.

1. Definitions

Definition

For $z=x+iy$ with $x,y$ real:

$$\operatorname{Re}z=x\quad(\text{real part}),\qquad \operatorname{Im}z=y\quad(\text{imaginary part}).$$

In $5+3i$: real part $5$, imaginary part $3$.

The most common mistake

$\operatorname{Im}(5+3i)=3$, not $3i$. As Boas says, the imaginary part of a complex number is not imaginary! Both $\operatorname{Re}z$ and $\operatorname{Im}z$ are real numbers.

Careful with hidden $i$'s and hidden real parts

To read off Re and Im you must first write the number in the form $x+iy$ with $x,y$ real:

  • $\operatorname{Re}(i^2)=-1$ and $\operatorname{Im}(i^2)=0$, because $i^2$ is real.
  • $\dfrac{1}{i}=-i$, so $\operatorname{Re}=0$ and $\operatorname{Im}=-1$ (not $+1$!).
  • $\operatorname{Im}(a+ib)=b$ only if $a,b$ are real. If $a=i$ and $b=1$, then $a+ib=2i$, so $\operatorname{Im}=2$, not $1$.

2. Equality: one complex equation = two real equations

Key fact $$x_1+iy_1=x_2+iy_2\iff x_1=x_2\ \text{ and }\ y_1=y_2\qquad(x_1,x_2,y_1,y_2\ \text{real}).$$

Why it is true. Suppose $x_1+iy_1=x_2+iy_2$. Then $x_1-x_2=i(y_2-y_1)$. If $y_2\ne y_1$, dividing gives $i=\dfrac{x_1-x_2}{y_2-y_1}$, a real number. Squaring a real number never gives $-1$, so this is impossible. Hence $y_1=y_2$, and then $x_1=x_2$. $\blacksquare$

Special case: $x+iy=0\iff x=0$ and $y=0$. This is the rule behind every "find real $x$ and $y$" problem (Section 2.5D).

H.K. Dass §20.5, 20.6, 20.8, 20.10Equality, addition, subtraction and multiplication by algebra

Equal complex numbers (§20.5): Dass's proof

Claim. If $a+ib=c+id$ with $a,b,c,d$ real, then $a=c$ and $b=d$.

  1. Move terms across: $a-c=i(d-b)$.
  2. Square both sides: $(a-c)^2=i^2(d-b)^2=-(d-b)^2$, so $(a-c)^2+(d-b)^2=0$.
  3. A sum of two squares of real numbers is zero only if each square is zero. So $a=c$ and $b=d$. $\blacksquare$

This is why one complex equation always gives two real equations, and why you may "equate real and imaginary parts" (used in almost every Dass example from here on).

Addition and subtraction (§20.6, §20.8)

$$(a+ib)+(c+id)=(a+c)+i(b+d),\qquad(a+ib)-(c+id)=(a-c)+i(b-d).$$

Procedure: combine real parts with real parts and imaginary parts with imaginary parts, exactly as you collect like terms in algebra.

Multiplication (§20.10)

Multiply out as ordinary brackets, then replace $i^2$ by $-1$:

$$(a+ib)(c+id)=ac+iad+ibc+i^2bd=(ac-bd)+i(ad+bc).$$

Notice the minus sign in the real part. It comes from $i^2=-1$, and it is the most common slip in quick calculations.

Worked example (Dass §20.6 style) — add $z_1=\frac12+\frac23i$, $z_2=-2+\frac14i$, $z_3=\frac34-i$
  1. Real parts: $\frac12-2+\frac34=\frac{2-8+3}{4}=-\frac34$.
  2. Imaginary parts: $\frac23+\frac14-1=\frac{8+3-12}{12}=-\frac1{12}$.
  3. So $z_1+z_2+z_3=-\frac34-\frac1{12}i$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: with fractions, keep the real and imaginary bookkeeping separate. A sign slip in one part changes every option in an MCQ.

Worked example (Dass §20.10) — $(3+4i)(7-3i)$ and $(2-5i)(2+5i)$
  1. $(3+4i)(7-3i)=21-9i+28i-12i^2=21+12+19i=33+19i$.
  2. $(2-5i)(2+5i)=4+10i-10i-25i^2=4+25=29$: the imaginary parts cancel completely.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: the second product is $|z|^2$, which is real. This is the step that rationalises denominators in division (§20.13) and normalises wavefunctions in quantum mechanics.

Worked example (using §20.5) — find real $x,y$ with $(x+2y)+i(3x-y)=5+i$
  1. Equate real parts: $x+2y=5$. Equate imaginary parts: $3x-y=1$.
  2. From the second, $y=3x-1$. Substituting: $x+6x-2=5$, so $x=1$ and $y=2$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: "find real $x,y$ such that…" is a standard 3.5-mark question, and it is always two linear equations in disguise.

3. A complex number is a pair of real numbers

Boas: "we could, if we liked, agree to write $5+3i$ as $(5,3)$." Hamilton (1833) used this to define complex numbers without ever mentioning $\sqrt{-1}$:

Complex numbers as ordered pairs $$(a,b)+(c,d)=(a+c,\ b+d),\qquad (a,b)\cdot(c,d)=(ac-bd,\ ad+bc).$$

Where does the multiplication rule come from? Multiply $a+ib$ and $c+id$ by ordinary algebra:

$$(a+ib)(c+id)=ac+iad+ibc+i^2bd=(ac-bd)+i(ad+bc).$$

Now check what the rule says about $(0,1)$:

$$(0,1)\cdot(0,1)=(0\cdot0-1\cdot1,\ 0\cdot1+1\cdot0)=(-1,0).$$

So the pair $(0,1)$ squares to the pair $(-1,0)$, which plays the role of the real number $-1$. Call $(0,1)=i$, identify $(x,0)$ with the real number $x$, and you have $i^2=-1$ with nothing "imaginary" about it. Every pair is then $(x,y)=(x,0)+(y,0)(0,1)=x+iy$.

This pair view is the bridge to the next section: the pair $(x,y)$ is a point in a plane.

Properties that follow from the pair rule

  • $(1,0)$ is the multiplicative identity: $(1,0)(c,d)=(c,d)$.
  • Multiplication is commutative and associative, and distributes over addition (just check the formulas).
  • Inverse: $(a,b)^{-1}=\left(\dfrac{a}{a^2+b^2},\ \dfrac{-b}{a^2+b^2}\right)$ for $(a,b)\ne(0,0)$. Check: $(a,b)\cdot(a,-b)=(a^2+b^2,0)$.
Interesting: complex numbers as $2\times2$ matrices

Map $a+ib\ \longmapsto\ \begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\ b&a\end{pmatrix}$. Then complex addition and multiplication become matrix addition and multiplication:

$$\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\ b&a\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c&-d\\ d&c\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}ac-bd&-(ad+bc)\\ ad+bc&ac-bd\end{pmatrix}.$$

Under this map $i\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, which is the rotation by $90^\circ$ matrix, and its square is $-I$. The determinant $a^2+b^2$ is $|z|^2$ (Section 2.4). This is one more way to see why "multiplying by $i$ rotates by $90^\circ$".

4. Rules for Re and Im (what works, what does not)

Linear rules — these always work

$$\operatorname{Re}(z+w)=\operatorname{Re}z+\operatorname{Re}w,\qquad \operatorname{Im}(z+w)=\operatorname{Im}z+\operatorname{Im}w,$$ $$\operatorname{Re}(kz)=k\operatorname{Re}z,\quad \operatorname{Im}(kz)=k\operatorname{Im}z\qquad(k\ \text{real only!}).$$

Multiplying by $i$ swaps the parts

$iz=i(x+iy)=-y+ix$, so

$$\operatorname{Re}(iz)=-\operatorname{Im}z,\qquad \operatorname{Im}(iz)=\operatorname{Re}z .$$

Also $\operatorname{Im}z=\operatorname{Re}(-iz)$, which is useful later: the physical current $I_0\sin\omega t$ is $\operatorname{Im}(I_0e^{i\omega t})$ (Section 2.16).

Products — Re and Im are NOT multiplicative

With $z=x_1+iy_1$ and $w=x_2+iy_2$:

$$\operatorname{Re}(zw)=x_1x_2-y_1y_2,\qquad\operatorname{Im}(zw)=x_1y_2+x_2y_1 .$$
Trap

$\operatorname{Re}(zw)\ne\operatorname{Re}z\cdot\operatorname{Re}w$ in general. Example: $z=w=i$. Then $\operatorname{Re}(i\cdot i)=-1$ but $\operatorname{Re}i\cdot\operatorname{Re}i=0$.

Formulas with the conjugate (preview of 2.4)

With $\bar z=x-iy$:

$$\operatorname{Re}z=\frac{z+\bar z}{2},\qquad \operatorname{Im}z=\frac{z-\bar z}{2i}.$$

These let you compute Re and Im of an expression without expanding into $x$ and $y$. They are proved in Boas Problem 17.27(a).

Inequalities

$$|\operatorname{Re}z|\le|z|,\qquad|\operatorname{Im}z|\le|z|,\qquad|z|\le|\operatorname{Re}z|+|\operatorname{Im}z|\le\sqrt2\,|z| ,$$

where $|z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. The first two hold because $x^2\le x^2+y^2$. For the last one, square both sides: $(|x|+|y|)^2\le2(x^2+y^2)$ is the same as $(|x|-|y|)^2\ge0$.

6. Worked examples

Example 1 — $\operatorname{Re}$ and $\operatorname{Im}$ of a product: $(2+3i)(4-i)$
  1. Expand: $8-2i+12i-3i^2$.
  2. Use $i^2=-1$: $8+3+10i=11+10i$.
  3. So $\operatorname{Re}=11$ and $\operatorname{Im}=10$.
  4. Check with the pair rule: $(2,3)(4,-1)=(2\cdot4-3\cdot(-1),\ 2\cdot(-1)+3\cdot4)=(11,10)$ ✔.
Example 2 — A quotient: $\dfrac{1}{3+4i}$
  1. Multiply the top and bottom by $3-4i$: $\dfrac{3-4i}{(3+4i)(3-4i)}=\dfrac{3-4i}{9+16}$.
  2. $=\dfrac{3}{25}-\dfrac{4}{25}i$, so $\operatorname{Re}=\tfrac{3}{25}$ and $\operatorname{Im}=-\tfrac{4}{25}$.
Example 3 — In terms of $x,y$: $\operatorname{Re}$ and $\operatorname{Im}$ of $z^3$
  1. $(x+iy)^3=x^3+3x^2(iy)+3x(iy)^2+(iy)^3$.
  2. $=x^3+3ix^2y-3xy^2-iy^3$.
  3. $\operatorname{Re}z^3=x^3-3xy^2$ and $\operatorname{Im}z^3=3x^2y-y^3$.
  4. Test with $z=1+2i$: Re $=1-12=-11$, Im $=6-8=-2$. Directly, $(1+2i)^2=-3+4i$ and $(-3+4i)(1+2i)=-3-6i+4i-8=-11-2i$ ✔.
Example 4 — $\operatorname{Re}\dfrac{z-1}{z+1}$
  1. Multiply the top and bottom by $\overline{z+1}=\bar z+1$.
  2. Top: $(z-1)(\bar z+1)=z\bar z+z-\bar z-1=(x^2+y^2-1)+2iy$.
  3. Bottom: $(z+1)(\bar z+1)=|z+1|^2=(x+1)^2+y^2$ (real).
  4. So $\operatorname{Re}=\dfrac{x^2+y^2-1}{(x+1)^2+y^2}$ and $\operatorname{Im}=\dfrac{2y}{(x+1)^2+y^2}$.
  5. Consequence: $\operatorname{Re}\frac{z-1}{z+1}=0$ exactly on the unit circle $x^2+y^2=1$ (except $z=-1$).
Example 5 — Using the pair rule: find the inverse of $(3,4)$
  1. We need $(c,d)$ with $(3,4)(c,d)=(1,0)$: $3c-4d=1$ and $3d+4c=0$.
  2. From the second equation $d=-\tfrac43c$. Substituting: $3c+\tfrac{16}{3}c=1\Rightarrow c=\tfrac{3}{25}$, so $d=-\tfrac{4}{25}$.
  3. The inverse is $\left(\tfrac3{25},-\tfrac4{25}\right)$, the same as Example 2 ✔.
Example 6 — Re of a complex expression without $x,y$: $\operatorname{Re}\dfrac{1}{1-z}$ when $|z|=1$
  1. Use $\operatorname{Re}w=\tfrac12(w+\bar w)$ with $w=\dfrac1{1-z}$: $\ w+\bar w=\dfrac1{1-z}+\dfrac1{1-\bar z}=\dfrac{2-z-\bar z}{(1-z)(1-\bar z)}$.
  2. Denominator: $1-z-\bar z+z\bar z=2-z-\bar z$ because $z\bar z=|z|^2=1$.
  3. So $w+\bar w=1$ and $\operatorname{Re}\dfrac{1}{1-z}=\dfrac12$ for every $z\ne1$ on the unit circle. A surprising constant!

7. Practice problems

P1. Find Re and Im of $(5+2i)(1-3i)$. Easy
Show solution

$5-15i+2i-6i^2=11-13i$. So Re $=11$ and Im $=-13$.

P2. Find Re and Im of $\dfrac{2+i}{1-i}$. Easy
Show solution

$\dfrac{(2+i)(1+i)}{2}=\dfrac{2+2i+i-1}{2}=\dfrac12+\dfrac32 i$. So Re $=\tfrac12$ and Im $=\tfrac32$.

P3. Find Re and Im of $i^7+3i^2$ and of $\dfrac1{i^3}$. Easy
Show solution

$i^7=-i$, so $i^7+3i^2=-3-i$: Re $=-3$, Im $=-1$. Next, $1/i^3=1/(-i)=i$: Re $=0$, Im $=1$.

P4. Find Re and Im of $(1+i)^4$ and $(1+i)^5$. Easy
Show solution

$(1+i)^2=2i$, so $(1+i)^4=-4$ (Re $-4$, Im $0$) and $(1+i)^5=-4-4i$ (Re $-4$, Im $-4$).

P5. Express Re and Im of $z^2+2\bar z$ in terms of $x,y$. Easy
Show solution

$z^2=x^2-y^2+2ixy$ and $2\bar z=2x-2iy$. So Re $=x^2-y^2+2x$ and Im $=2xy-2y=2y(x-1)$.

P6. Prove $\operatorname{Re}(iz)=-\operatorname{Im}z$ and $\operatorname{Im}(iz)=\operatorname{Re}z$. Easy
Show solution

$iz=i(x+iy)=ix+i^2y=-y+ix$. Read off Re $=-y$ and Im $=x$.

P7. Give an example showing $\operatorname{Im}(zw)\ne\operatorname{Im}z\operatorname{Im}w$. Easy
Show solution

$z=w=1+i$: $zw=2i$, so $\operatorname{Im}(zw)=2$, but $\operatorname{Im}z\operatorname{Im}w=1$.

P8. Find real $x,y$ with $(x+iy)(2-3i)=4+i$. Medium
Show solution

$x+iy=\dfrac{4+i}{2-3i}=\dfrac{(4+i)(2+3i)}{13}=\dfrac{8+12i+2i-3}{13}=\dfrac{5+14i}{13}$. So $x=\tfrac5{13}$ and $y=\tfrac{14}{13}$.

Alternative (two real equations): $(2x+3y)+i(2y-3x)=4+i$ gives $2x+3y=4$ and $-3x+2y=1$, with the same answer.

P9. Solve $z+2\bar z=3-i$. Medium
Show solution

$x+iy+2x-2iy=3x-iy=3-i$. Equating parts: $3x=3$ and $-y=-1$, so $z=1+i$.

P10. For which real $a$ is $\dfrac{a+i}{1+i}$ (a) purely real, (b) purely imaginary? Medium
Show solution

$\dfrac{(a+i)(1-i)}{2}=\dfrac{(a+1)+(1-a)i}{2}$. (a) Purely real needs $1-a=0$, so $a=1$ (value $1$). (b) Purely imaginary needs $a+1=0$, so $a=-1$ (value $i$).

P11. Describe the set of $z$ with $\operatorname{Re}(z^2)=0$, and the set with $\operatorname{Im}(z^2)=0$. Medium
Show solution

$\operatorname{Re}z^2=x^2-y^2=0$ gives the two lines $y=\pm x$. $\operatorname{Im}z^2=2xy=0$ gives the two axes. (Compare Boas 5.63–64: $z^2=\bar z^2$ means $\operatorname{Im}z^2=0$, and $z^2=-\bar z^2$ means $\operatorname{Re}z^2=0$.)

P12. Using the pair rule, compute $(2,3)\cdot(1,-1)$ and $(0,1)^3$. Easy
Show solution

$(2\cdot1-3\cdot(-1),\ 2\cdot(-1)+3\cdot1)=(5,1)$. Next, $(0,1)^2=(-1,0)$ and $(-1,0)(0,1)=(0,-1)$, which is $-i$ ✔.

P13. Prove that the pair product is commutative and associative. Medium
Show solution

Commutative: $(c,d)(a,b)=(ca-db,\ cb+da)=(ac-bd,\ ad+bc)$. Associative: expand both $[(a,b)(c,d)](e,f)$ and $(a,b)[(c,d)(e,f)]$. Each gives $(ace-adf-bcf-bde,\ acf+ade+bce-bdf)$.

P14. Find Re and Im of $\dfrac{z-i}{z+i}$ and show that $\operatorname{Re}\frac{z-i}{z+i}\lt0$ exactly inside the unit circle. Medium
Show solution

Multiply by $\bar z-i$: the top is $z\bar z-iz-i\bar z+i^2=(x^2+y^2-1)-2ix$ and the bottom is $x^2+(y+1)^2$. Since the denominator is positive, Re has the sign of $x^2+y^2-1$, which is negative exactly when $|z|\lt1$.

P15. Show that for $|z|=1$, $z\neq1$: $\operatorname{Re}\dfrac{1}{1-z}=\dfrac12$. Also find $\operatorname{Im}$ when $z=e^{i\theta}$. Hard
Show solution

See Example 6. For Im: $1-e^{i\theta}=-2i\sin\frac\theta2\,e^{i\theta/2}$, so $\dfrac1{1-e^{i\theta}}=\dfrac{ie^{-i\theta/2}}{2\sin(\theta/2)}=\dfrac{i\cos\frac\theta2+\sin\frac\theta2}{2\sin\frac\theta2}=\dfrac12+\dfrac i2\cot\dfrac\theta2$. So $\operatorname{Im}=\tfrac12\cot\tfrac\theta2$. (This uses Euler's formula from Section 2.9.)

P16. Find Re and Im of $z^4$ for $z=2-i$, first using the table formula, then directly. Medium
Show solution

Table: Re $=x^4-6x^2y^2+y^4=16-24+1=-7$, Im $=4x^3y-4xy^3=4(8)(-1)-4(2)(-1)=-32+8=-24$. Directly: $(2-i)^2=3-4i$ and $(3-4i)^2=9-24i-16=-7-24i$ ✔.

P17. Prove $|\operatorname{Re}z|+|\operatorname{Im}z|\le\sqrt2|z|$. When is there equality? Hard
Show solution

$2(x^2+y^2)-(|x|+|y|)^2=(|x|-|y|)^2\ge0$. Equality holds when $|x|=|y|$, that is, on the lines $y=\pm x$.

P18. Show that the matrix map $a+ib\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{pmatrix}$ sends $z^{-1}$ to the inverse matrix. Hard
Show solution

The inverse of $M=\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{pmatrix}$ is $\dfrac1{a^2+b^2}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\-b&a\end{pmatrix}$. That is the image of $\dfrac{a-ib}{a^2+b^2}=z^{-1}$ ✔.

P19. If $z=x+iy$ and $\dfrac{z-1}{z+1}$ is purely imaginary, find the locus of $z$. Medium
Show solution

By Example 4, the real part is zero when $x^2+y^2=1$. The locus is the unit circle without $z=-1$ (where the expression is undefined) and without $z=1$ (where the value is $0$, which some books exclude as "not purely imaginary").

P20. Using $\operatorname{Re}z=\frac{z+\bar z}{2}$, show $\operatorname{Re}(z\bar w)=x_1x_2+y_1y_2$, the dot product of the vectors $z$ and $w$. Hard
Show solution

$z\bar w=(x_1+iy_1)(x_2-iy_2)=(x_1x_2+y_1y_2)+i(y_1x_2-x_1y_2)$. So $\operatorname{Re}(z\bar w)=\vec z\cdot\vec w$, and $\operatorname{Im}(z\bar w)=-(x_1y_2-y_1x_2)$ is minus the 2-D cross product. Useful fact: $z\perp w$ (as vectors) $\iff \operatorname{Re}(z\bar w)=0$.

9. Going deeper: $z$ and $\bar z$ as coordinates, and Re/Im as dot and cross products

Every function of $x,y$ is a function of $z$ and $\bar z$

From $z=x+iy$ and $\bar z=x-iy$ we can solve for $x$ and $y$:

$$x=\frac{z+\bar z}{2},\qquad y=\frac{z-\bar z}{2i}.$$

So any expression in $x,y$ can be rewritten in $z,\bar z$. For example $x^2+y^2=z\bar z$, $x^2-y^2=\frac{z^2+\bar z^2}{2}$ and $2xy=\frac{z^2-\bar z^2}{2i}$. This substitution sorts expressions into two kinds:

  • Functions of $z$ alone (no $\bar z$): $x^2-y^2+2ixy=z^2$, $\ e^x(\cos y+i\sin y)=e^z$. These turn out to be the analytic functions of Chapter 14.
  • Functions that need $\bar z$: $x^2+y^2=z\bar z$ and $x-iy=\bar z$. These are not analytic.

The test "does $\bar z$ appear?" is the Cauchy–Riemann condition in disguise. Formally $\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bar z}=\frac12\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}+i\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)=0$, and this is equivalent to $u_x=v_y$, $u_y=-v_x$ (see 14.2).

Re and Im of $\bar z_1z_2$: geometry in one product

Let $z_1=x_1+iy_1$ and $z_2=x_2+iy_2$. Then

$$\bar z_1z_2=(x_1x_2+y_1y_2)+i(x_1y_2-y_1x_2).$$
  • $\operatorname{Re}(\bar z_1z_2)=\vec z_1\cdot\vec z_2=|z_1||z_2|\cos\phi$, the dot product.
  • $\operatorname{Im}(\bar z_1z_2)=(\vec z_1\times\vec z_2)_z=|z_1||z_2|\sin\phi$, the cross product. Here $\phi$ is the angle from $z_1$ to $z_2$.

Consequences you can use straight away:

$$z_1\perp z_2\iff\operatorname{Re}(\bar z_1z_2)=0,\qquad z_1\parallel z_2\iff\operatorname{Im}(\bar z_1z_2)=0,\qquad\text{Area}(0,z_1,z_2)=\tfrac12\left|\operatorname{Im}(\bar z_1z_2)\right|.$$

Traps that cost marks
  • $\operatorname{Im}z$ is the real number $y$, not $iy$.
  • $\operatorname{Re}(zw)\ne\operatorname{Re}z\operatorname{Re}w$. The correct formula is $\operatorname{Re}(zw)=\operatorname{Re}z\operatorname{Re}w-\operatorname{Im}z\operatorname{Im}w$.
  • When you cross-multiply a "real" condition, remove points where the denominator is $0$ from the locus.
30-second shortcuts
  • $\operatorname{Re}\frac1z=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}$ and $\operatorname{Im}\frac1z=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}$. Memorise these two.
  • If $|z|=1$ then $\bar z=1/z$, so $z+\frac1z=2\operatorname{Re}z$ is real.

11. Exam-style problems (original, CSIR/GATE pattern)

X1. If $\dfrac{1+iz}{1-iz}$ is real, then $z$ lies on CSIR Part BMedium
(a) the real axis(b) the imaginary axis, except $z=-i$(c) the unit circle(d) the line $y=x$
Show solution

Real means $w=\bar w$: $\frac{1+iz}{1-iz}=\frac{1-i\bar z}{1+i\bar z}$. Cross-multiplying gives $1+iz+i\bar z-z\bar z=1-iz-i\bar z-z\bar z$, so $2i(z+\bar z)=0$, i.e. $\operatorname{Re}z=0$. The point $z=-i$ makes the denominator zero and must be removed. Option (b).

X2. Solve $|z|+z=2+i$ and give $\operatorname{Re}z$. GATE NATEasy
Show solution

The imaginary part gives $y=1$. The real part gives $\sqrt{x^2+1}+x=2$, so $x^2+1=(2-x)^2$ and $x=\frac34$. So $z=\frac34+i$ and $\operatorname{Re}z=\mathbf{0.75}$.

X3. Find the area of the triangle with vertices $0$, $3+i$ and $1+4i$. GATE NATEasy
Show solution

$\bar z_1z_2=(3-i)(1+4i)=7+11i$. The area is $\frac12\cdot11=\mathbf{5.5}$.

X4. Which of the following can be written as a function of $z=x+iy$ alone, with no $\bar z$? CSIR Part BMedium
(a) $x^2-y^2+2ixy$(b) $x^2+y^2$(c) $x-iy$(d) $x+2iy$
Show solution

(a) $=z^2$. (b) $=z\bar z$. (c) $=\bar z$. (d) $=\frac{z+\bar z}2+(z-\bar z)=\frac32z-\frac12\bar z$. Only (a) has no $\bar z$, and it is the only analytic one.

X5. (Multiple correct) For all complex $z,w$: CSIR Part CMedium
(a) $\operatorname{Re}(z+w)=\operatorname{Re}z+\operatorname{Re}w$(b) $\operatorname{Re}(zw)=\operatorname{Re}z\,\operatorname{Re}w$(c) $|\operatorname{Re}z|\le|z|$(d) $\operatorname{Im}(i\bar z)=\operatorname{Re}z$
Show solution

(a), (c), (d). For (d): $i\bar z=i(x-iy)=y+ix$, whose imaginary part is $x$. (b) fails for $z=w=i$, where the left side is $-1$ and the right side is $0$.

X6. How many complex numbers satisfy $\bar z=z^3$? CSIR Part CHard
Show solution

Taking moduli: $|z|=|z|^3$, so $|z|=0$ or $1$. $z=0$ works. If $|z|=1$ then $\bar z=1/z$, and the equation becomes $z^4=1$, which has 4 roots. Total: 5.

13. Summary

  • $z=x+iy$ with $\operatorname{Re}z=x$ and $\operatorname{Im}z=y$, both real.
  • Two complex numbers are equal ⇔ their real parts are equal and their imaginary parts are equal.
  • Pair form: $(a,b)(c,d)=(ac-bd,\ ad+bc)$, and $(0,1)^2=(-1,0)$.
  • Re and Im are linear (for real scalars) but not multiplicative.
  • $\operatorname{Re}z=\tfrac{z+\bar z}2$, $\operatorname{Im}z=\tfrac{z-\bar z}{2i}$, $\operatorname{Re}(iz)=-\operatorname{Im}z$.
  • To find Re and Im of an expression, write it in the form $x+iy$ first (expand; for quotients, multiply by the conjugate).
