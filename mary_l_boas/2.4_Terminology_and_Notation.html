2.4 Terminology and Notation

Boas, Chapter 2, Section 4 · modulus $|z|$, argument (angle, phase) and its many values, principal value, complex conjugate $\bar z$, and the rules connecting them.

Goals
  • Use the words modulus, absolute value, argument, phase, amplitude, principal value, conjugate correctly.
  • Find all values of the argument, and the principal value under either convention.
  • Use the properties of $|z|$ and $\bar z$ (including the triangle inequality) with proofs.
  • Solve all 20 of Boas's Section 4 problems: five labels plus the conjugate for each number.

1. The standard notation

$$z=x+iy=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)=re^{i\theta}\qquad(4.1)$$
SymbolNamesFormula
$x$real part, $\operatorname{Re}z$$r\cos\theta$

Engineers write $j$ instead of $i$, because $i$ means current. A physicist should be comfortable with both. As with a vector $\vec v$, we use a single letter $z$ even though it carries two real numbers.

2. The modulus $|z|$

Definition $$|z|=r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\qquad(\text{the positive square root})$$

Geometrically, $|z|$ is the distance from $0$ to $z$, and $|z_1-z_2|$ is the distance between the points $z_1$ and $z_2$ (Boas Problem 5.65).

Properties (with one-line reasons)

PropertyWhy
$|z|\ge0$, and $|z|=0\iff z=0$a sum of squares of reals
$|{-z}|=|\bar z|=|z|$only signs change, and squares remove them
$|z|^2=z\bar z$$(x+iy)(x-iy)=x^2+y^2$
$|z_1z_2|=|z_1||z_2|$$r_1e^{i\theta_1}r_2e^{i\theta_2}=r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}$
$\left|\dfrac{z_1}{z_2}\right|=\dfrac{|z_1|}{|z_2|}$same, dividing
$|z^n|=|z|^n$repeat the product rule

Trap

$|z_1+z_2|\ne|z_1|+|z_2|$ in general. Example: $|1+(-1)|=0$, but $|1|+|-1|=2$. Instead we have an inequality:

Triangle inequality $$|z_1+z_2|\le|z_1|+|z_2|,\qquad\big||z_1|-|z_2|\big|\le|z_1-z_2| .$$

Proof. $|z_1+z_2|^2=(z_1+z_2)(\bar z_1+\bar z_2)=|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2+z_1\bar z_2+\bar z_1z_2=|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(z_1\bar z_2)$.

Since $\operatorname{Re}(z_1\bar z_2)\le|z_1\bar z_2|=|z_1||z_2|$, we get $|z_1+z_2|^2\le(|z_1|+|z_2|)^2$. Take square roots.

For the second inequality, apply the first to $z_1=(z_1-z_2)+z_2$, which gives $|z_1|-|z_2|\le|z_1-z_2|$. Swapping the roles of $z_1$ and $z_2$ gives the other sign. $\blacksquare$

Equality holds iff $z_1\bar z_2$ is real and $\ge0$, i.e. $z_1$ and $z_2$ point in the same direction (or one of them is $0$).

Geometrically: one side of a triangle is never longer than the sum of the other two.

3. The argument: infinitely many values

Adding a full turn ($2\pi$) to $\theta$ gives the same point. So the angle of $z\ne0$ has infinitely many values:

$$\arg z=\theta_0+2n\pi,\qquad n=0,\pm1,\pm2,\dots$$

Boas's example (Fig. 4.1): $z=-1-i$ has $x=-1$, $y=-1$, $r=\sqrt2$, and

$$\theta=\frac{5\pi}4+2n\pi\qquad(4.3)$$ $$z=-1-i=\sqrt2\left[\cos\left(\tfrac{5\pi}{4}+2n\pi\right)+i\sin\left(\tfrac{5\pi}{4}+2n\pi\right)\right]=\sqrt2\left(\cos\tfrac{5\pi}4+i\sin\tfrac{5\pi}4\right)=\sqrt2\,e^{5\pi i/4}.$$

(Equivalently $\sqrt2(\cos225^\circ+i\sin225^\circ)$, or with $\theta=-3\pi/4$.)

Principal value — two conventions

ConventionRange$\arg(-1-i)$Where used
A$0\le\theta\lt2\pi$$5\pi/4$Boas (Section 13, principal $\ln$)
B$-\pi\lt\theta\le\pi$$-3\pi/4$most complex-analysis books, $\operatorname{Arg}z$, the atan2 function, most software
Not the same as the principal value of arctan

For $-1-i$, $\tan\theta=1$, and the calculus principal value is $\arctan1=\pi/4$. But $\pi/4$ is the angle of $1+i$, not $-1-i$. Boas: the angle of a complex number must be in the same quadrant as the point representing the number.

For most work in this chapter any value of $\theta$ will do. The choice matters for logarithms and complex powers (Sections 2.13 and 2.14), where different $\theta$'s give genuinely different answers.

Rules for the argument (valid "modulo $2\pi$")

$$\arg(z_1z_2)=\arg z_1+\arg z_2,\quad \arg\frac{z_1}{z_2}=\arg z_1-\arg z_2,\quad \arg\bar z=-\arg z,\quad\arg(-z)=\arg z+\pi,\quad \arg z^n=n\arg z .$$
Principal values can break these rules

Take $z_1=z_2=-1+i$ with $\operatorname{Arg}=3\pi/4$ (convention B). Then $z_1z_2=(-1+i)^2=-2i$, with $\operatorname{Arg}=-\pi/2$. But $3\pi/4+3\pi/4=3\pi/2$. The two differ by $2\pi$. The rule holds for the set of all values, but for principal values you may need to add or subtract $2\pi$ to get back into the range.

H.K. Dass §20.15Modulus and argument: the principal value in every quadrant

Put $x=r\cos\theta$ and $y=r\sin\theta$. The positive root $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=|x+iy|$ is the modulus, and $\theta$, the argument (amplitude), satisfies $\cos\theta=\frac{x}{r}$ and $\sin\theta=\frac{y}{r}$. Because $\theta$ and $\theta+2k\pi$ give the same point, the argument has infinitely many values. The one in $-\pi\lt\theta\le\pi$ is the principal value. Dass writes it "between $0$ and $\pi$" for points above the real axis and "between $0$ and $-\pi$" for points below.

Let $\alpha=\tan^{-1}\left|\frac yx\right|$ be the acute reference angle. All the cases are:

Worked example (Dass §20.15) — modulus and argument of $1+\cos\alpha+i\sin\alpha$, $\ 0\lt\alpha\lt\pi$
  1. Half-angle forms: $1+\cos\alpha=2\cos^2\frac\alpha2$ and $\sin\alpha=2\sin\frac\alpha2\cos\frac\alpha2$.
  2. So $1+\cos\alpha+i\sin\alpha=2\cos\frac\alpha2\left(\cos\frac\alpha2+i\sin\frac\alpha2\right)$.
  3. $\cos\frac\alpha2\gt0$, so this is already polar: modulus $2\cos\frac\alpha2$, argument $\frac\alpha2$.
  4. Companion case: $1-\cos\alpha+i\sin\alpha=2\sin\frac\alpha2\left(\sin\frac\alpha2+i\cos\frac\alpha2\right)$, with modulus $2\sin\frac\alpha2$ and argument $\frac{\pi-\alpha}{2}$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: $1\pm e^{i\alpha}$ appears in every two-slit, thin-film and Fabry–Pérot amplitude. Its modulus gives the intensity and its argument the phase.

Worked example (Dass §20.15) — if $|z_1+z_2|=|z_1-z_2|$, then $\arg z_1-\arg z_2=\pm\frac\pi2$
  1. Square both sides with $z_k=x_k+iy_k$: $(x_1+x_2)^2+(y_1+y_2)^2=(x_1-x_2)^2+(y_1-y_2)^2$, so $x_1x_2+y_1y_2=0$.
  2. $\tan(\arg z_1-\arg z_2)=\dfrac{\frac{y_1}{x_1}-\frac{y_2}{x_2}}{1+\frac{y_1y_2}{x_1x_2}}=\dfrac{x_2y_1-x_1y_2}{x_1x_2+y_1y_2}$, and the denominator is $0$, so the tangent is infinite.
  3. Hence $\arg z_1-\arg z_2=\pm\frac\pi2$ (Dass writes $\frac\pi2$; the sign depends on which arrow is ahead). Geometrically, a parallelogram with equal diagonals is a rectangle.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: equal moduli of sum and difference is the quickest test that two phasors or vectors are perpendicular.

Worked example (Dass §20.15) — find $z$ with $\arg(z+2)=\frac\pi4$ and $\arg(z-2)=\frac{3\pi}4$
  1. With $z=x+iy$: $\frac{y}{x+2}=\tan\frac\pi4=1$, so $y=x+2$.
  2. $\frac{y}{x-2}=\tan\frac{3\pi}4=-1$, so $y=2-x$.
  3. Solving gives $x=0$, $y=2$, so $z=2i$. Check: $2+2i$ has argument $45^\circ$ and $-2+2i$ has $135^\circ$. ✓

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: each condition is a ray, and the answer is where two rays meet. Always check the quadrant, not just the tangent.

Worked example (Dass §20.15) — the triangle inequality, by algebra
  1. $|z_1+z_2|^2=(x_1+x_2)^2+(y_1+y_2)^2=|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2+2(x_1x_2+y_1y_2)$.
  2. Since $(x_1y_2-x_2y_1)^2\ge0$, we have $x_1x_2+y_1y_2\le\sqrt{(x_1^2+y_1^2)(x_2^2+y_2^2)}=|z_1||z_2|$ (Cauchy–Schwarz).
  3. So $|z_1+z_2|^2\le(|z_1|+|z_2|)^2$, i.e. $|z_1+z_2|\le|z_1|+|z_2|$. Writing $z_1=(z_1-z_2)+z_2$ then gives $|z_1-z_2|\ge|z_1|-|z_2|$.
  4. By geometry (Dass's other proof): in triangle $OPR$, $OR\lt OP+PR$, with equality only when $O,P,R$ are collinear.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: max/min of $|z-a|$ questions, and the ML estimate for contour integrals, are both the triangle inequality.

4. The complex conjugate

Definition

The conjugate of $z=x+iy$ is $\bar z=x-iy$ (also written $z^*$). Change the sign of the $i$ term only. The conjugate of $7i-5$ is $-7i-5$, not $5-7i$.

In polar form (4.4): $\bar z=r[\cos(-\theta)+i\sin(-\theta)]=r(\cos\theta-i\sin\theta)=re^{-i\theta}$.

Geometrically, $z$ and $\bar z$ are mirror images in the real axis (Boas Fig. 4.2). They have the same $r$ and opposite angles.

H.K. Dass §20.12–20.13Conjugate pairs and division, in Cartesian and polar form

Conjugates (§20.12)

Two complex numbers that differ only in the sign of the imaginary part, $a+ib$ and $a-ib$, are conjugate to each other. The conjugate of $z$ is written $\bar z$.

Theorem

The sum and the product of a complex number and its conjugate are both real.

Proof. $(x+iy)+(x-iy)=2x$, which is real. $(x+iy)(x-iy)=x^2-i^2y^2=x^2+y^2$, which is real. $\blacksquare$

The converse is also true (Dass Example 26, §20.18): if $z_1+z_2$ and $z_1z_2$ are both real, then $z_1,z_2$ are either both real or a conjugate pair. They are the two roots of the real quadratic $t^2-(z_1+z_2)t+z_1z_2=0$.

Division (§20.13): two methods

Cartesian method. Multiply the top and bottom by the conjugate of the denominator:

$$\frac{a+ib}{c+id}=\frac{(a+ib)(c-id)}{(c+id)(c-id)}=\frac{ac+bd}{c^2+d^2}+i\,\frac{bc-ad}{c^2+d^2}.$$

Polar method. With $z_1=r_1(\cos\theta_1+i\sin\theta_1)$ and $z_2=r_2(\cos\theta_2+i\sin\theta_2)$, multiply the top and bottom by $\cos\theta_2-i\sin\theta_2$. The bottom becomes $r_2(\cos^2\theta_2+\sin^2\theta_2)=r_2$, and the top becomes the subtraction formulas:

$$\frac{z_1}{z_2}=\frac{r_1}{r_2}\left[\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)+i\sin(\theta_1-\theta_2)\right].$$

The modulus of a quotient is the quotient of the moduli, and its argument is the difference of the arguments.

Interactive — $z$, $\bar z$, and why the conjugate makes division work
Drag $z$. $\bar z$ is its mirror image in the real axis. Both $z+\bar z$ and $z\bar z$ land on the real axis, which is the theorem above. The point $\frac1z=\frac{\bar z}{|z|^2}$ points the same way as $\bar z$, with length $\frac1{|z|}$.
Worked example — $\dfrac{4+3i}{2-i}$
  1. $\frac{(4+3i)(2+i)}{(2-i)(2+i)}=\frac{8+4i+6i+3i^2}{4+1}=\frac{5+10i}{5}=1+2i$.
  2. Polar check: $|4+3i|/|2-i|=5/\sqrt5=\sqrt5=|1+2i|$. ✓

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: rationalising with the conjugate is the step that turns an impedance $V/Z$ or a transmission amplitude into real and imaginary parts you can read off.

Worked example (Dass §20.13) — when is $\dfrac{3+2i\sin\theta}{1-2i\sin\theta}$ purely imaginary?
  1. Multiply by the conjugate $1+2i\sin\theta$: the result is $\dfrac{(3-4\sin^2\theta)+8i\sin\theta}{1+4\sin^2\theta}$.
  2. Purely imaginary means the real part is $0$ while the imaginary part is not: $3-4\sin^2\theta=0$ and $\sin\theta\ne0$.
  3. $\sin^2\theta=\frac34$, so $\theta=n\pi\pm\frac\pi3$, $n\in\mathbb Z$. ($\theta=\frac\pi3$ is the smallest positive solution.)

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: "for which parameter is this expression real or purely imaginary?" becomes "set the other part to zero" once the denominator is real.

Worked example (Dass §20.13) — if $z=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$, then $\dfrac{2}{1+z}=1-i\tan\frac\theta2$ and $\dfrac{1+z}{1-z}=i\cot\frac\theta2$
  1. Half-angle factoring: $1+z=e^{i\theta/2}\left(e^{-i\theta/2}+e^{i\theta/2}\right)=2\cos\frac\theta2\,e^{i\theta/2}$ and $1-z=-2i\sin\frac\theta2\,e^{i\theta/2}$.
  2. $\frac2{1+z}=\frac{e^{-i\theta/2}}{\cos(\theta/2)}=\frac{\cos\frac\theta2-i\sin\frac\theta2}{\cos\frac\theta2}=1-i\tan\frac\theta2$.
  3. $\frac{1+z}{1-z}=\frac{2\cos(\theta/2)}{-2i\sin(\theta/2)}=i\cot\frac\theta2$.
  4. (Dass gets the same results by rationalising with $(1+\cos\theta)-i\sin\theta$ and then using the half-angle formulas.)

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: $\frac{1+z}{1-z}$ with $|z|=1$ is the Möbius map that sends the unit circle onto the imaginary axis (Ch. 14). The same algebra appears in Fabry–Pérot and transmission-line problems.

Worked example (Dass §20.13) — if $x=e^{i\theta}$ and $y=e^{i\phi}$, show $\dfrac{x-y}{x+y}=i\tan\dfrac{\theta-\phi}2$
  1. Factor out the average angle: $x\mp y=e^{i(\theta+\phi)/2}\left(e^{i(\theta-\phi)/2}\mp e^{-i(\theta-\phi)/2}\right)$.
  2. The brackets are $2i\sin\frac{\theta-\phi}2$ and $2\cos\frac{\theta-\phi}2$, and the common factor cancels. So the ratio is $i\tan\frac{\theta-\phi}{2}$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: factoring out the average phase is the standard trick in two-beam interference and beat problems.

6. Worked examples

Example 1 — All arguments of $-\sqrt3-i$
  1. The point is in quadrant III, $r=2$, and the reference angle is $\arctan\frac1{\sqrt3}=\frac\pi6$.
  2. $\theta=\pi+\frac\pi6=\frac{7\pi}6$, so all values are $\frac{7\pi}6+2n\pi$.
  3. Principal value: convention A gives $7\pi/6$, and convention B gives $7\pi/6-2\pi=-5\pi/6$.
Example 2 — Modulus without multiplying out: $\left|\dfrac{(3+4i)(1-i)}{2+i}\right|$
  1. Use $|z_1z_2/z_3|=|z_1||z_2|/|z_3|$.
  2. $=\dfrac{5\cdot\sqrt2}{\sqrt5}=\sqrt{10}$.
Example 3 — Argument of a product: $\arg[(1+i)(\sqrt3+i)]$
  1. $\arg(1+i)=\pi/4$ and $\arg(\sqrt3+i)=\pi/6$.
  2. Sum: $\pi/4+\pi/6=5\pi/12$ $(75^\circ)$.
  3. Check: $(1+i)(\sqrt3+i)=(\sqrt3-1)+i(\sqrt3+1)$, and $\arctan\frac{\sqrt3+1}{\sqrt3-1}=\arctan(2+\sqrt3)=75^\circ$ ✔. (This also proves $\tan75^\circ=2+\sqrt3$!)
Example 4 — Solve $\bar z=z^2$
  1. Take moduli: $|z|=|z|^2$, so $|z|=0$ or $|z|=1$.
  2. $|z|=0$ gives $z=0$.
  3. If $|z|=1$, then $\bar z=1/z$, so $1/z=z^2$, i.e. $z^3=1$.
  4. Solutions: $z=0,\ 1,\ -\tfrac12\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}2i$.
Example 5 — Range of $|z|$ on a circle

If $|z-3-4i|=2$, find the largest and smallest values of $|z|$.

  1. $z$ lies on the circle with centre $3+4i$ and radius 2. The centre is at distance $5$ from $0$.
  2. By the triangle inequality, $5-2\le|z|\le5+2$.
  3. Minimum $3$ at $z=\tfrac35(3+4i)=1.8+2.4i$, and maximum $7$ at $z=\tfrac75(3+4i)=4.2+5.6i$.

P4. Show that $\operatorname{Arg}(z_1z_2)=\operatorname{Arg}z_1+\operatorname{Arg}z_2$ fails for $z_1=z_2=-1$ (convention B), and say what the correct relation is. Medium
Show solution

$\operatorname{Arg}(-1)=\pi$, so the sum is $2\pi$. But $(-1)(-1)=1$ has $\operatorname{Arg}=0$. Correct relation: $\operatorname{Arg}(z_1z_2)=\operatorname{Arg}z_1+\operatorname{Arg}z_2+2k\pi$ with $k\in\{-1,0,1\}$ chosen to land in $(-\pi,\pi]$. Here $k=-1$.

P5. Prove the parallelogram law $|z_1+z_2|^2+|z_1-z_2|^2=2(|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2)$. Medium
Show solution

From the proof of the triangle inequality, $|z_1\pm z_2|^2=|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2\pm2\operatorname{Re}(z_1\bar z_2)$. Adding the two cancels the cross terms. Geometrically: the sum of the squares of the diagonals of a parallelogram equals the sum of the squares of its four sides.

P6. If $|z_1+z_2|=|z_1|+|z_2|$ with $z_1,z_2\ne0$, show $\arg z_1=\arg z_2$. Medium
Show solution

Equality forces $\operatorname{Re}(z_1\bar z_2)=|z_1\bar z_2|$, so $z_1\bar z_2$ is real and positive. Its argument $\theta_1-\theta_2$ is then $0\pmod{2\pi}$.

P7. Prove that $|z|=1\iff\bar z=1/z$, and use it to show $\left|\dfrac{z-a}{1-\bar az}\right|=1$ when $|z|=1$ (and $\bar a z\ne1$). Hard
Show solution

$z\bar z=|z|^2$, so $\bar z=1/z\iff|z|^2=1$. If $|z|=1$: $|z-a|=|\bar z||z-a|=|z\bar z-a\bar z|=|1-a\bar z|=|\overline{1-\bar a z}|=|1-\bar az|$. So the ratio has modulus 1. (These Möbius maps send the unit circle to itself.)

P8. Find the maximum of $|z+2-i|$ when $|z|=3$. Medium
Show solution

$|z+2-i|\le|z|+|2-i|=3+\sqrt5$. Equality holds when $z$ points along $2-i$: $z=\frac{3}{\sqrt5}(2-i)$. So the maximum is $3+\sqrt5$.

P9. Show that $z^2+\bar z^2$ is real and $z^2-\bar z^2$ is purely imaginary. Easy
Show solution

Let $w=z^2$, so $\bar w=\bar z^2$. Then $w+\bar w=2\operatorname{Re}w$ (real) and $w-\bar w=2i\operatorname{Im}w$ (imaginary).

P10. If $z=re^{i\theta}$, find the modulus and argument of $\dfrac{z}{\bar z}$ and of $z\bar z$. Easy
Show solution

$z/\bar z=e^{2i\theta}$ has modulus 1 and argument $2\theta$. $z\bar z=r^2$ has modulus $r^2$ and argument 0.

P11. (NET style) If $|z-1|=|z+1|$, then $z$ lies on: Easy
Show solution

Points equidistant from $1$ and $-1$ lie on the perpendicular bisector, which is the imaginary axis: $\operatorname{Re}z=0$. Algebraically, $(x-1)^2+y^2=(x+1)^2+y^2$ gives $x=0$.

P12. Show that for any $z_1,z_2$: $|1-\bar z_1z_2|^2-|z_1-z_2|^2=(1-|z_1|^2)(1-|z_2|^2)$. Challenge
Show solution

Left side: $(1-\bar z_1z_2)(1-z_1\bar z_2)-(z_1-z_2)(\bar z_1-\bar z_2)$. Expanding: $1-z_1\bar z_2-\bar z_1z_2+|z_1|^2|z_2|^2-|z_1|^2+z_1\bar z_2+\bar z_1z_2-|z_2|^2$. The cross terms cancel, leaving $1-|z_1|^2-|z_2|^2+|z_1|^2|z_2|^2=(1-|z_1|^2)(1-|z_2|^2)$. $\blacksquare$

9. Going deeper: the triangle inequality from one identity

Almost every modulus inequality follows from

$$|z_1+z_2|^2=(z_1+z_2)(\bar z_1+\bar z_2)=|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(z_1\bar z_2).\tag{$\ast$}$$
  1. Triangle inequality. Since $\operatorname{Re}w\le|w|$, we have $\operatorname{Re}(z_1\bar z_2)\le|z_1||z_2|$. So $|z_1+z_2|^2\le(|z_1|+|z_2|)^2$. Equality holds only when $z_1\bar z_2$ is real and $\ge0$, i.e. the two arrows point the same way.
  2. Reverse triangle inequality. Apply the same bound to $z_1=(z_1+z_2)+(-z_2)$ to get $\big||z_1|-|z_2|\big|\le|z_1+z_2|$.
  3. Parallelogram law. Add $(\ast)$ to the same identity with $z_2\to-z_2$. The cross terms cancel and you get $|z_1+z_2|^2+|z_1-z_2|^2=2|z_1|^2+2|z_2|^2$.
  4. Perpendicularity. $|z_1+z_2|=|z_1-z_2|\iff\operatorname{Re}(z_1\bar z_2)=0\iff z_1\perp z_2$. A parallelogram has equal diagonals exactly when it is a rectangle.

Extreme distances: the "line through the centre" rule

If $z$ moves on the circle $|z-c|=r$, its distance to a fixed point $a$ satisfies

$$\big||a-c|-r\big|\le|z-a|\le|a-c|+r .$$

Both bounds are reached on the line through $a$ and $c$. This one fact answers most max/min questions about the modulus.

Why $\arg$ rules hold only "mod $2\pi$"

$\arg(z_1z_2)=\arg z_1+\arg z_2$ is an identity between sets of angles, because each $\arg$ is defined up to $2\pi k$. For principal values the sum can leave $(-\pi,\pi]$. Then $\operatorname{Arg}(z_1z_2)=\operatorname{Arg}z_1+\operatorname{Arg}z_2-2\pi k$, with $k\in\{-1,0,1\}$ chosen to bring the result back into range.

X2. If $|z_1|=|z_2|=1$ and $z_1z_2\ne-1$, then $\dfrac{z_1+z_2}{1+z_1z_2}$ is CSIR Part BMedium

13. Summary

  • $z=x+iy=re^{i\theta}$, $\ |z|=r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=\sqrt{z\bar z}$, $\ \arg z=\theta+2n\pi$.
  • Principal value: $[0,2\pi)$ (Boas) or $(-\pi,\pi]$ (most texts). The point's quadrant decides $\theta$.
  • $|z_1z_2|=|z_1||z_2|$, and $\arg(z_1z_2)=\arg z_1+\arg z_2$ (mod $2\pi$).
  • $\big||z_1|-|z_2|\big|\le|z_1\pm z_2|\le|z_1|+|z_2|$.
  • $\bar z=x-iy=re^{-i\theta}$ is the mirror image in the real axis, and $1/z=\bar z/|z|^2$.
