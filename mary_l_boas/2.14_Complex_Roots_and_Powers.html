2.14 Complex Roots and Powers

Boas, Chapter 2, Section 14 · $a^b=e^{b\ln a}$ for complex $a$ and $b$, why powers are usually multivalued, how many values there are in each case, and famous results like $i^i=e^{-\pi/2}$.

Goals
  • Evaluate any $a^b$ (all values and the principal value) in $x+iy$ form.
  • Predict how many values $a^b$ has: 1, $q$, or infinitely many, and where they lie in the plane.
  • Know which power laws fail (e.g. $(a^b)^c\ne a^{bc}$) and why.
  • Solve all of Boas's Section 14 problems (8–25; problems 1–7 are on the 2.13 page).

1. Definition

For real positive $a$, $\ln a^b=b\ln a$ is the same as $a^b=e^{b\ln a}$. We use this to define complex powers:

$$\boxed{a^b=e^{b\ln a}}\qquad(a\ne e)\qquad(14.1)$$

The case $a=e$ is excluded because $e^z$ was already defined by its power series (single-valued). Since $\ln a$ has infinitely many values, $a^b$ is usually multivalued. Unless you want only the principal value, use all values of $\theta$.

2. How many values? The factor $e^{2\pi inb}$

Write $\ln a=\operatorname{Ln}a+2n\pi i$, where $\operatorname{Ln}a$ is the principal value. Then

$$a^b=e^{b\operatorname{Ln}a}\cdot e^{2\pi i\,nb},\qquad n=0,\pm1,\pm2,\dots$$

Everything depends on the factor $e^{2\pi inb}$:

Exponent $b$$e^{2\pi inb}$ as $n$ variesNumber of values of $a^b$Where they lie
integeralways $1$1a single point (ordinary power)
rational $p/q$ (lowest terms)the $q$-th roots of unity$q$a regular $q$-gon on a circle (like Section 2.10)
irrational realnever repeatsinfinitely manyall on one circle $|w|=|a|^b$, filling it densely

3. Boas's examples

Example 1 — all values of $i^{-2i}$
  1. $\ln i=\operatorname{Ln}1+i\left(\frac\pi2\pm2n\pi\right)=i\left(\frac\pi2\pm2n\pi\right)$.
  2. $i^{-2i}=e^{-2i\ln i}=e^{-2i\cdot i(\pi/2\pm2n\pi)}=e^{\pi\pm4n\pi}=e^\pi,\ e^{5\pi},\ e^{-3\pi},\dots$
  3. $e^\pi=23.14\ldots$ So there are infinitely many values, all real.
  4. Boas's warning: here $\pi$ is not used as an angle at the end (we never take sin or cos of it), so it must be in radians. Writing $\ln i=90^\circ i$ would be nonsense.
Example 2 — all values of $i^{1/2}$
  1. $i^{1/2}=e^{\frac12\ln i}=e^{i(\pi/4+n\pi)}=e^{i\pi/4}e^{in\pi}$.
  2. $e^{in\pi}=+1$ for even $n$ and $-1$ for odd $n$.
  3. $i^{1/2}=\pm e^{i\pi/4}=\pm\frac{1+i}{\sqrt2}$. Although $\ln i$ has infinitely many values, $i^{1/2}$ has exactly two, as a square root should. (The method of Section 2.10 is easier here.)
Example 3 — all values of $(1+i)^{1-i}$
  1. $\ln(1+i)=\operatorname{Ln}\sqrt2+i\left(\frac\pi4\pm2n\pi\right)$ (Section 2.13).
  2. $(1+i)^{1-i}=e^{(1-i)[\operatorname{Ln}\sqrt2+i(\pi/4\pm2n\pi)]}=e^{\operatorname{Ln}\sqrt2}\,e^{-i\operatorname{Ln}\sqrt2}\,e^{i\pi/4}\,e^{\pm2n\pi i}\,e^{\pi/4}\,e^{\pm2n\pi}$.
  3. $=\sqrt2\,e^{\pi/4}e^{\pm2n\pi}\left[\cos\left(\frac\pi4-\operatorname{Ln}\sqrt2\right)+i\sin\left(\frac\pi4-\operatorname{Ln}\sqrt2\right)\right]$ (using $e^{\pm2n\pi i}=1$).
  4. $=e^{\pm2n\pi}(2.808+1.318i)$: infinitely many values, all on the ray through $2.808+1.318i$. Why a ray and not a spiral: the extra factor is $e^{2\pi inb}=e^{2\pi in(1-i)}=e^{2\pi in}e^{2\pi n}=e^{2\pi n}$, a positive real number, because $\operatorname{Re}b=1$ is an integer.
The famous one: $i^i$

$$i^i=e^{i\ln i}=e^{i\cdot i(\pi/2+2n\pi)}=e^{-\pi/2-2n\pi}.$$ The principal value is $e^{-\pi/2}=0.20788\ldots$: an imaginary number raised to an imaginary power gives real numbers. Euler found this in 1746.

H.K. Dass §20.17, 20.28–20.29 (examples)Complex powers through the general logarithm

H.K. Dass handles every complex power with one rule, using the general logarithm of §20.29:

$$a^b=e^{b\operatorname{Log}a}=e^{b(\log a+2n\pi i)} .$$

Taking $n=0$ gives the principal value. Keeping $n$ general gives all the values.

Interactive — all values of $a^b$
Drag $a$, and set $b=p+iq$ with the sliders. The orange points are $e^{b(\log a+2n\pi i)}$ for $n=-3,\dots,3$, and the ringed one is the principal value. With $q=0$ the points lie on a circle. With $q\ne0$ they spread out along a spiral. The view is scaled so that all the points fit.
Worked example (Dass §20.17) — if $i^{\alpha+i\beta}=\alpha+i\beta$, then $\alpha^2+\beta^2=e^{-(4n+1)\pi\beta}$
  1. $\operatorname{Log}i=i\left(2n\pi+\frac\pi2\right)=\frac{(4n+1)\pi i}{2}$.
  2. $i^{\alpha+i\beta}=e^{(\alpha+i\beta)(4n+1)\pi i/2}=e^{-(4n+1)\pi\beta/2}\,e^{i(4n+1)\pi\alpha/2}$.
  3. The modulus squared of the right side is $e^{-(4n+1)\pi\beta}$, and it must equal $|\alpha+i\beta|^2=\alpha^2+\beta^2$. $\blacksquare$

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: taking the modulus of $e^{(\cdot)}$ needs only the real part of the exponent. That is a fast way to answer "$|z^w|=$?" questions.

Worked example (Dass §20.28) — real and imaginary parts of $\left(\sqrt i\right)^{\sqrt i}$ (principal value)
  1. $\sqrt i=e^{i\pi/4}=\frac{1+i}{\sqrt2}$, so $\log\sqrt i=\frac{i\pi}4$.
  2. $\left(\sqrt i\right)^{\sqrt i}=e^{\frac{1+i}{\sqrt2}\cdot\frac{i\pi}4}=e^{-\frac{\pi}{4\sqrt2}}\,e^{\frac{i\pi}{4\sqrt2}}$.
  3. Real part $e^{-\pi/4\sqrt2}\cos\frac{\pi}{4\sqrt2}\approx0.488$, imaginary part $e^{-\pi/4\sqrt2}\sin\frac{\pi}{4\sqrt2}\approx0.303$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: nested powers like $i^{\sqrt i}$ look intimidating but need only polar form and $a^b=e^{b\log a}$.

Worked example (Dass §20.29) — general and principal values of $i^i$, and $i^{1+i}$
  1. $i^i=e^{i\cdot(4n+1)\pi i/2}=e^{-(4n+1)\pi/2}$. All values are real. The principal value is $e^{-\pi/2}\approx0.208$.
  2. $i^{1+i}=i\cdot i^i=i\,e^{-(4n+1)\pi/2}$. All values are purely imaginary, with principal value $ie^{-\pi/2}$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: "$i^i$ is real" is one of the most frequently asked one-liners. Remember it has infinitely many real values.

4. Which laws of exponents survive?

LawStatus for complex powers
$a^{b}a^{c}=a^{b+c}$✔ if the same value of $\ln a$ is used in all three
$(a^b)^c=a^{bc}$✘ in general: the left side can have more values (Boas Problem 24)
$(ab)^c=a^cb^c$✔ as sets of all values; ✘ for principal values
$a^{-b}=1/a^b$✔ (same branch)
Classic traps
  • The principal value of $(-8)^{1/3}$ is $1+i\sqrt3$, not $-2$! $\operatorname{Ln}(-8)=\operatorname{Ln}8+i\pi$, so the principal power is $2e^{i\pi/3}$. The real cube root $-2$ is one of the three values ($n=1$). Calculators and software usually return the principal value.
  • $1^{\sqrt2}$ has infinitely many values $e^{2\pi i n\sqrt2}$, all on the unit circle. Only $n=0$ gives $1$.
  • $\left(e^{2\pi i}\right)^{i}=1^i$, whose values are $e^{-2n\pi}$. But $e^{2\pi i\cdot i}=e^{-2\pi}$ is just one of them. So "$1=e^{2\pi i}$, raise both sides to the power $i$" gives $1=e^{-2\pi}$ only if you mix up branches.

Show solution 24 — $(a^b)^c$ can have more values than $a^{bc}$

(b) $i^i=e^{-(\pi/2+2n\pi)}$, which is real and positive. Its logarithms are $-(\frac\pi2+2n\pi)+2m\pi i$. So

$$(i^i)^i=e^{i[-(\frac\pi2+2n\pi)+2m\pi i]}=e^{-2m\pi}e^{-i(\frac\pi2+2n\pi)}=-i\,e^{-2m\pi}:$$

infinitely many values. But $i^{i\cdot i}=i^{-1}=-i$ has just one.

(a) $(-i)^{2+i}=e^{(2+i)\,i(-\frac\pi2+2n\pi)}=e^{\frac\pi2-2n\pi}e^{-i\pi}=-e^{c}$ with $c=\frac\pi2-2n\pi$. Raising to the power $2-i$ (with $\ln(-e^c)=c+i(\pi+2m\pi)$):

$$e^{(2-i)[c+i(\pi+2m\pi)]}=e^{2c+\pi+2m\pi}\,e^{-ic}\,e^{2i(\pi+2m\pi)}=-i\,e^{2\pi k}\quad(k\in\mathbb Z),$$

whereas $(-i)^{(2+i)(2-i)}=(-i)^5=-i$. The left side has infinitely many values ($-ie^{2\pi k}$), and the right side has one.

Show solution 25 — roots of $x^3-3x-1=0$

Try $x=2\cos\theta$: $8\cos^3\theta-6\cos\theta=2(4\cos^3\theta-3\cos\theta)=2\cos3\theta$. So the equation becomes $2\cos3\theta=1$, i.e. $\cos3\theta=\frac12$, so $3\theta=\pm\frac\pi3+2k\pi$.

$\theta=\frac\pi9,\ \frac{5\pi}9,\ \frac{7\pi}9$ give $x=2\cos\frac\pi9=1.879$, $\ 2\cos\frac{5\pi}9=-2\cos\frac{4\pi}9=-0.347$, $\ 2\cos\frac{7\pi}9=-2\cos\frac{2\pi}9=-1.532$.

A computer may give these as complicated expressions involving complex cube roots (the casus irreducibilis, Section 2.1), even though all three roots are real.

6. More practice

P1. Find all values of $8^{2/3}$ and the principal value of $(-27)^{1/3}$. Easy
Show solution

$8^{2/3}=e^{\frac23(\operatorname{Ln}8+2n\pi i)}=4e^{4n\pi i/3}$: the values $4,\ 4\omega,\ 4\omega^2=-2\pm2\sqrt3i$. Principal $(-27)^{1/3}=3e^{i\pi/3}=\frac32+\frac{3\sqrt3}2i$.

P2. Show that all values of $i^i$ are real, and find the largest one that is less than 1. Medium
Show solution

$i^i=e^{-\pi/2-2n\pi}$, all real and positive. For $n=0$ it is $0.208$. For $n=-1$ it is $e^{3\pi/2}>1$. So the largest value below 1 is $e^{-\pi/2}\approx0.208$.

P3. Find $|\,i^{\,1+i}|$ for all values. Medium
Show solution

$i^{1+i}=e^{(1+i)i(\pi/2+2n\pi)}=e^{-(\pi/2+2n\pi)}\cdot e^{i(\pi/2+2n\pi)}=ie^{-\pi/2-2n\pi}$. The moduli are $e^{-\pi/2-2n\pi}$.

P4. How many distinct values do $(1+i)^{3/4}$, $2^{\pi}$, $(-1)^{0.4}$ have? Medium
Show solution

4 ($q=4$). Infinitely many ($\pi$ is irrational): they lie on the circle of radius $2^\pi$ (the principal one is the real $8.825$). And $0.4=\frac25$, so 5 values.

P5. Find the principal value of $(1+i)^i$ and its modulus. Medium
Show solution

$e^{i(\operatorname{Ln}\sqrt2+i\pi/4)}=e^{-\pi/4}e^{i\operatorname{Ln}\sqrt2}=0.4559(\cos0.3466+i\sin0.3466)=0.4288+0.1549i$. Modulus $e^{-\pi/4}=0.456$.

P6. Solve $z^i=1$. Hard
Show solution

$e^{i\ln z}=1$ means $i\ln z=2k\pi i$, so $\ln z=2k\pi$ and $z=e^{2k\pi}$. (Using all branches of $\ln z$ gives the same set.) So $z=1,\ e^{\pm2\pi},\ e^{\pm4\pi},\dots$, all positive reals.

P7. (NET) The principal value of $i^{\,i}\cdot i^{-i}$ is: Easy
Show solution

With the same branch: $e^{i\operatorname{Ln}i}e^{-i\operatorname{Ln}i}=e^0=1$. (In general $a^ba^{-b}=1$ when the same $\ln a$ is used.)

8. Going deeper: counting the values of $a^b$, and choosing the physical branch

How many values? A complete classification

With $a^b=e^{b\ln a}=e^{b(\operatorname{Ln}a+2\pi ik)}=a_{\text{pr}}^b\cdot e^{2\pi ikb}$, the values differ by the factors $e^{2\pi ikb}$, $k\in\mathbb Z$. Two values coincide exactly when $e^{2\pi i(k-k')b}=1$, i.e. when $(k-k')b$ is an integer. So:

Exponent $b$Number of distinct values of $a^b$Example
integer1$i^4=1$
rational $p/q$ in lowest terms$q$$8^{2/6}=8^{1/3}$: 3 values (reduce the fraction first)
irrational realinfinitely many, all with modulus $|a|^b$, dense on a circle$2^{\sqrt2}$
non-realinfinitely many, with different moduli$i^i=e^{-\pi/2-2\pi k}$

For a non-real exponent, $|e^{2\pi ikb}|=e^{-2\pi k\operatorname{Im}b}$. That is why the values of $i^i$ form a geometric sequence on the positive real axis, instead of points on a circle.

The power function is analytic on a cut plane

Fix the principal branch $z^c=e^{c\operatorname{Ln}z}$ on $\mathbb C\setminus(-\infty,0]$. The chain rule gives

$$\frac{d}{dz}z^c=e^{c\operatorname{Ln}z}\cdot\frac cz=c\,z^{c-1}\qquad(\text{same branch on both sides}).$$

Physics: picking the branch of $\sqrt{\ }$

In quantum mechanics $\psi\sim e^{ikx}$ with $k=\sqrt{2m(E-V)}/\hbar$. When $E\lt V$ the square root is imaginary, and both signs $\pm i\kappa$ are mathematically valid. Physics chooses: for $x\to+\infty$ we need $\operatorname{Im}k\gt0$, so that $e^{ikx}=e^{-\kappa x}$ decays. Branch choices like this are a matter of the boundary conditions. This is the same reasoning as choosing which way to close a contour in 14.7.

12. Summary

  • $a^b=e^{b\ln a}=e^{b\operatorname{Ln}a}e^{2\pi inb}$.
  • $b$ integer gives 1 value; $b=p/q$ gives $q$ values; irrational $b$ gives infinitely many on a circle; complex $b$ gives infinitely many on a spiral or ray.
  • $i^i=e^{-\pi/2-2n\pi}$ is real. The principal $(-8)^{1/3}$ is $1+i\sqrt3$.
  • $(a^b)^c$ may have more values than $a^{bc}$. Always say which branch you use.
