14.5 The Residue Theorem

Boas, Chapter 14, Section 5 · why only the $1/(z-z_0)$ term "survives" integration, the residue theorem $\oint_Cf\,dz=2\pi i\sum\text{residues inside }C$, and how it contains Cauchy's integral formula as a special case.

Goals
  • State, prove and picture the residue theorem.
  • Evaluate closed contour integrals by finding the poles inside $C$ and adding their residues.
  • Handle contours that wind around a point more than once, or clockwise (winding numbers).
  • Solve Boas Problems 5.1–5.3 (the theory behind Laurent coefficients and Cauchy's formula).

1. One singular point inside $C$

Let $z_0$ be an isolated singular point of $f$, and let $C$ be a simple closed curve around $z_0$ enclosing no other singularity. Expand $f$ in its Laurent series near $z_0$:

$$f(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_n(z-z_0)^n+\frac{b_1}{z-z_0}+\frac{b_2}{(z-z_0)^2}+\cdots$$
  • The $a$-series is analytic, so its integral is $0$ (Cauchy's theorem).
  • Shrink $C$ to a small circle $z=z_0+\rho e^{i\theta}$ (allowed by the deformation principle). Then (5.1):
$$\oint\frac{b_1\,dz}{z-z_0}=b_1\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{i\rho e^{i\theta}}{\rho e^{i\theta}}d\theta=2\pi i\,b_1,\qquad\oint\frac{b_n\,dz}{(z-z_0)^n}=0\ \ (n\ge2)\quad\text{(Problem 1)}.$$

Only the $b_1$ term survives the integration, which is why $b_1$ is called the residue (what is left over):

$$\oint_Cf(z)\,dz=2\pi i\cdot(\text{residue of }f\text{ at the singular point inside }C).$$

2. Several singular points: the residue theorem

If $C$ encloses isolated singular points $z_0,z_1,z_2,\dots$, draw a small circle around each (Boas Fig. 5.1) and connect each one to $C$ by a cut. $f$ is analytic in the region between them, so (as in Section 3) the integral around $C$ equals the sum of the integrals around the little circles. Each one gives $2\pi i\times$(its residue):

The residue theorem (5.2) $$\boxed{\oint_Cf(z)\,dz=2\pi i\cdot\sum\big(\text{residues of }f\text{ at the singular points inside }C\big)}$$

$C$ is traversed counter-clockwise (the enclosed area is on the left). $f$ must be analytic on $C$ and inside $C$ except at finitely many isolated singular points.

Generalisation: winding numbers

If a closed curve goes around a point $a$ several times, or clockwise, then

$$\oint_Cf\,dz=2\pi i\sum_kn(C,z_k)\,\operatorname{Res}(f,z_k),\qquad n(C,a)=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{dz}{z-a}=\frac{\text{total change of }\arg(z-a)}{2\pi}.$$

$n=+1$ for counter-clockwise once around, $-1$ for clockwise, $0$ for outside, $2$ for twice around, and so on.

H.K. Dass §26.12The residue theorem: Dass's proof and examples (Ex 28–37, Exercise 26.3)

Residue theorem (Dass)

If $f$ is analytic inside and on a closed curve $C$ except at finitely many poles $a_1,\dots,a_n$ inside $C$, then $\oint_Cf\,dz=2\pi i\left[\operatorname{Res}f(a_1)+\cdots+\operatorname{Res}f(a_n)\right]$.

  1. Draw small non-overlapping circles $C_1,\dots,C_n$ about the poles, all inside $C$. $f$ is analytic in the region between $C$ and these circles.
  2. By Cauchy's theorem for multiply connected regions (Dass §24.4): $\oint_C=\oint_{C_1}+\cdots+\oint_{C_n}$.
  3. By the definition of the residue, $\oint_{C_k}f\,dz=2\pi i\operatorname{Res}f(a_k)$. ∎ (The same proof works for isolated essential singularities.)
Worked example (Dass Ex 28, 29, 30) — which poles are inside?

Ex 28: $\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{1+z}{z(2-z)}dz$. Only $z=0$ is inside, with residue $\frac12$. Answer: $\pi i$.

Ex 29: $\oint_{|z|=3/2}\frac{4-3z}{z(z-1)(z-2)}dz$: residues $2$ (at $0$) and $-1$ (at $1$). Answer: $2\pi i$.

Ex 30: $\oint\frac{12z-7}{(z-1)^2(2z+3)}dz$. The residue at the double pole $1$ is $\frac{d}{dz}\frac{12z-7}{2z+3}\big|_1=\frac{50}{25}=2$. At $-\frac32$ it is $\frac{12z-7}{2(z-1)^2}\big|_{-3/2}=-2$.

  • (i) $|z|=2$ encloses both: $2\pi i(2-2)=0$.
  • (ii) $|z+i|=3$: the distance from $-i$ to $1$ is $\sqrt2\lt3$ (inside), and to $-\frac32$ it is $\frac{\sqrt{13}}{2}\approx1.80\lt3$. Both are inside, so the answer is again $0$.

(The printed solution says the second distance is greater than $3$, keeps only $z=1$, and gets $4\pi i$. But $\left|-\frac32+i\right|=\sqrt{\frac94+1}=\frac{\sqrt{13}}2\approx1.80\lt3$, so $-\frac32$ is inside as well and the integral is $0$. A numerical contour integral confirms $0$.)

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: most wrong answers in residue MCQs come from the "inside or outside" step, not from the residues. Compute every distance $|a_k-\text{centre}|$ and compare it with the radius explicitly.

Worked example (Dass Ex 32, Ex 33) — a parameter, and transcendental poles

Ex 32: $\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z|=3}\frac{e^{zt}\,dz}{z^2(z^2+2z+2)}$. Poles: $0$ (double) and $-1\pm i$, all inside.

  • $\operatorname{Res}_0=\frac{d}{dz}\frac{e^{zt}}{z^2+2z+2}\Big|_0=\frac{2t-2}{4}=\frac{t-1}{2}$.
  • $\operatorname{Res}_{-1\pm i}=\frac{e^{(-1\pm i)t}}{(-1\pm i)^2(\pm2i)}=\frac{e^{(-1\pm i)t}}{4}$.
  • Answer: $\frac{t-1}{2}+\frac{e^{-t}}{2}\cos t$. This is the inverse Laplace transform of $\frac{1}{s^2(s^2+2s+2)}$ (see the Bromwich integral in 14.7).

Ex 33: $\oint_{|z|=4}\frac{dz}{\sinh z}$. The poles are $z=n\pi i$; inside $|z|=4$ are $0$ and $\pm\pi i$. The residues are $\frac{1}{\cosh(n\pi i)}=\frac1{\cos n\pi}$, i.e. $1,-1,-1$. Answer: $2\pi i(-1)=-2\pi i$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: Ex 32 is exactly how inverse Laplace transforms are evaluated by residues. The $e^{zt}$ factor is the Bromwich kernel.

3. Cauchy's integral formula as a special case (Problem 3)

If $f$ is analytic inside $C$ and $a$ is inside, then $\frac{f(z)}{z-a}$ has a simple pole at $a$ with residue $\lim_{z\to a}(z-a)\frac{f(z)}{z-a}=f(a)$. The residue theorem gives

$$\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{z-a}dz=2\pi if(a),$$

which is Cauchy's formula (3.9). Similarly $\frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}$ has residue $\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}$ at $a$, giving the derivative formula. So the residue theorem contains everything in Section 3.

4. How to evaluate $\oint_Cf\,dz$ by residues (checklist)

  1. Find all singular points of $f$ (zeros of denominators, points where $\frac1{z-a}$ appears inside $e$, $\sin$, …).
  2. Decide which lie inside $C$ (draw it!). Points on $C$ need special care (Section 14.7).
  3. Find the residue at each inside point (Section 14.6 gives fast methods).
  4. Multiply the sum by $2\pi i$ (with winding numbers if $C$ is not simple and counter-clockwise).

5. Worked examples

Example 1 — $\oint_{|z|=2}\dfrac{z\,dz}{z^2+1}$
  1. Poles at $\pm i$, both inside $|z|=2$.
  2. Residues: at $i$, $\frac{z}{z+i}\big|_{i}=\frac12$; at $-i$, $\frac{z}{z-i}\big|_{-i}=\frac12$.
  3. Integral $=2\pi i\left(\frac12+\frac12\right)=2\pi i$.
Example 2 — $\oint_{|z|=1}\dfrac{e^z\,dz}{z(z-2)}$
  1. Poles at $0$ (inside) and $2$ (outside).
  2. Residue at $0$: $\frac{e^z}{z-2}\big|_0=-\frac12$.
  3. Integral $=2\pi i\left(-\frac12\right)=-\pi i$.
Example 3 — $\oint_{|z|=2}\tan z\,dz$
  1. Poles where $\cos z=0$: $\pm\frac\pi2$ are inside ($1.57\lt2$), $\pm\frac{3\pi}2$ are outside.
  2. Residue at $\frac\pi2$: $\frac{\sin z}{(\cos z)'}=\frac{\sin z}{-\sin z}=-1$, and the same at $-\frac\pi2$.
  3. Integral $=2\pi i(-2)=-4\pi i$.
Example 4 — an essential singularity: $\oint_{|z|=1}e^{1/z}dz$

$e^{1/z}=1+\frac1z+\frac1{2z^2}+\cdots$ has residue $1$, so the integral is $2\pi i$. No Cauchy formula applies, but the residue theorem still works.

Example 5 — residues that cancel: $\oint_{|z|=3}\dfrac{dz}{z^2(z-1)}$
  1. At $0$ (double pole): $\frac{d}{dz}\frac1{z-1}\big|_0=-\frac{1}{(z-1)^2}\big|_0=-1$.
  2. At $1$: $\frac1{z^2}\big|_1=1$.
  3. The sum is $0$, so the integral is $0$. Check: on a big circle $|f|\sim\frac1{R^3}$, so the integral $\to0$ (ML), and it is independent of $R$ beyond $1$.

6. Boas Problems, Section 5 — solutions

1. If $C$ is a circle of radius $\rho$ about $z_0$, show $\oint_C\frac{dz}{(z-z_0)^n}=2\pi i$ if $n=1$, and $0$ for every other integer $n$.
Show solution

$z=z_0+\rho e^{i\theta}$, $dz=i\rho e^{i\theta}d\theta$: $\ \int_0^{2\pi}\rho^{-n}e^{-in\theta}i\rho e^{i\theta}d\theta=i\rho^{1-n}\int_0^{2\pi}e^{i(1-n)\theta}d\theta$. For $n=1$ this is $2\pi i$. For $n\ne1$ it is $i\rho^{1-n}\left[\frac{e^{i(1-n)\theta}}{i(1-n)}\right]_0^{2\pi}=0$.

2. Verify the coefficient formulas (4.3).
Show solution

Divide the Laurent series (4.1) by $(z-z_0)^{n+1}$ and integrate term by term around $C$ (allowed, by uniform convergence on $C$). By Problem 1, the only term that survives is the one with total power $-1$, namely $a_n(z-z_0)^n/(z-z_0)^{n+1}$, which gives $2\pi ia_n$. Hence $a_n=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f\,dz}{(z-z_0)^{n+1}}$. For $b_n$, multiply by $(z-z_0)^{n-1}$: the surviving term is $b_n(z-z_0)^{-n}(z-z_0)^{n-1}=\frac{b_n}{z-z_0}$, giving $b_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint f\,(z-z_0)^{n-1}dz$.

3. Obtain Cauchy's integral formula (3.9) from the residue theorem (5.2).
Show solution

See Section 3: $\frac{f(z)}{z-a}$ has residue $f(a)$ at $a$ (a simple pole if $f(a)\ne0$, otherwise a removable singularity with residue $0=f(a)$), and no other singularities inside $C$. So $\oint\frac{f\,dz}{z-a}=2\pi if(a)$.

7. Extra practice (exam level)

P1. $\oint_{|z|=1}\sin\frac1z\,dz$ and $\oint_{|z|=1}\cot z\,dz$. Easy
Show solution

$\sin\frac1z=\frac1z-\frac1{6z^3}+\cdots$ has residue 1, so the first is $2\pi i$. $\cot z=\frac1z-\frac z3-\cdots$ has residue 1 at $0$ (the only pole inside, since $\pm\pi$ are outside), so the second is $2\pi i$.

P2. $\oint_{|z|=4}\dfrac{z\,dz}{e^z-1}$. Medium
Show solution

$z=0$ is removable ($\frac{z}{e^z-1}\to1$). The poles $2\pi ik$, $k\ne0$, have $|2\pi k|\ge6.28>4$, so they are outside. The integral is $0$.

P3. $\oint_C\dfrac{dz}{z^2-1}$ where $C$ is the figure-eight that goes counter-clockwise around $1$ and clockwise around $-1$. Hard
Show solution

Residues: $\frac12$ at $1$ and $-\frac12$ at $-1$. Winding numbers $+1$ and $-1$. So the integral is $2\pi i\left[(1)\frac12+(-1)\left(-\frac12\right)\right]=2\pi i$. (Try it in the lab with the figure-eight button.)

P4. Show that the sum of the residues of a rational function $P/Q$ with $\deg Q\ge\deg P+2$ at all its poles is $0$. Hard
Show solution

Take $C$ a circle of radius $R$ enclosing all poles. By ML, $|\oint|\le2\pi R\cdot\frac{c}{R^2}\to0$. But the integral equals $2\pi i\sum\text{Res}$ for every such $R$, so the sum is 0. (See also residues at infinity, 14.8.)

P5. (NET) $\oint_{|z|=1}\dfrac{dz}{\sin z}$ equals: Easy
Show solution

Only $z=0$ is inside, with residue $\frac1{\cos0}=1$. Answer: $2\pi i$.

9. Going deeper: counting zeros with residues — $\oint f'/f$ and Rouché

The logarithmic derivative

Suppose $f$ has a zero of order $m$ at $a$: $f=(z-a)^mg$ with $g(a)\ne0$. Then

$$\frac{f'}{f}=\frac{m}{z-a}+\frac{g'}{g},$$

so $\frac{f'}{f}$ has a simple pole at $a$ with residue $m$. A pole of order $k$ gives residue $-k$ in the same way. Summing over everything inside $C$:

$$\boxed{\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}dz=Z-P}\qquad\text{(zeros minus poles, counted with multiplicity).}$$

Weighting with an analytic $h$ gives more: $\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_Ch\frac{f'}{f}dz=\sum_{\text{zeros}}h(a_j)-\sum_{\text{poles}}h(b_j)$. With $h=z$ this is the sum of the zeros inside $C$.

Rouché's theorem: count zeros without computing anything

Rouché

If $f$ and $g$ are analytic inside and on a simple closed curve $C$, and $|g|\lt|f|$ on $C$, then $f$ and $f+g$ have the same number of zeros inside $C$.

Why: on $C$, $\frac{f+g}{f}=1+\frac gf$ stays inside the disk $|w-1|\lt1$, so it never winds around $0$. Its winding number is $0$. Hence $\oint\frac{(f+g)'}{f+g}=\oint\frac{f'}{f}$.

Recipe: on the circle, pick the term of the polynomial with the largest modulus as $f$. Everything else is $g$. Then the zero count is the power of that dominant term.

Example — zeros of $z^6-5z^2+1$ in $|z|\lt1$
  1. On $|z|=1$: $|{-5z^2}|=5$ and $|z^6+1|\le2$.
  2. $f=-5z^2$ dominates and has 2 zeros (both at $0$).
  3. So $z^6-5z^2+1$ has 2 zeros in the unit disk. On $|z|=2$, $z^6$ dominates ($64\gt21$), so all 6 zeros lie in $|z|\lt2$, and 4 of them are in the ring $1\lt|z|\lt2$.

13. Summary

  • Only $\frac{b_1}{z-z_0}$ survives a closed integral around $z_0$: $\oint f\,dz=2\pi ib_1$.
  • Residue theorem: $\oint_Cf\,dz=2\pi i\sum$ (residues inside $C$), counter-clockwise. More generally, weight each residue by its winding number.
  • Cauchy's formula and its derivative form are special cases.
  • Method: find the singular points, keep the ones inside, find their residues, multiply by $2\pi i$.
