14.1 Introduction — Functions of a Complex Variable

Boas, Chapter 14, Section 1 · a function of $z$ is a pair of real functions $u(x,y)$ and $v(x,y)$; how to find them, how to "see" a complex function, and what single-valued branches of $\sqrt z$, $\ln z$, $\arctan z$ are.

Goals
  • Write any $f(z)$ as $u(x,y)+iv(x,y)$, quickly and correctly (Boas Problems 1–21 are all solved below).
  • Picture a complex function four ways: domain colouring, grid mapping, $|f|$ landscape, and the $u$, $v$ surfaces.
  • Understand why functions must be single valued, and what a branch, branch cut and branch point are.
  • See the plan of the chapter: derivatives (14.2) → integrals (14.3) → series (14.4) → residues (14.5–14.8) → mapping (14.9–14.10).

1. What this chapter is about

Chapter 2 treated complex numbers: arithmetic, $e^z$, logs, powers. Chapter 14 treats the calculus of functions of $z$: derivatives, integrals and power series. The payoff is large:

  • Real definite integrals that are very hard by real methods, such as $\int_0^\infty\frac{\cos x}{1+x^2}dx=\frac{\pi}{2e}$, become a few lines of algebra (residue theorem, 14.7).
  • Every analytic function gives two solutions of Laplace's equation, so 2-D problems in heat, electrostatics and fluid flow can be solved by mapping (14.10).
  • It explains puzzles about real functions, e.g. why the real series of $\frac1{1+x^2}$ converges only for $|x|\lt1$ (14.2).
  • It gives inverse Laplace transforms (Bromwich integral), dispersion relations (Kramers–Kronig), and counting of zeros for stability (argument principle).

2. A function of $z$ is two real functions

The value of $f(z)$ is a complex number, so

$$f(z)=f(x+iy)=u(x,y)+i\,v(x,y),\qquad(1.1)$$

where $u=\operatorname{Re}f$ and $v=\operatorname{Im}f$ are real functions of the real variables $x,y$.

Boas example — $f(z)=z^2$
$$z^2=(x+iy)^2=x^2-y^2+2ixy\quad\Rightarrow\quad u=x^2-y^2,\qquad v=2xy .$$

H.K. Dass §22.1–22.7Neighbourhoods, limits and continuity of $f(z)$

Complex variable and function (§22.2–22.3). The complex variable $z$ can be written in three forms: $x+iy$ (Cartesian), $r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$ (polar) or $re^{i\theta}$ (exponential). A function $w=f(z)=u+iv$ assigns to each $z$ a value $w$, and $u$, $v$ are its real and imaginary parts.

Limit (§22.5)

$\displaystyle\lim_{z\to z_0}f(z)=l$ means: for every $\epsilon\gt0$ there is a $\delta\gt0$ such that $|f(z)-l|\lt\epsilon$ whenever $0\lt|z-z_0|\lt\delta$. In pictures, every point of the deleted $\delta$-disk in the $z$-plane is sent into the $\epsilon$-disk around $l$ in the $w$-plane.

The key difference from real limits

In the plane, $z$ can approach $z_0$ along infinitely many paths: straight lines from every direction, spirals, parabolas, and so on. The limit exists only if every path gives the same value. To show that a limit does not exist, find two paths that disagree.

Continuity (§22.6–22.7)

$f$ is continuous at $z_0$ if $\lim_{z\to z_0}f(z)=f(z_0)$. That requires three things: $f(z_0)$ is defined, the limit exists, and the two are equal. Because $|f-l|$ is small exactly when both $|u-\operatorname{Re}l|$ and $|v-\operatorname{Im}l|$ are small:

§22.7

$f=u+iv$ is continuous at $z_0=x_0+iy_0$ if and only if $u(x,y)$ and $v(x,y)$ are both continuous at $(x_0,y_0)$.

Interactive — approach $0$ along different paths
Pick a function and a family of paths ($y=mx$, or the curves $y=kx^2$ and $y=kx^3$). Change the slider to change the path. The point slides in towards $0$, and the readout shows the value $f$ approaches along that path. If the value changes as you change the path, the limit does not exist.
Worked example (Dass §22.5) — $\displaystyle\lim_{z\to1-i}\frac{z^2+4z+3}{z+1}$
  1. Factor: $\frac{(z+1)(z+3)}{z+1}=z+3$ for $z\ne-1$.
  2. The limit is $(1-i)+3=4-i$. The function is a polynomial after cancelling, so every path gives the same answer.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: removable "$\frac00$" forms are the first step in classifying singularities. If the factor cancels, the singularity is removable.

Worked example (Dass §22.5) — $\displaystyle\lim_{z\to0}\frac{z}{|z|}$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{z\to0}\frac{z}{\bar z}$ do not exist
  1. Along $y=mx$ ($x\gt0$): $\frac{x+imx}{|x|\sqrt{1+m^2}}=\frac{1+im}{\sqrt{1+m^2}}$, which depends on $m$. So $\frac{z}{|z|}$ has no limit.
  2. For $\frac{z}{\bar z}$: along the real axis ($y=0$ first) it is $\frac xx=1$, and along the imaginary axis ($x=0$ first) it is $\frac{iy}{-iy}=-1$. The two values differ, so there is no limit.
  3. In polar form, $\frac z{\bar z}=e^{2i\theta}$: it depends only on the direction of approach.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: any function that depends only on the direction $\theta$ (and not on $r$) has no limit at $0$ unless it is constant. This one-line test settles most "does the limit exist?" MCQs.

Worked example (Dass §22.6–22.7) — continuity of $f(z)=\dfrac{z^3-iz^2+z-i}{z-i}$ ($z\ne i$), $f(i)=0$
  1. $z^3-iz^2+z-i=z^2(z-i)+(z-i)=(z-i)(z^2+1)$.
  2. For $z\ne i$, $f(z)=z^2+1\to i^2+1=0=f(i)$. So $f$ is continuous at $i$.
  3. In contrast, $f=\frac{\operatorname{Re}z}{z}$ ($f(0)=0$) is not continuous at $0$: along $y=0$ it equals $1$, and along $x=0$ it equals $0$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: continuity is necessary for differentiability (§22.8), so a discontinuous function can be eliminated immediately in an "analytic at $z_0$?" question.

Worked example (Dass §22.5) — a limit at infinity: $\displaystyle\lim_{z\to\infty}\frac{iz^3+iz-1}{(2z+3i)(z-i)^2}$
  1. Divide the top and bottom by $z^3$: $\frac{i+i/z^2-1/z^3}{(2+3i/z)(1-i/z)^2}$.
  2. As $z\to\infty$, the result is $\frac{i}{2}$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: leading-coefficient ratios at $\infty$ give the residue at infinity and the behaviour of big contour arcs in Ch. 14.7–14.8.

4. Functions must be single-valued: branches

A function gives one value for each $z$. But from Chapter 2,

$$\ln z=\ln|z|+i(\theta+2n\pi)$$

has infinitely many values. To use a formula like $\ln z$, $\sqrt z$ or $\arctan z$ as a function, we allow $\theta$ a range of only $2\pi$ (for example $0\le\theta\lt2\pi$ or $-\pi\lt\theta\le\pi$). This single-valued function is called a branch of $\ln z$. The whole collection of branches is sometimes called a "multiple-valued function".

Definitions (used throughout the chapter)
  • Branch: one single-valued choice from a multivalued formula, e.g. $\sqrt z=\sqrt r\,e^{i\theta/2}$ with $0\le\theta\lt2\pi$.
  • Branch cut: the line we agree not to cross (here the positive real axis). The branch jumps across it.
  • Branch point: a point you cannot circle (on an arbitrarily small circle) without crossing the cut and changing branch. For $\sqrt z$ and $\ln z$ it is $z=0$ (and also $\infty$).

Going once around the origin, $\theta\to\theta+2\pi$: $\sqrt z\to\sqrt r\,e^{i(\theta+2\pi)/2}=-\sqrt z$ (the other branch), and $\ln z\to\ln z+2\pi i$ (the next branch). The cut can be placed along any ray from $0$; only the branch point is fixed by the function.

Interactive 3 — Move the branch cut
Domain colouring of one branch of the chosen function, with $\theta$ restricted to $(\alpha,\alpha+2\pi]$. The colour jump marks the cut. Rotate $\alpha$: the jump moves, but the branch point at $0$ stays. Away from the cut the function is perfectly smooth (analytic).

6. Extra practice

P1. Find $u,v$ for $f(z)=z^2+2iz$ and for $f(z)=\frac{1}{z-i}$. Easy
Show solution

$z^2+2iz=(x^2-y^2-2y)+i(2xy+2x)$. And $\frac{1}{x+i(y-1)}=\frac{x-i(y-1)}{x^2+(y-1)^2}$, so $u=\frac{x}{x^2+(y-1)^2}$ and $v=\frac{1-y}{x^2+(y-1)^2}$.

P2. Find $u,v$ for $\tan z$. Medium
Show solution

$\tan z=\dfrac{\sin2x+i\sinh2y}{\cos2x+\cosh2y}$ (Chapter 2, §11). So $u=\frac{\sin2x}{\cos2x+\cosh2y}$ and $v=\frac{\sinh2y}{\cos2x+\cosh2y}$.

P3. If $u=e^{x}\cos y$ and $v=e^x\sin y$, which $f(z)$ is it? And for $u=x^2-y^2+x$, $v=2xy+y$? Medium
Show solution

$e^z$ and $z^2+z$. A quick trick: put $y=0$ to get $f(x)=u(x,0)+iv(x,0)$, then replace $x$ by $z$ (valid for analytic $f$, Section 14.2).

P4. Using the principal branch of $\sqrt z$ ($-\pi\lt\theta\le\pi$, cut along the negative real axis), find $\sqrt{-4}$ and the limiting values of $\sqrt z$ as $z\to-4$ from above and from below. Medium
Show solution

With $-\pi\lt\theta\le\pi$, $\sqrt{-4}=2e^{i\pi/2}=2i$ (on the cut, by convention). Approaching from below ($\theta\to-\pi$) gives $2e^{-i\pi/2}=-2i$. The jump across the cut is $4i$.

P5. Where are the branch points of $\sqrt{z^2-1}$ and of $\ln(z-i)$? Medium
Show solution

$\sqrt{z^2-1}=\sqrt{(z-1)(z+1)}$ has branch points at $\pm1$. Going around both together changes the sign twice, which means no change, so a cut can join $-1$ to $1$. $\ln(z-i)$ has a branch point at $z=i$ (and at $\infty$).

7. Going deeper: finding all branch points, including $\infty$

A point $a$ is a branch point of $f$ if going once around a small circle about $a$ does not bring $f$ back to its starting value. For products of powers there is a simple bookkeeping rule.

The exponent-sum rule

Let $f(z)=\prod_k(z-a_k)^{\alpha_k}$. Going once around $a_k$ alone multiplies $f$ by $e^{2\pi i\alpha_k}$. So:

  • $a_k$ is a branch point $\iff\alpha_k$ is not an integer.
  • A large circle goes around all the $a_k$ and multiplies $f$ by $e^{2\pi i\sum\alpha_k}$. So $\infty$ is a branch point $\iff\sum_k\alpha_k$ is not an integer.

Why a segment cut works for $\sqrt{z^2-1}$: a loop that encloses both $+1$ and $-1$ picks up $e^{i\pi}e^{i\pi}=1$. Such loops do not cross $[-1,1]$, and they are harmless. Only loops around one branch point must be blocked, and every such loop crosses the segment.

Choosing the branch by its behaviour at infinity

With the cut $[-1,1]$, $\sqrt{z^2-1}=z\sqrt{1-1/z^2}$. For large $|z|$ there are exactly two branches, $\approx+z$ and $\approx-z$. Fixing "$\approx+z$" pins down the value everywhere off the cut. For example, at $z=i$ it gives $+i\sqrt2$ (follow the positive imaginary axis inward from $\infty$).
