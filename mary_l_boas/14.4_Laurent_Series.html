14.4 Laurent Series

Boas, Chapter 14, Section 4 · series with negative powers, convergence in rings (annuli), one series per ring, the principal part, and the classification of singular points: regular, pole of order $n$, essential singularity. The residue $b_1$ makes its first appearance.

Goals
  • Find the Laurent series of a function in each ring between its singular points (partial fractions plus geometric series).
  • Read off the residue $b_1$, using the series that converges near the point.
  • Classify any isolated singular point (removable, pole of order $n$, essential) without computing the whole series.
  • Solve Boas Problems 3–12 completely.

1. Laurent's theorem

Theorem VII (Laurent)

Let $C_1$ and $C_2$ be two circles centred at $z_0$, and let $f$ be analytic in the ring $R$ between them. Then $f$ can be expanded in a series convergent in $R$:

$$f(z)=\underbrace{a_0+a_1(z-z_0)+a_2(z-z_0)^2+\cdots}_{\text{"a" series: analytic part}}+\underbrace{\frac{b_1}{z-z_0}+\frac{b_2}{(z-z_0)^2}+\cdots}_{\text{"b" series: principal part}}\qquad(4.1)$$

Why a ring? The $a$-series is a power series, so it converges inside some circle $C_2$. The $b$-series is a series in $\frac1{z-z_0}$, so it converges outside some circle $C_1$. Both converge between them. The inner circle may shrink to the point $z_0$, and the outer circle may be infinite.

Boas Example 1 — where does this series converge?
$$f(z)=1+\frac z2+\frac{z^2}{4}+\cdots+\left(\frac z2\right)^n+\cdots+\frac2z\left(1-\frac1z+\frac1{z^2}-\cdots+\frac{(-1)^n}{z^n}+\cdots\right)\qquad(4.2)$$

Positive powers: ratio $|z/2|\lt1$ means $|z|\lt2$. Negative powers: $|1/z|\lt1$ means $|z|>1$. So it converges in the ring $1\lt|z|\lt2$.

Coefficient formulas (Boas Problem 5.2)

$$a_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)\,dz}{(z-z_0)^{n+1}},\qquad b_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)\,dz}{(z-z_0)^{-n+1}},\qquad(4.3)$$

where $C$ is any simple closed curve around $z_0$ inside the ring. These prove uniqueness, but they are rarely the easiest way to compute.

Uniqueness, and the practical consequence

In a given ring about $z_0$, the Laurent series of $f$ is unique, so we may find it by any method: known series, partial fractions, geometric series, substitution, multiplication or division of series.

Warning: several rings, several series

If $f$ has several isolated singularities, there are several rings $R_1,R_2,\dots$ about $z_0$ (Boas Fig. 4.2), and a different Laurent series in each. The one that matters for the residue at $z_0$ is the series that converges near $z_0$ (the innermost ring).

H.K. Dass §25.8Laurent's theorem: Dass's proof, and expanding one function in every region

When Laurent instead of Taylor. To expand $f$ about a point where it is not analytic, or in a ring that has singularities inside its inner circle, a Taylor series is impossible. A Laurent series, with negative powers allowed, is what you get.

Statement (Dass)

Let $f$ be analytic on two concentric circles $c_1$ (radius $r_1$) and $c_2$ (radius $r_2\lt r_1$) about $a$, and in the ring between them. Then for every $z$ in the ring

$$f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n(z-a)^n+\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{b_n}{(z-a)^n},\qquad a_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_{c_1}\frac{f(w)\,dw}{(w-a)^{n+1}},\quad b_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_{c_2}\frac{f(w)\,dw}{(w-a)^{-n+1}} .$$
  1. Cut the ring. A cross-cut $AB$ from $c_2$ to $c_1$ makes the ring simply connected. Cauchy's formula on its boundary ($c_1$ anticlockwise, $AB$, $c_2$ clockwise, $BA$) gives, since the two passes along the cut cancel, $$f(z)=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_{c_1}\frac{f(w)\,dw}{w-z}-\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_{c_2}\frac{f(w)\,dw}{w-z}.$$
  2. Outer circle: $|z-a|\lt|w-a|$. Expand exactly as in Taylor's proof, $\frac1{w-z}=\sum\frac{(z-a)^n}{(w-a)^{n+1}}$. This gives $\sum a_n(z-a)^n$.
  3. Inner circle: now $|w-a|\lt|z-a|$, so expand the other way: $-\frac1{w-z}=\frac{1}{(z-a)-(w-a)}=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(w-a)^{n-1}}{(z-a)^n}$. Integrating term by term gives $\sum\frac{b_n}{(z-a)^n}$ with $b_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint f(w)(w-a)^{n-1}dw$. ∎

Notes. (1) By §24.4 both integrals may be taken over any circle in the ring, so one formula covers both: $c_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f(w)\,dw}{(w-a)^{n+1}}$ for all integers $n$. (2) Dass's practical advice: "to expand by Laurent's theorem directly is cumbersome". Use partial fractions and geometric/binomial series instead. By uniqueness, you get the same coefficients.

The algorithm ("take the bigger term common"). Split $f$ into partial fractions $\frac{A}{z-p}$. For each term, compare $|z-a|$ with $|p-a|$ in the region:

  • $|z-a|\lt|p-a|$ (the pole is outside the region): $\frac{A}{z-p}=\frac{-A}{(p-a)\left(1-\frac{z-a}{p-a}\right)}=-A\sum\frac{(z-a)^n}{(p-a)^{n+1}}$. Positive powers.
  • $|z-a|\gt|p-a|$ (the pole is inside the inner circle): $\frac{A}{z-p}=\frac{A}{(z-a)\left(1-\frac{p-a}{z-a}\right)}=A\sum\frac{(p-a)^n}{(z-a)^{n+1}}$. Negative powers.
  • $p=a$: the term is already a power of $(z-a)$. Leave it alone.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: the coefficient of $\frac1{z-a}$ in these series is what the residue theorem needs. In Ex 21 it is $1$ in the outer region, while the residue at $z=1$ itself is $-1$. Different regions give different "$b_1$".

Worked example (Dass Ex 22) — $\frac{1}{(1+z^2)(z+2)}$ in (i) $1\lt|z|\lt2$ and (ii) $|z|\gt2$
  1. Partial fractions: $\frac{1}{(1+z^2)(z+2)}=-\frac15\cdot\frac{z-2}{1+z^2}+\frac15\cdot\frac1{z+2}$.
  2. (i) $|z|\gt1$, so $\frac{1}{1+z^2}=\frac1{z^2}\left(1+\frac1{z^2}\right)^{-1}$. And $|z|\lt2$, so $\frac1{z+2}=\frac12\left(1+\frac z2\right)^{-1}$. The result is $$\cdots-\frac{2}{5z^4}+\frac{1}{5z^3}+\frac{2}{5z^2}-\frac{1}{5z}+\frac1{10}-\frac{z}{20}+\frac{z^2}{40}-\cdots$$ The coefficients of $z^{-1},z^{-2},z^{-3},z^{-4}$ are $-\frac15,+\frac25,+\frac15,-\frac25$, and then the pattern repeats. (The widget shows the bars.)
  3. (ii) $|z|\gt2$: now $\frac1{z+2}=\frac1z\left(1+\frac2z\right)^{-1}$ too. The leading terms cancel, leaving $\frac{1}{z^3}-\frac{2}{z^4}+\frac{3}{z^5}-\frac{6}{z^6}+\cdots$. It starts at $z^{-3}$, as it must, since $f\sim\frac1{z^3}$ at infinity.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: the outermost Laurent series starts at $z^{-(\deg q-\deg p)}$. That is a quick check on any "$|z|\gt R$" expansion.

Worked example (Dass Ex 24, 25) — Laurent series from known Taylor series

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: $\frac{z}{e^z-1}$ (and hence $\coth$ and $\cot$) is the series behind many residue and summation questions. Know its first three terms.

2. The standard method: partial fractions plus geometric series

Everything rests on two expansions of $\frac1{z-a}$:

$$\frac{1}{z-a}=-\frac1a\cdot\frac{1}{1-z/a}=-\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{z^n}{a^{n+1}}\quad(|z|\lt|a|),\qquad\frac1{z-a}=\frac1z\cdot\frac{1}{1-a/z}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{a^n}{z^{n+1}}\quad(|z|>|a|).$$

Rule: in a given ring, each pole $a$ lies either outside the ring (inside the inner circle is impossible for those) or inside the inner circle. Use powers of $z/a$ for poles with $|a|$ larger than the ring, and powers of $a/z$ for poles with $|a|$ smaller than the ring.

Boas Example 2 — $f(z)=\dfrac{12}{z(2-z)(1+z)}$, all three Laurent series about 0
  1. The singular points are $0$, $2$, $-1$, giving the rings $R_1$: $0\lt|z|\lt1$, $R_2$: $1\lt|z|\lt2$, $R_3$: $|z|>2$.
  2. Partial fractions (4.5): $f=\dfrac4z\left(\dfrac{1}{1+z}+\dfrac{1}{2-z}\right)$.
  3. $R_1$: both in powers of $z$: $\frac1{1+z}=1-z+z^2-\dots$ and $\frac{1}{2-z}=\frac12\left(1+\frac z2+\frac{z^2}4+\dots\right)$. Result (4.6): $f=\frac6z-3+\frac{9z}2-\frac{15z^2}4+\frac{33z^3}8-\cdots$. Residue at $0$: $\mathbf{6}$.
  4. $R_2$: $\frac1{2-z}$ in powers of $z$, but $\frac1{1+z}=\frac1z\cdot\frac1{1+1/z}$ in powers of $1/z$. This gives (4.2).
  5. $R_3$: both in powers of $1/z$ (4.7). This gives (4.8): $f=-\frac{12}{z^3}\left(1+\frac1z+\frac3{z^2}+\frac5{z^3}+\frac{11}{z^4}+\cdots\right)$.

H.K. Dass §25.8 (cont.)Laurent series about a singular point: "all possible" expansions, and Exercise 25.3

About a pole $a$ there are always at least two Laurent series: one in the punctured disk $0\lt|z-a|\lt d$, where $d$ is the distance to the next singularity, and one in $|z-a|\gt d$. The first one is the series that tells you the type of singularity and the residue.

Worked example (Dass Ex 26) — all Laurent series of $\frac{1}{z(z+2)^3}$ about $z=-2$
  1. Put $t=z+2$: $f=\frac{1}{t^3(t-2)}$. The other singularity, $z=0$, is at distance $|t|=2$, so there are two regions.
  2. $0\lt|z+2|\lt2$: $\frac{1}{t-2}=-\frac12\left(1-\frac t2\right)^{-1}$, so $$f=-\frac{1}{2(z+2)^3}-\frac{1}{4(z+2)^2}-\frac{1}{8(z+2)}-\frac1{16}-\frac{z+2}{32}-\cdots$$ This shows a pole of order 3 at $-2$ with residue $-\frac18$.
  3. $|z+2|\gt2$: $\frac1{t-2}=\frac1t\left(1-\frac2t\right)^{-1}$, so $$f=\frac{1}{(z+2)^4}+\frac{2}{(z+2)^5}+\frac{4}{(z+2)^6}+\cdots=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{2^k}{(z+2)^{k+4}} .$$

(The printed solution gives only the first series and labels its region $0\lt|z-2|\lt1$. The region is $0\lt|z+2|\lt2$, as the step $\left|\frac t2\right|\lt1$ shows.)

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: "the residue at $z=-2$" must be read from the series valid near $-2$ (the punctured disk). Reading $b_1$ from the outer series gives $0$, which is wrong.

Worked example (Dass Ex 27) — $\frac{e^z}{(z-1)^2}$ about $z=1$

With $t=z-1$: $\frac{e\cdot e^t}{t^2}=e\left[\frac1{(z-1)^2}+\frac1{z-1}+\frac1{2!}+\frac{z-1}{3!}+\cdots\right]$, valid for $0\lt|z-1|\lt\infty$. Double pole, residue $e$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: when the only singularity is the expansion point, the shift-and-substitute method gives the whole Laurent series at once.

3. Classifying isolated singular points

Let $z_0$ be a regular point or an isolated singular point, and expand $f$ in the Laurent series that converges near $z_0$ (in $0\lt|z-z_0|\lt r$).

Residue

The coefficient $b_1$ of $\frac{1}{z-z_0}$ (in the series that converges near $z_0$) is the residue of $f$ at $z_0$. In Boas Example 3: the residue of $e^z$ at $0$ is $0$, of $\frac{e^z}{z^3}$ is $\frac1{2!}$, and of $e^{1/z}$ is $1$.

A function analytic except for poles is called meromorphic. Near a pole the graph of $|f|$ over the plane looks like a tapered pole, which is where the name comes from.

Finding the order without the series

Rule (Boas Example 4)

Write $f=\dfrac{g(z)}{(z-z_0)^n}$ with $g$ analytic at $z_0$. If $g(z_0)\ne0$, the pole has order exactly $n$. If $g(z_0)=0$, factor out the zero of $g$: the order drops by the order of that zero.

Equivalently: (order of the zero of the denominator) − (order of the zero of the numerator), if positive, is the order of the pole. If it is $\le0$, the point is regular.

  • $\dfrac{z+3}{z^2(z-1)^3(z+1)}$: pole of order 2 at $0$, order 3 at $1$, simple at $-1$.
  • $\dfrac{\sin^2z}{z^3}$: $\sin^2z$ has a double zero at $0$ ($\sin z\approx z$), so $3-2=1$: a simple pole.
  • Essential singularities usually come from functions of $\frac1{z-z_0}$ inside $e^{(\ )}$, $\sin$, $\cos$, … (infinitely many negative powers).
How wild is an essential singularity?

Casorati–Weierstrass: near an essential singularity, $f$ comes arbitrarily close to every complex value. Picard's great theorem: it actually takes every complex value, with at most one exception, infinitely often in every neighbourhood. E.g. $e^{1/z}$ takes every value except $0$ infinitely often near $z=0$.

H.K. Dass §25.9At a pole $|f|\to\infty$; and Laurent coefficients as integrals (Bessel functions appear)

Theorem (§25.9)

If $f$ has a pole of order $m$ at $a$, then $|f(z)|\to\infty$ as $z\to a$.

  1. Near $a$: $f(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_n(z-a)^n+\sum_{n=1}^m\frac{b_n}{(z-a)^n}$, with $b_m\ne0$. The analytic part stays bounded.
  2. Write the principal part as $\frac{1}{(z-a)^m}\left[b_m+\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}b_n(z-a)^{m-n}\right]$.
  3. By $|A+B|\ge|A|-|B|$, this is at least $\frac{1}{|z-a|^m}\left[|b_m|-\sum_{n\lt m}|b_n|\,|z-a|^{m-n}\right]$. The bracket tends to $|b_m|\gt0$ while $\frac1{|z-a|^m}\to\infty$. ∎

All three cases, side by side. The type of an isolated singularity is decided by the behaviour of $|f|$:

  • removable: $f$ stays bounded near $a$ (e.g. $\frac{\sin z}{z-\pi}$ at $\pi$, Dass Ex 17);
  • pole: $|f|\to\infty$ (this theorem). Then $(z-a)^mf$ is bounded and non-zero;
  • essential: neither. $f$ takes values arbitrarily close to every complex number near $a$ (Casorati–Weierstrass). Examples are $e^{1/z}$ and Dass Ex 28 below.

When the coefficients must come from the integral formula (Dass Ex 28, 29)

For $e^{\frac c2(z-\frac1z)}$ or $\sin\left[c\left(z+\frac1z\right)\right]$ there are no partial fractions to use, so take $c_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f(z)\,dz}{z^{n+1}}$ on $|z|=1$, with $z=e^{i\theta}$ and $dz=ie^{i\theta}d\theta$:

Worked example (Dass Ex 28) — $\sin\left[c\left(z+\frac1z\right)\right]$
  1. On $|z|=1$, $z+\frac1z=2\cos\theta$. So $a_n=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\sin(2c\cos\theta)e^{-in\theta}d\theta=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\sin(2c\cos\theta)\cos n\theta\,d\theta$. The $\sin n\theta$ part vanishes by the symmetry $\theta\to2\pi-\theta$.
  2. The function is unchanged when $z$ is replaced by $\frac1z$, so $b_n=a_n$: $\ f=a_0+\sum_{n\ge1}a_n\left(z^n+\frac1{z^n}\right)$.
  3. Going further: $\sin(x\cos\theta)=2\sum_{k\ge0}(-1)^kJ_{2k+1}(x)\cos(2k+1)\theta$. So $a_n=0$ for even $n$ (including $a_0$), and $a_{2k+1}=(-1)^kJ_{2k+1}(2c)$: $$\sin\left[c\left(z+\tfrac1z\right)\right]=\sum_{k\ge0}(-1)^kJ_{2k+1}(2c)\left(z^{2k+1}+z^{-(2k+1)}\right),\qquad0\lt|z|\lt\infty .$$ Infinitely many negative powers: $z=0$ is an essential singularity.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: Fourier coefficients of $\sin(x\cos\theta)$ and $\cos(x\sin\theta)$ are Bessel functions. This links complex analysis to Chapter 12 and the Jacobi–Anger expansion used in scattering problems.

Worked example (Dass Ex 29) — $e^{\frac c2\left(z-\frac1z\right)}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_n(c)\,z^n$: the Bessel generating function
  1. On $|z|=1$, $\frac c2\left(z-\frac1z\right)=ic\sin\theta$. So $a_n=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}e^{i(c\sin\theta-n\theta)}d\theta=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\cos(c\sin\theta-n\theta)\,d\theta$, the sine part integrating to $0$.
  2. That integral is Bessel's integral, so $a_n=J_n(c)$.
  3. Replacing $z$ by $-\frac1z$ leaves $f$ unchanged, so the coefficient of $z^{-n}$ is $(-1)^nJ_n(c)$. That is, $J_{-n}=(-1)^nJ_n$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: "the coefficient of $z^n$ in $e^{\frac x2(z-1/z)}$" is $J_n(x)$. This is one of the most-asked Bessel facts, and here it is just a Laurent coefficient.

5. Extra practice (exam level)

P1. Find the Laurent series of $\dfrac{1}{z^2-1}$ about $z=1$ valid for $0\lt|z-1|\lt2$, and the residue at $1$. Medium
Show solution

Put $w=z-1$: $\frac{1}{w(w+2)}=\frac1{2w}\cdot\frac{1}{1+w/2}=\frac{1}{2w}-\frac14+\frac w8-\cdots$. The residue is $\frac12$.

P2. Laurent series of $z^2\sin\frac1z$ about $0$, and its residue. Medium
Show solution

$z^2\left(\frac1z-\frac1{6z^3}+\frac{1}{120z^5}-\cdots\right)=z-\frac{1}{6z}+\frac1{120z^3}-\cdots$. It is an essential singularity with residue $-\frac16$.

P3. First three terms of the Laurent series of $\cot z$ and of $\frac{1}{e^z-1}$ about $0$. Hard
Show solution

$\cot z=\frac{\cos z}{\sin z}=\frac{1-z^2/2+\cdots}{z(1-z^2/6+\cdots)}=\frac1z-\frac z3-\frac{z^3}{45}-\cdots$ (residue 1).

$\frac{1}{e^z-1}=\frac{1}{z(1+z/2+z^2/6+\cdots)}=\frac1z-\frac12+\frac{z}{12}-\cdots$ (residue 1).

P4. Find the Laurent series of $\dfrac{e^z}{(z-1)^2}$ about $z=1$ and the residue there. Medium
Show solution

$e^z=e\cdot e^{z-1}=e\left[1+(z-1)+\frac{(z-1)^2}{2}+\cdots\right]$, so $f=\frac{e}{(z-1)^2}+\frac{e}{z-1}+\frac e2+\cdots$. A pole of order 2 with residue $e$.

7. Going deeper: order arithmetic, uniqueness, and how wild essential singularities are

Uniqueness: any method gives the same series

In a given annulus the Laurent coefficients are fixed by $a_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^{n+1}}dz$. So any legitimate way of getting a series, whether partial fractions, geometric series, or multiplying known series, produces the Laurent series of that annulus. Different annuli give different series. The number of annuli about $z_0$ equals the number of distinct distances from $z_0$ to the singularities, counting the outer region (and the inner punctured disk if $z_0$ is itself singular).

Order arithmetic (no series needed)

Write $\operatorname{ord}_{z_0}f=m$ if $f=(z-z_0)^mg$ with $g$ analytic and $g(z_0)\ne0$. A zero of order $m$ has $m\gt0$, and a pole of order $k$ has $m=-k$. Then

$$\operatorname{ord}(fg)=\operatorname{ord}f+\operatorname{ord}g,\qquad\operatorname{ord}(f/g)=\operatorname{ord}f-\operatorname{ord}g,\qquad\operatorname{ord}(f^n)=n\operatorname{ord}f .$$

Standard orders at $0$: $\sin z$, $\tan z$, $e^z-1$ and $\ln(1+z)$ have order $1$; $1-\cos z$ has order $2$; $z-\sin z$ has order $3$. Example: $\frac{1}{z\sin^2z}$ has order $-1-2=-3$, a pole of order 3.

Essential singularities: Casorati–Weierstrass and Picard

TypePrincipal partBehaviour as $z\to z_0$
Removablenone$f$ has a finite limit
Pole of order $k$finitely many terms, ending at $(z-z_0)^{-k}$$|f|\to\infty$
Essentialinfinitely many termsno limit, finite or infinite

Near an essential singularity, $f$ comes arbitrarily close to every complex value (Casorati–Weierstrass). Picard's theorem goes further: $f$ actually takes every value, with at most one exception, infinitely often. Example: $e^{1/z}$ near $0$ takes every value except $0$.

Traps that cost marks
  • $\frac{\sin z}{z^4}$ has a pole of order 3, not 4, because $\sin z$ has a zero of order 1.
  • A Laurent series about $z_0$ in an annulus not touching $z_0$ can have infinitely many negative powers even though $z_0$ is a regular point. This does not make $z_0$ an essential singularity. Only the innermost punctured-disk series classifies $z_0$.

11. Summary

  • Laurent series $\sum a_n(z-z_0)^n+\sum b_n(z-z_0)^{-n}$ converge in rings. There is one series per ring, and each is unique.
  • Method: partial fractions, then expand $\frac1{z-a}$ in $z/a$ (pole outside the ring) or in $a/z$ (pole inside the inner circle).
  • No $b$'s: regular. Finitely many: pole of order $n$. Infinitely many: essential.
  • Residue $=b_1$ from the series valid near the point. Order of a pole = (zero order of the denominator) − (zero order of the numerator).
