2.6 Complex Infinite Series

Boas, Chapter 2, Section 6 · convergence of $\sum z_n$, absolute convergence, the ratio test with $|\ |$, the complex geometric series, and summing trigonometric series with complex numbers.

Goals
  • Know what convergence of a complex series means (two real series).
  • Test any complex series: preliminary test, absolute convergence (ratio, comparison, root), or splitting into real and imaginary parts.
  • Sum geometric series with complex ratio, and use them to sum $\sum r^n\cos n\theta$, $\sum r^n\sin n\theta$.
  • Solve all of Boas's Section 6 problems.

1. What convergence means

For a series $\sum_{n}z_n$ with complex terms, the partial sums are complex:

$$S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}z_n=X_N+iY_N .$$
Definition

The series converges to $S=X+iY$ if $S_N\to S$ as $N\to\infty$, i.e. $|S_N-S|\to0$. This is the same as $X_N\to X$ and $Y_N\to Y$:

$$\sum z_n\ \text{converges}\iff\sum\operatorname{Re}z_n\ \text{and}\ \sum\operatorname{Im}z_n\ \text{both converge}.$$

Picture: the partial sums $S_1,S_2,S_3,\dots$ are points in the plane, joined head to tail like a chain of arrows. The series converges if this chain settles down to one point.

2. Absolute convergence

Definition

$\sum z_n$ is absolutely convergent if $\sum|z_n|$ converges. Here $|z_n|=\sqrt{x_n^2+y_n^2}\ge0$ is a series of positive real terms, so every test from Chapter 1 (comparison, ratio, integral, root) can be used on it.

Theorem (Boas Problem 6.1)

An absolutely convergent complex series converges.

Proof. Let $z_n=a_n+ib_n$. Since $|a_n|\le\sqrt{a_n^2+b_n^2}=|z_n|$, the comparison test shows that $\sum|a_n|$ converges, so $\sum a_n$ converges (absolute convergence of a real series implies convergence). The same holds for $\sum b_n$. So both the real and imaginary parts converge. $\blacksquare$

As for real series, absolutely convergent series can be rearranged and multiplied freely. Boas uses this when deriving Euler's formula (Section 2.9).

The tests, adapted

TestStatement for complex $z_n$
Preliminary (n-th term)If $z_n\not\to0$ (equivalently $|z_n|\not\to0$), the series diverges.
Ratio test$\rho=\lim\left|\dfrac{z_{n+1}}{z_n}\right|$. If $\rho\lt1$: absolutely convergent. If $\rho>1$: divergent (Boas 6.14). If $\rho=1$: no conclusion.
Root test$\sigma=\lim|z_n|^{1/n}$, with the same conclusions.
ComparisonIf $|z_n|\le M_n$ and $\sum M_n$ converges, then $\sum z_n$ converges absolutely.
SplitTest $\sum\operatorname{Re}z_n$ and $\sum\operatorname{Im}z_n$ separately (useful when $\rho=1$).

Why $\rho>1$ means divergence: eventually $|z_{n+1}|>|z_n|$, so the terms do not tend to zero, and the series fails the preliminary test.

3. The complex geometric series

Key result (Boas Example 3) $$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^n=1+z+z^2+\dots=\frac{1}{1-z}\qquad\text{iff }|z|\lt1 .$$

Partial sums: $S_N=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}z^n=\dfrac{1-z^N}{1-z}$ for $z\ne1$. If $|z|\lt1$, then $|z^N|=|z|^N\to0$. If $|z|\ge1$, then $|z^n|\ge1$ and the terms do not go to zero.

With $z=re^{i\theta}$: $\sum r^ne^{in\theta}$ converges iff $r\lt1$. Only the modulus matters, not the angle.

Using it to sum trigonometric series

Take real and imaginary parts of $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty r^ne^{in\theta}=\frac{1}{1-re^{i\theta}}$ for $0\le r\lt1$. Multiply the top and bottom by the conjugate $1-re^{-i\theta}$:

$$\frac{1-re^{-i\theta}}{(1-re^{i\theta})(1-re^{-i\theta})}=\frac{1-r\cos\theta+ir\sin\theta}{1-2r\cos\theta+r^2}.$$ $$\boxed{\sum_{n=0}^\infty r^n\cos n\theta=\frac{1-r\cos\theta}{1-2r\cos\theta+r^2},\qquad \sum_{n=1}^\infty r^n\sin n\theta=\frac{r\sin\theta}{1-2r\cos\theta+r^2}}$$

(This is how the Poisson kernel and many Fourier-series sums are found. With $r=\tfrac12$: $\sum\frac{\cos n\theta}{2^n}=\frac{4-2\cos\theta}{5-4\cos\theta}$.)

4. Boas's examples

Example 1 — $1+\frac{1+i}{2}+\frac{(1+i)^2}{4}+\dots=\sum\left(\frac{1+i}{2}\right)^n$
  1. Ratio test: $\rho=\lim\left|\dfrac{(1+i)^{n+1}}{2^{n+1}}\cdot\dfrac{2^n}{(1+i)^n}\right|=\left|\dfrac{1+i}{2}\right|=\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\lt1$.
  2. So the series converges absolutely.
  3. Bonus, the sum: $\dfrac{1}{1-\frac{1+i}{2}}=\dfrac{2}{1-i}=1+i$.
Example 2 — $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\dfrac{i^n}{n}$ (the ratio test fails)
  1. $\rho=\lim\dfrac{n}{n+1}=1$, so the ratio test says nothing.
  2. Write out the terms: $i-\tfrac12-\tfrac i3+\tfrac14+\tfrac i5-\tfrac16-\dots$
  3. Real part: $-\tfrac12+\tfrac14-\tfrac16+\dots=\sum_{n\ge1}\dfrac{(-1)^n}{2n}$, which is alternating with decreasing terms going to 0, so it converges.
  4. Imaginary part: $1-\tfrac13+\tfrac15-\dots=\sum_{n\ge0}\dfrac{(-1)^n}{2n+1}$, which also converges by the alternating series test.
  5. So the series converges, but not absolutely, since $\sum\frac1n$ diverges. It is conditionally convergent.
  6. Its value: $-\ln(1-i)=-\tfrac12\ln2+\tfrac{\pi}{4}i\approx-0.3466+0.7854i$. The imaginary part is Leibniz's series for $\pi/4$.
Example 3 — $\sum z^n=\sum r^ne^{in\theta}$

A geometric series with ratio $z$. It converges iff $|z|=r\lt1$.

Trap

$\rho$ uses the absolute value of the ratio. $\lim\frac{z_{n+1}}{z_n}=i$ means $\rho=|i|=1$ (no conclusion), not "$\rho=i$". Never compare a complex number with 1: $i\lt1$ means nothing.

6. Interesting facts

Did you know?
  • Riemann's rearrangement theorem says a conditionally convergent real series can be rearranged to sum to any real number. For complex series, the Lévy–Steinitz theorem says the possible sums of rearrangements form either a single point, a line, or the whole plane.
  • $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\dfrac{e^{in\theta}}{n}=-\ln(1-e^{i\theta})$ for $\theta\ne2k\pi$. Taking imaginary parts gives $\sum\frac{\sin n\theta}{n}=\frac{\pi-\theta}{2}$ for $0\lt\theta\lt2\pi$, which is the Fourier series of a sawtooth wave.
  • The $N$ terms $\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{2\pi ikn/N}$ add to $0$ unless $k$ is a multiple of $N$. This is the heart of the discrete Fourier transform.

7. Boas Problems, Section 6 — solutions

1. Prove that an absolutely convergent series of complex numbers converges.
Show solution

See the theorem in Section 2: $|a_n|,|b_n|\le\sqrt{a_n^2+b_n^2}$. By comparison, $\sum a_n$ and $\sum b_n$ converge absolutely, hence converge. So $\sum(a_n+ib_n)$ converges.

14. Prove that a series of complex terms diverges if $\rho>1$.
Show solution

If $\rho>1$, pick $\rho'$ with $1\lt\rho'\lt\rho$. For $n\ge N$, $|z_{n+1}|>\rho'|z_n|$, so $|z_n|\ge\rho'^{\,n-N}|z_N|\to\infty$ (assuming $z_N\ne0$). The terms do not tend to 0, so the series diverges.

8. More worked examples

Sum $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty\left(\frac i2\right)^n$
  1. A geometric series with $|z|=\tfrac12\lt1$.
  2. Sum $=\dfrac{1}{1-i/2}=\dfrac{2}{2-i}=\dfrac{2(2+i)}{5}=\dfrac45+\dfrac25i$.
  3. Reading off parts: $1-\tfrac14+\tfrac1{16}-\dots=\tfrac45$ and $\tfrac12-\tfrac18+\dots=\tfrac25$ ✔.
Sum $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{\sin n\theta}{2^n}$
  1. This is $\operatorname{Im}\sum_{n\ge1}w^n$ with $w=\tfrac12e^{i\theta}$, and $\sum_{n\ge1}w^n=\dfrac{w}{1-w}$.
  2. Multiply the top and bottom by $1-\bar w$: $\dfrac{w-|w|^2}{|1-w|^2}$, with $|1-w|^2=1-\cos\theta+\tfrac14$.
  3. Im $=\dfrac{\frac12\sin\theta}{\frac54-\cos\theta}=\dfrac{2\sin\theta}{5-4\cos\theta}$.
Show $\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{2\pi ikn/N}=0$ for $k\not\equiv0\pmod N$
  1. A geometric sum with ratio $q=e^{2\pi ik/N}\ne1$.
  2. Sum $=\dfrac{1-q^N}{1-q}=\dfrac{1-e^{2\pi ik}}{1-q}=0$.
  3. Geometrically: the $N$ arrows are the vertices of a regular polygon, and they cancel. (This also proves that the sum of the $n$-th roots of unity is 0; see Section 2.10.)

9. Practice problems

P1. Test $\sum\dfrac{n(1+i)^n}{3^n}$. Easy
Show solution

$\rho=\lim\frac{n+1}{n}\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{3}=\frac{\sqrt2}3\lt1$, so it converges absolutely.

P2. Test $\sum\dfrac{(1+i)^n}{n^2}$. Easy
Show solution

$|z_n|=\frac{2^{n/2}}{n^2}\to\infty$, so it diverges.

P3. Test $\sum\dfrac{1}{n+i}$. Medium
Show solution

$\frac{1}{n+i}=\frac{n-i}{n^2+1}$. The real part $\frac{n}{n^2+1}\sim\frac1n$ diverges by comparison, so the series diverges (even though the imaginary part converges).

P4. Test $\sum\dfrac{i^n}{\sqrt n}$ and $\sum\dfrac{i^n}{n^2}$. Medium
Show solution

$\sum\frac{i^n}{\sqrt n}$: the real and imaginary parts are alternating series with terms decreasing to 0, so it converges conditionally ($\sum\frac1{\sqrt n}$ diverges). $\sum\frac{i^n}{n^2}$ converges absolutely.

P5. Find the sum of $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{\cos n\theta}{3^n}$. Medium
Show solution

Use the boxed formula with $r=\frac13$: $\dfrac{1-\frac13\cos\theta}{1-\frac23\cos\theta+\frac19}=\dfrac{9-3\cos\theta}{10-6\cos\theta}$.

P6. Show $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty nz^{n}=\frac{z}{(1-z)^2}$ for $|z|\lt1$, and evaluate $\sum\frac{n}{2^n}i^n$. Hard
Show solution

Differentiate $\sum z^n=\frac1{1-z}$ term by term (allowed inside the disk of convergence, Section 2.7): $\sum nz^{n-1}=\frac1{(1-z)^2}$. Multiply by $z$.

With $z=i/2$: $\dfrac{i/2}{(1-i/2)^2}=\dfrac{i/2}{\frac34-i}=\dfrac{(i/2)(\frac34+i)}{\frac9{16}+1}=\dfrac{-\frac12+\frac38i}{\frac{25}{16}}=-\dfrac{8}{25}+\dfrac{6}{25}i$.

P7. For which real $\theta$ does $\sum\frac{e^{in\theta}}{n}$ converge? Hard
Show solution

If $\theta=2k\pi$, it is $\sum\frac1n$, which diverges. Otherwise it converges (conditionally) by Dirichlet's test: the partial sums of $e^{in\theta}$ are bounded by $\frac{2}{|1-e^{i\theta}|}$, and $\frac1n$ decreases to 0.

P8. (NET) The series $\sum_{n=0}^\infty\left(\frac{3+4i}{6}\right)^n$: Easy
Show solution

$|z|=\frac56\lt1$, so it converges, to $\dfrac{1}{1-\frac{3+4i}6}=\dfrac{6}{3-4i}=\dfrac{6(3+4i)}{25}=\dfrac{18}{25}+\dfrac{24}{25}i$.

11. Summary

  • $\sum z_n$ converges ⇔ $\sum\operatorname{Re}z_n$ and $\sum\operatorname{Im}z_n$ converge.
  • Absolute convergence ($\sum|z_n|$, which can be tested with all the positive-term tests) implies convergence.
  • Ratio test with $\rho=\lim|z_{n+1}/z_n|$. For $\rho=1$, split into real and imaginary parts.
  • $\sum z^n=\frac1{1-z}$ for $|z|\lt1$. Its real and imaginary parts sum trigonometric series.
