2.13 Logarithms

Boas, Chapter 2, Section 13 · $\ln z=\operatorname{Ln}r+i\theta$: logarithms of negative and complex numbers, infinitely many values, the principal value, branch cuts, and the spiral staircase (Riemann surface).

Goals
  • Find all values of $\ln z$ for any $z\ne0$, and the principal value under either convention.
  • Understand why there are infinitely many values (because $e^w$ is periodic).
  • Know which log laws hold (as sets of values) and where principal values break them.
  • See the logarithm as a spiral staircase (3-D), and the branch cut as a choice of where to "cut" it.

1. Definition

For positive reals you learned that negative numbers have no logarithm. With complex numbers allowed as answers, every $z\ne0$ has logarithms. By definition (13.1–13.2):

$$w=\ln z\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z=e^{w}.$$

(Boas uses $\ln$ for natural logarithms.) The law of exponents $e^{w_1}e^{w_2}=e^{w_1+w_2}$ becomes the familiar law (13.4) $\ln z_1z_2=\ln z_1+\ln z_2$. Applying it to $z=re^{i\theta}$:

$$\boxed{\ln z=\ln\!\left(re^{i\theta}\right)=\operatorname{Ln}r+\ln e^{i\theta}=\operatorname{Ln}r+i\theta}\qquad(13.5)$$

where $\operatorname{Ln}r$ is the ordinary real natural log of the positive number $r=|z|$.

So $$\operatorname{Re}(\ln z)=\operatorname{Ln}|z|,\qquad\operatorname{Im}(\ln z)=\arg z=\theta+2n\pi .$$

2. Infinitely many values

$\theta$ is only defined up to multiples of $2\pi$, so

$$\ln z=\operatorname{Ln}r+i(\theta+2n\pi),\qquad n=0,\pm1,\pm2,\dots$$

A complex number has infinitely many logarithms, differing by multiples of $2\pi i$. The reason: $e^w$ has period $2\pi i$ (Section 2.8), so $e^{w}=e^{w+2\pi i n}$, and "undoing" $e^w$ cannot pick out just one $w$.

In the $w$-plane the values of $\ln z$ sit on a vertical line $\operatorname{Re}w=\operatorname{Ln}r$, spaced $2\pi$ apart.

Principal value

The principal value, often written $\operatorname{Ln}z$, uses the principal value of $\theta$. Boas uses $0\le\theta\lt2\pi$, and many references use $-\pi\lt\theta\le\pi$. Always say which one you use.

Notation clash

Boas writes $\operatorname{Ln}r$ for the real log of a positive number and $\operatorname{Ln}z$ for the principal complex log. They agree for positive real $z$, because the principal $\theta$ of a positive real is $0$ in both conventions.

3. Boas's examples, and more

Example 1 — $\ln(-1)$
  1. $-1$ has $r=1$ and $\theta=\pi,-\pi,3\pi,\dots$ (Fig. 9.2).
  2. $\ln(-1)=\operatorname{Ln}1+i(\pi\pm2n\pi)=i\pi,\ -i\pi,\ 3i\pi,\dots$
  3. Principal value $i\pi$ (in both conventions). So the logarithm of a negative number exists: it is purely imaginary here. (This is Euler's $e^{i\pi}=-1$ read backwards.)
Example 2 — $\ln(1+i)$
  1. $r=\sqrt2$ and $\theta=\frac\pi4\pm2n\pi$ (Fig. 5.1).
  2. $\ln(1+i)=\operatorname{Ln}\sqrt2+i\left(\frac\pi4\pm2n\pi\right)=0.347\ldots+i\left(\frac\pi4\pm2n\pi\right)$.
Even positive numbers have infinitely many logarithms

$\ln 5=\operatorname{Ln}5+2n\pi i=1.609,\ 1.609\pm6.283i,\dots$ Only one of them (the principal one, $n=0$) is real.

Logarithm of a negative real: $\ln(-e)$ (Boas 14.1)

$|-e|=e$ and $\theta=\pi$, so $\ln(-e)=1+i(\pi+2n\pi)$, with principal value $1+i\pi$.

4. Which logarithm laws survive?

LawAs sets of all valuesFor principal values
$e^{\ln z}=z$✔ always✔ always
$\ln(e^w)=w$✔ in the sense $\ln e^w=w+2n\pi i$✘ only if $\operatorname{Im}w$ is in the principal range
$\ln(z_1z_2)=\ln z_1+\ln z_2$✔ (13.4)✘ can be off by $\pm2\pi i$
$\ln(z_1/z_2)=\ln z_1-\ln z_2$✔✘ can be off by $\pm2\pi i$
$\ln(z^n)=n\ln z$✘ the right side is a smaller set (spacing $2n\pi i$)✘ in general
Counterexamples with principal values ($-\pi\lt\theta\le\pi$)
  • $\operatorname{Ln}(-1)+\operatorname{Ln}(-1)=2\pi i$, but $\operatorname{Ln}((-1)(-1))=\operatorname{Ln}1=0$.
  • $\operatorname{Ln}(i^2)=\operatorname{Ln}(-1)=i\pi$ and $2\operatorname{Ln}i=i\pi$ ✔ (here it happens to work). But $\operatorname{Ln}((-1)^2)=0\ne2\operatorname{Ln}(-1)=2\pi i$.
  • $\ln(z^2)$ has values $2\operatorname{Ln}r+i(2\theta+2n\pi)$, while $2\ln z$ has values $2\operatorname{Ln}r+i(2\theta+4n\pi)$, only every other one.

5. The spiral staircase (Riemann surface)

Think of $\operatorname{Im}\ln z=\theta$ as a height above the point $z$. As $z$ goes once around the origin, $\theta$ increases by $2\pi$, so you climb one floor of a spiral staircase (a helicoid). Every floor sits above the same $z$-plane: those are the different values $\ln z+2n\pi i$.

  • A branch is a choice of one floor. The principal branch is one particular floor.
  • To make one floor a single-valued function you must cut the plane along a ray from $0$ (the branch cut). Crossing the cut, the principal value jumps by $2\pi i$.
  • The origin is the branch point: going round it changes the value. $\ln z$ is not defined at $0$, and $\operatorname{Re}\ln z=\operatorname{Ln}|z|\to-\infty$ there.
  • On any branch, $\dfrac{d}{dz}\ln z=\dfrac1z$ (away from the cut).

7. More worked examples

Solve $e^z=-2$
  1. $z=\ln(-2)=\operatorname{Ln}2+i(\pi+2n\pi)$.
  2. Check: $e^{\operatorname{Ln}2+i\pi}=2\cdot(-1)=-2$ ✔.
Solve $e^{2z}=1+i\sqrt3$
  1. $2z=\ln(1+i\sqrt3)=\operatorname{Ln}2+i\left(\frac\pi3+2n\pi\right)$.
  2. $z=\frac12\operatorname{Ln}2+i\left(\frac\pi6+n\pi\right)$.
Find $\operatorname{Ln}\left[(1+i)^4\right]$ vs $4\operatorname{Ln}(1+i)$ (principal, $-\pi\lt\theta\le\pi$)
  1. $(1+i)^4=-4$, so $\operatorname{Ln}(-4)=\operatorname{Ln}4+i\pi$.
  2. $4\operatorname{Ln}(1+i)=4\left(\frac12\operatorname{Ln}2+i\frac\pi4\right)=\operatorname{Ln}4+i\pi$. Here they agree.
  3. With $(1+i)^5=-4-4i$: $\operatorname{Ln}=\operatorname{Ln}(4\sqrt2)-\frac{3\pi}4i$, but $5\operatorname{Ln}(1+i)=\operatorname{Ln}(4\sqrt2)+\frac{5\pi}{4}i$. These differ by $2\pi i$.

8. Practice problems

P1. Find all values of $\ln(-5)$, $\ln(2i)$, $\ln(-1-i\sqrt3)$. Easy
Show solution

$\operatorname{Ln}5+i(\pi+2n\pi)$. $\ \operatorname{Ln}2+i\left(\frac\pi2+2n\pi\right)$. $\ \operatorname{Ln}2+i\left(-\frac{2\pi}3+2n\pi\right)$.

P2. Find all $z$ with $\ln z=1-\frac{i\pi}{2}$. Is there just one? Easy
Show solution

$z=e^{1-i\pi/2}=-ie$. Exactly one $z$: $e^w$ is single-valued. (Many $w$ map to one $z$, but each $w$ gives only one $z$.)

P3. Show that $\operatorname{Re}\ln z$ is the same for all branches, and find where $\operatorname{Re}\ln z=0$. Medium
Show solution

$\operatorname{Re}\ln z=\operatorname{Ln}|z|$, which does not depend on $\theta$. It is zero on the unit circle $|z|=1$.

P4. Solve $\ln(z^2)=i\pi$ (all values of the log allowed). Medium
Show solution

$z^2=e^{i\pi}=-1$, so $z=\pm i$.

P5. With principal values ($-\pi\lt\theta\le\pi$), find $z_1,z_2$ for which $\operatorname{Ln}(z_1z_2)\ne\operatorname{Ln}z_1+\operatorname{Ln}z_2$, and state the general rule. Medium
Show solution

$z_1=z_2=-1+i$: $\operatorname{Ln}z_1+\operatorname{Ln}z_2=\operatorname{Ln}2+\frac{3\pi}2i$, but $z_1z_2=-2i$ has $\operatorname{Ln}=\operatorname{Ln}2-\frac\pi2i$. In general $\operatorname{Ln}(z_1z_2)=\operatorname{Ln}z_1+\operatorname{Ln}z_2+2k\pi i$, where $k\in\{-1,0,1\}$ brings the imaginary part back into $(-\pi,\pi]$.

P6. Show that $\displaystyle\int_{|z|=1}\frac{dz}{z}=2\pi i$ by going once around the circle with $\ln z$. Hard
Show solution

An antiderivative of $1/z$ is $\ln z$. Going once around, $\ln z$ changes by $[\operatorname{Ln}1+i(\theta+2\pi)]-[\operatorname{Ln}1+i\theta]=2\pi i$. Directly: $z=e^{i\phi}$, $dz=ie^{i\phi}d\phi$, so $\int_0^{2\pi}i\,d\phi=2\pi i$. (This is the seed of the residue theorem, Boas Ch. 14.)

P7. (GATE-type) Find the principal value of $i\ln i$, and of $\ln(i^{\,i})$. Medium
Show solution

$\operatorname{Ln}i=\frac{i\pi}{2}$, so $i\operatorname{Ln}i=-\frac\pi2$, a real number. Then $i^i=e^{-\pi/2}$ (principal), and its principal log is $-\frac\pi2$. Both routes agree here.

10. Summary

  • $\ln z=\operatorname{Ln}|z|+i(\theta+2n\pi)$ for $z\ne0$: infinitely many values, $2\pi i$ apart.
  • Principal value: $\theta$ in $[0,2\pi)$ (Boas) or $(-\pi,\pi]$. Negative numbers have logs, e.g. $\ln(-1)=i\pi$.
  • Log laws hold for the full sets of values. Principal values can be off by $2\pi i$.
  • Picture: a spiral staircase over the plane, cut along a ray to get a single-valued branch.
