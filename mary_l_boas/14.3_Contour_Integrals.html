14.3 Contour Integrals

Boas, Chapter 14, Section 3 · integrating $f(z)$ along paths, path independence, Cauchy's theorem (Theorem V), Cauchy's integral formula (Theorem VI) and its derivatives, and their surprising consequences.

Goals
  • Evaluate $\int_Cf(z)\,dz$ directly by parametrising the path, or with an antiderivative when allowed.
  • Know exactly when an integral is path-independent and when a closed integral vanishes.
  • Use Cauchy's integral formula and its derivative form to evaluate closed integrals in seconds.
  • Understand the one integral everything rests on: $\oint\frac{dz}{z-a}=2\pi i$.
  • Solve all 24 of Boas's Section 3 problems (all checked by numerical integration).

1. What a contour integral is

A contour integral is a line integral in the complex plane. With $f=u+iv$ and $dz=dx+i\,dy$ (Boas 3.2):

$$\int_Cf(z)\,dz=\int_C(u+iv)(dx+i\,dy)=\int_C(u\,dx-v\,dy)+i\int_C(v\,dx+u\,dy).$$

So it is two real line integrals of the kind in vector analysis (Chapter 6).

Working method: parametrise

If the path is $z=z(t)$, $a\le t\le b$, then

$$\boxed{\int_Cf(z)\,dz=\int_a^bf\big(z(t)\big)\,z'(t)\,dt}$$

The most important integral in the chapter

On the circle $z=a+\rho e^{i\theta}$:

$$\oint(z-a)^n\,dz=\int_0^{2\pi}\rho^ne^{in\theta}\,i\rho e^{i\theta}\,d\theta=i\rho^{n+1}\int_0^{2\pi}e^{i(n+1)\theta}d\theta=\begin{cases}2\pi i,&n=-1\\0,&n\ne-1\end{cases}$$

(This is Boas Problem 5.1.) Every power of $(z-a)$ integrates to zero around $a$, except $\frac{1}{z-a}$, which gives $2\pi i$ for every radius. This one fact is the seed of Cauchy's formula and of the residue theorem.

The ML estimate (bound on any contour integral)

$$\left|\int_Cf(z)\,dz\right|\le M\cdot L,\qquad M=\max_C|f|,\quad L=\text{length of }C .$$

It is used constantly in 14.7 to show that integrals over big semicircles vanish.

H.K. Dass §24.1Line integrals in practice: Dass's path-by-path examples

In real calculus $\int_a^bf(x)\,dx$ always runs along the $x$-axis. In the complex plane $\int_a^bf(z)\,dz$ can run along any curve from $a$ to $b$, and $dz=dx+i\,dy$ points in a different direction on each piece. On the real axis $dz=dx$, and on a vertical line $dz=i\,dy$. So, in general, the value can depend on the path. It does not when $f$ is analytic in a simply connected region containing the paths (§24.3).

The working recipe (all of Dass's examples use it).

  1. Write the integrand in $x,y$ and set $dz=dx+i\,dy$.
  2. On each piece of the path, express $y$ in terms of $x$ (or both in terms of $t$), and $dy$ in terms of $dx$. For example: on $y=x$, $dz=(1+i)\,dx$; on $y=x^2$, $dz=(1+2ix)\,dx$; on $x=$ const, $dz=i\,dy$; on a circle $z=a+re^{i\theta}$, $dz=ire^{i\theta}d\theta$.
  3. Integrate the resulting single-variable function. Then add the pieces.
  4. Shortcut: if $f$ is analytic (the integrand contains $z$ only, no $\bar z$, $x$, $y$ or $|z|$ separately), use an antiderivative $F$: the answer is $F(b)-F(a)$ for every path.

  1. $P=x+y$ and $Q=x^2y$, so $P_y=1\ne Q_x=2xy$. The form is not exact, so expect path dependence.
  2. Along $y=x^2$ ($dy=2x\,dx$): $\int_0^3\left(x+x^2+2x^5\right)dx=\frac92+9+243=\boxed{\frac{513}2}$. (The book prints the sum as $256$; the terms add to $256.5$.)

  4. The two values differ, so the integral depends on the path.

Worked example (Dass Ex 2) — $\displaystyle\int_{1-i}^{2+i}(2x+iy+1)\,dz$ along (i) $x=t+1,\ y=2t^2-1$ and (ii) the straight line
  1. (i) $dz=(1+4it)\,dt$ with $t:0\to1$. The integrand becomes $(2t+3)+i(2t^2-1)$. Multiplying out and integrating: $\int_0^1\left[(-8t^3+6t+3)+i(10t^2+12t-1)\right]dt=\boxed{4+\frac{25}{3}i}$.
  2. (ii) The line is $y=2x-3$, so $dz=(1+2i)\,dx$ with $x:1\to2$. $\int_1^2(-2x+7+i(6x-1))\,dx=\boxed{4+8i}$.
  3. The answers differ because $2x+iy+1$ is not analytic: $u_x=2\ne v_y=1$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: before doing any integral, check whether the integrand is analytic. If it is not, you must parametrise the stated path.

Worked example (Dass Ex 3, Ex 4, Ex 5) — broken lines, straight lines and parabolas

On $AB$: $z=2+iy$, so $\bar z^{\,2}=(2-iy)^2$ and $dz=i\,dy$, giving $i\int_0^1(4-4iy-y^2)dy=2+\frac{11}3i$. Total $\boxed{\frac{14+11i}{3}}$. Along the straight line $y=\frac x2$ instead (Dass Exercise 24.1 Q5): $\frac53(2-i)$, which is different.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: the three standard path types are a straight line ($dz=(1+im)\,dx$), axis-parallel pieces ($dz=dx$ or $i\,dy$), and a parabola ($dz=(1+2ix)\,dx$). Every "evaluate along the path" question is one of these.

Worked example (Dass Ex 6, Ex 7) — analytic integrands: the path does not matter

Ex 6: $\int z^2dz$ from $0$ to $3+i$. Along $OM$ ($x=3y$): $\int_0^1(8+6i)(3+i)y^2dy=\frac{18+26i}{3}=6+\frac{26}3i$. Along $O\to3\to3+i$: $9+i\left(9-\frac13+3i\right)=6+\frac{26}3i$. They are the same, and both equal $\left[\frac{z^3}{3}\right]_0^{3+i}=\frac{(3+i)^3}{3}=\frac{18+26i}3$.

Ex 7: $\int(12z^2-4iz)\,dz$ from $1+i$ to $2+3i$. Dass parametrises $y=2x-1$ and grinds through. With the antiderivative it is one line: $\left[4z^3-2iz^2\right]_{1+i}^{2+3i}=\boxed{-156+38i}$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: an analytic polynomial integrand means "use the antiderivative". Parametrising wastes minutes in a timed exam.

Worked example (Dass Ex 8, Ex 9, Ex 10) — closed paths, and the one integral that survives

Ex 8: $\oint z^2dz$ around the triangle $0,\ 1+i,\ -1+i$ is $0$. Dass checks it side by side, and also with Green's theorem: $\iint\left[2i(x+iy)-2i(x+iy)\right]dx\,dy=0$.

Ex 9: $\oint(z+1)^2dz$ around the rectangle $\pm a\pm ib$ is $0$. The four sides cancel in pairs, by odd/even symmetry.

Ex 10, the key integral: on $z=a+re^{i\theta}$, $\oint(z-a)^ndz=ir^{n+1}\int_0^{2\pi}e^{i(n+1)\theta}d\theta$.

  • For an integer $n\ne-1$: $\frac{r^{n+1}}{n+1}\left[e^{2\pi i(n+1)}-1\right]=0$. This covers both positive powers and $n=-2,-3,\dots$
  • For $n=-1$: $i\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta=\boxed{2\pi i}$, independent of $r$.
  • For non-integer $n$, $(z-a)^n$ is multivalued. On a closed loop around $a$ it does not return to its starting value, so you must specify a branch cut, and the answer is in general non-zero.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: "$\oint\frac{dz}{z-a}=2\pi i$ and every other integer power gives $0$" is the whole content of the residue theorem in one line.

Worked example (Dass Ex 11, Ex 12, Ex 13) — $|z|$ and $\log z$: non-analytic and multivalued integrands

Ex 11: $\int|z|\,dz$ on the straight line from $-i$ to $i$. $z=iy$, so $|z|=|y|$: $i\int_{-1}^1|y|\,dy=\boxed{i}$.

Ex 12: the same along the left half of $|z|=1$. There $|z|=1$, so $\int dz=z\Big|_{-i}^{i}=\boxed{2i}$. The right half also gives $2i$: on the unit circle $|z|$ is constant, so any arc of it from $-i$ to $i$ gives $2i$. But the straight line gives $i$, so the integral is path dependent.

Ex 13: $\oint_{|z|=1}\log z\,dz$. On the circle $\log z=i\theta$, so $\oint=-\int\theta e^{i\theta}d\theta$. The answer depends on where the branch cut crosses the circle:

  • $\theta$ from $0$ to $2\pi$ (cut along the positive real axis): $\boxed{2\pi i}$ (Dass's answer);
  • principal branch, $\theta$ from $-\pi$ to $\pi$ (cut along the negative real axis): $\boxed{-2\pi i}$;
  • in general, if the cut meets the circle at $e^{i\theta_0}$: $2\pi i\,e^{i\theta_0}$. Reason: $\int\log z\,dz=z\log z-z$, and across the cut $\log z$ jumps by $2\pi i$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: $\log z$ is not single-valued on a loop around $0$, so Cauchy's theorem does not apply. Options that say "$0$ by Cauchy" are traps.

2. Path independence and antiderivatives

When can you just use an antiderivative?

If $f$ is analytic in a simply connected region containing the path, and $F'=f$ there, then

$$\int_{z_1}^{z_2}f(z)\,dz=F(z_2)-F(z_1),$$

independent of the path. Example (Boas 3.2): $\int_0^{1+i}(z^2-z)\,dz=\left[\frac{z^3}3-\frac{z^2}2\right]_0^{1+i}=-\frac23-\frac i3$ along any path.

When you can NOT
  • $f$ not analytic (contains $\bar z$, $|z|$, …): the value depends on the path. Boas 3.12: $\int_0^{1+2i}|z|^2dz$ is $\frac53(1+2i)$ along the straight line but $\frac{13}3+\frac83i$ along the broken line.
  • The region has a hole (a singularity) between two paths: e.g. $\int\frac{dz}{z}$ from $1$ to $-1$ gives $i\pi$ along the upper semicircle and $-i\pi$ along the lower one. Their difference, $2\pi i$, is the closed integral around $0$.

3. Cauchy's theorem (Theorem V)

Theorem V — Cauchy's theorem

Let $C$ be a simple closed curve (it does not cross itself; finitely many corners are allowed). If $f(z)$ is analytic on and inside $C$, then

$$\oint_Cf(z)\,dz=0.\qquad(3.1)$$

Proof (assuming $f'$ is continuous). By Green's theorem in the plane (3.3), $\oint_C(P\,dx+Q\,dy)=\iint\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)dx\,dy$. Applied to the two real integrals in (3.2):

$$\oint(u\,dx-v\,dy)=\iint\left(-v_x-u_y\right)dx\,dy=0,\qquad\oint(v\,dx+u\,dy)=\iint\left(u_x-v_y\right)dx\,dy=0,$$

because both integrands vanish by the Cauchy–Riemann equations. $\blacksquare$ (Boas Problem 13 is the converse view: path independence needs CR, just as curl-free fields need $\partial F_y/\partial x=\partial F_x/\partial y$.)

Deformation of contours

A consequence used constantly: you may deform a contour freely as long as you do not pass it across a singular point; the integral does not change. In particular, an integral around a big complicated curve enclosing one singularity equals the integral around a tiny circle about it (Boas eq. 3.6).

Multiply connected regions (§24.4)

Theorem

If $f$ is analytic in the ring-shaped region between two simple closed curves $C_1$ (outer) and $C_2$ (inner), and on them, then $\displaystyle\oint_{C_1}f\,dz=\oint_{C_2}f\,dz$, both taken anticlockwise.

  1. Join the curves by a cut $AB$. The path "along $AB$, round $C_2$ clockwise, back along $BA$, round $C_1$ anticlockwise" encloses a simply connected region in which $f$ is analytic.
  2. Cauchy gives $\int_{AB}-\oint_{C_2}+\int_{BA}+\oint_{C_1}=0$. The two passes along the cut cancel, since $\int_{BA}=-\int_{AB}$.
  3. So $\oint_{C_1}=\oint_{C_2}$. ∎

Corollary. If $C$ encloses several holes (singularities) with small curves $C_2,C_3,\dots$ around them, then $\oint_C=\oint_{C_2}+\oint_{C_3}+\cdots$. This is how every multi-pole example in §24.5 is done.

4. Cauchy's integral formula (Theorem VI)

Theorem VI

If $f$ is analytic on and inside a simple closed curve $C$ and $a$ is a point inside $C$, then

$$\boxed{f(a)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{z-a}\,dz}\qquad(3.9)$$

or, renaming variables, $f(z)=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(w)}{w-z}\,dw$ (3.10). If $a$ is outside $C$, the integral is $0$ (Cauchy's theorem).

Proof. $\phi(z)=\frac{f(z)}{z-a}$ is analytic except at $a$. Cut out a small circle $C'$ of radius $\rho$ about $a$ and join it to $C$ by a cut (Boas Fig. 3.1). Cauchy's theorem on the region between gives $\oint_C\phi\,dz=\oint_{C'}\phi\,dz$ (both counter-clockwise). On $C'$, $z=a+\rho e^{i\theta}$:

$$\oint_{C'}\frac{f(z)}{z-a}dz=\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{f(a+\rho e^{i\theta})}{\rho e^{i\theta}}\,i\rho e^{i\theta}d\theta=i\int_0^{2\pi}f(a+\rho e^{i\theta})\,d\theta\xrightarrow{\rho\to0}2\pi i\,f(a).\ \blacksquare$$
Meaning
  • The values of an analytic function inside a curve are completely determined by its values on the curve. (A real smooth function has no such property.)
  • Mean-value property: taking $C$ to be a circle, $f(a)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(a+\rho e^{i\theta})d\theta$. The value at the centre is the average over the circle. The same holds for harmonic $u$ and $v$, so they cannot have a maximum or minimum inside a region.

Derivatives (Boas Problem 21)

Differentiating (3.10) with respect to $z$ under the integral sign, $n$ times:

$$\boxed{f^{(n)}(a)=\frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)\,dz}{(z-a)^{n+1}}}$$

This proves Theorem III (an analytic function has derivatives of all orders), and it evaluates integrals with higher-power denominators.

How to use the formulas: the checklist

  1. Write the integrand as $\dfrac{g(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}$ with $g$ analytic inside $C$. Pull out constants: $\frac{1}{2z-\pi}=\frac12\cdot\frac1{z-\pi/2}$, $\frac1{2\ln2-z}=-\frac{1}{z-2\ln2}$.
  2. Check whether $a$ is inside $C$. If not, the answer is $0$.
  3. Answer: $\dfrac{2\pi i}{n!}\,g^{(n)}(a)$.

Dass's proof of $f(a)=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{z-a}dz$ (§24.5)

  1. $\frac{f(z)}{z-a}$ is analytic everywhere inside $C$ except at $a$. Draw a small circle $C_1$ of radius $r$ about $a$. By §24.4, $\oint_C\frac{f\,dz}{z-a}=\oint_{C_1}\frac{f\,dz}{z-a}$.
  2. Add and subtract $f(a)$: $\oint_{C_1}\frac{f(z)}{z-a}dz=\oint_{C_1}\frac{f(z)-f(a)}{z-a}dz+f(a)\oint_{C_1}\frac{dz}{z-a}$.
  3. On $C_1$, $z=a+re^{i\theta}$. The first integral becomes $i\int_0^{2\pi}\left[f(a+re^{i\theta})-f(a)\right]d\theta\to0$ as $r\to0$, by continuity of $f$.
  4. The second is $f(a)\cdot2\pi i$ (Dass Ex 10 with $n=-1$). Hence $\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{z-a}dz=2\pi i\,f(a)$. ∎

Derivatives (§24.6)

Differentiate under the integral sign with respect to $a$: $\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\frac1{z-a}=\frac1{(z-a)^2}$, $\frac{\partial^2}{\partial a^2}\frac1{z-a}=\frac{2!}{(z-a)^3}$, and so on. This gives

$$f'(a)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^2}dz,\qquad f''(a)=\frac{2!}{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^3}dz,\qquad f^{(n)}(a)=\frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}dz .$$

So the derivative of an analytic function is itself analytic, and so are all higher derivatives.

Morera's theorem: the converse of Cauchy (§24.7)

Morera

If $f$ is continuous in a region $D$ and $\oint_Cf\,dz=0$ for every simple closed contour $C$ in $D$, then $f$ is analytic in $D$.

  1. Since closed integrals vanish, $\phi(z)=\int_{z_0}^zf(z)\,dz$ is independent of the path, so it is a genuine function of $z$. Write $\phi=U+iV$ with $U=\int(u\,dx-v\,dy)$ and $V=\int(v\,dx+u\,dy)$.
  2. Differentiating the line integrals: $U_x=u$, $U_y=-v$, $V_x=v$, $V_y=u$. So $U_x=V_y$ and $U_y=-V_x$: Cauchy–Riemann holds, with continuous partials.
  3. So $\phi$ is analytic with $\phi'=U_x+iV_x=u+iv=f$. Since the derivative of an analytic function is analytic (§24.6), $f$ is analytic. ∎

Cauchy's inequality (§24.8)

If $f$ is analytic inside and on $|z-a|=R$, and $|f|\le M$ on that circle, then

$$|f^{(n)}(a)|\le\frac{n!\,M}{R^n}.$$

Proof. $|f^{(n)}(a)|=\left|\frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f(z)\,dz}{(z-a)^{n+1}}\right|\le\frac{n!}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{M}{R^{n+1}}\cdot2\pi R=\frac{n!M}{R^n}$, using $|dz|=R\,d\theta$. ∎

Liouville's theorem (§24.9): two proofs

Liouville

If $f$ is analytic for all finite $z$ (it is entire) and bounded, $|f|\le M$, then $f$ is constant.

Proof 1 (Dass's main proof). Take any two points $z_1,z_2$ and a large circle $|z|=R$ enclosing both. Subtracting the two Cauchy formulas:

$$f(z_1)-f(z_2)=\frac{z_1-z_2}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f(z)\,dz}{(z-z_1)(z-z_2)},\qquad|f(z_1)-f(z_2)|\le\frac{|z_1-z_2|\,M\cdot2\pi R}{2\pi(R-|z_1|)(R-|z_2|)}\xrightarrow{R\to\infty}0 .$$

So $f(z_1)=f(z_2)$ for every pair of points.

Proof 2 (from the inequality). With $n=1$: $|f'(a)|\le\frac{M}{R}\to0$ as $R\to\infty$, so $f'\equiv0$.

Generalisation (same proof). If $|f(z)|\le M|z|^k$ for large $|z|$, then $|f^{(n)}(a)|\lesssim\frac{n!\,MR^k}{R^n}\to0$ for $n\gt k$. So $f$ is a polynomial of degree at most $k$.

Fundamental theorem of algebra (§24.10)

Every polynomial $p(z)$ of degree $\ge1$ has a root in $\mathbb C$. Proof: if $p(z)\ne0$ everywhere, then $\frac1{p(z)}$ is entire. Since $|p(z)|\to\infty$ as $|z|\to\infty$, $\frac1{p}$ is also bounded. By Liouville $\frac1p$ is constant, so $p$ is constant: a contradiction. ∎ (Applying this repeatedly, a degree-$n$ polynomial has exactly $n$ roots counted with multiplicity.)

Poisson's integral formula for a circle (§24.11)

If $f$ is analytic inside and on $|z|=R$, and $z=re^{i\theta}$ with $r\lt R$, then

$$f(re^{i\theta})=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{(R^2-r^2)\,f(Re^{i\phi})}{R^2-2rR\cos(\theta-\phi)+r^2}\,d\phi .$$
  1. By Cauchy's formula, $f(z)=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f(w)\,dw}{w-z}$.
  2. The inverse point $\frac{R^2}{\bar z}$ lies outside the circle, so $0=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f(w)\,dw}{w-R^2/\bar z}$ by Cauchy's theorem.
  3. Subtract, put $w=Re^{i\phi}$, and simplify $\frac1{w-z}-\frac1{w-R^2/\bar z}$. The kernel becomes the real expression $\frac{R^2-r^2}{R^2-2rR\cos(\theta-\phi)+r^2}$.
  4. Taking real parts: the same formula holds for any harmonic $u$. It gives $u$ inside the disk from its values on the boundary (the Dirichlet problem).
Worked example (Dass Ex 18, corrected) — $\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{e^{\cos\phi}\cos(\sin\phi)}{5-4\cos(\theta-\phi)}\,d\phi$
  1. $e^{\cos\phi}\cos(\sin\phi)=\operatorname{Re}e^{e^{i\phi}}$, i.e. the values of $u=\operatorname{Re}e^{z}$ on the unit circle.
  2. Match the kernel with $R=1$ and $r=\frac12$: $R^2-2rR\cos(\cdot)+r^2=\frac54-\cos(\cdot)=\frac{5-4\cos(\cdot)}{4}$ and $R^2-r^2=\frac34$. So Poisson reads $u\left(\tfrac12,\theta\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\frac{(3/4)\,u(1,\phi)}{(5-4\cos)/4}d\phi=\frac{3}{2\pi}\int\frac{u(1,\phi)}{5-4\cos(\theta-\phi)}d\phi$.
  3. Hence $\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{e^{\cos\phi}\cos(\sin\phi)}{5-4\cos(\theta-\phi)}d\phi=\frac{2\pi}{3}\,u\!\left(\tfrac12,\theta\right)=\boxed{\frac{2\pi}{3}\,e^{\frac12\cos\theta}\cos\!\left(\tfrac12\sin\theta\right)}$.

Note on the printed result. The book states the right side as $\frac{2\pi}3e^{\cos\theta}\cos(\sin\theta)$. The book matches $R=2$, $r=1$ and treats $e^{\cos\phi}\cos(\sin\phi)$ as the values of $\operatorname{Re}e^z$ on $|z|=2$. But on $|z|=2$, $\operatorname{Re}e^z=e^{2\cos\phi}\cos(2\sin\phi)$, so that matching does not fit the given data. Numerically, at $\theta=0.3$ the integral is $3.3400$, which agrees with $\frac{2\pi}3e^{0.5\cos0.3}\cos(0.5\sin0.3)=3.3400$, while $\frac{2\pi}3e^{\cos0.3}\cos(\sin0.3)=5.2085$.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: spotting "$a-b\cos(\theta-\phi)$ in the denominator means Poisson, with $\frac{R^2+r^2}{2rR}=\frac ab$" turns a nasty real integral into evaluating a known function at a point.

Poisson's integral formula for the upper half-plane (§24.12)

If $f$ is analytic in $\operatorname{Im}z\ge0$ (and decays suitably at infinity), then at $a=\alpha+i\beta$ with $\beta\gt0$:

$$f(\alpha+i\beta)=\frac1\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\beta\,f(x)}{(x-\alpha)^2+\beta^2}\,dx .$$
  1. Close the real segment $[-R,R]$ with the upper semicircle $C_R$. Cauchy's formula gives $f(a)=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f\,dz}{z-a}$. The mirror point $\bar a$ is outside, so $0=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f\,dz}{z-\bar a}$.
  2. Subtract: $\frac1{z-a}-\frac1{z-\bar a}=\frac{a-\bar a}{(z-a)(z-\bar a)}=\frac{2i\beta}{(z-\alpha)^2+\beta^2}$. So $f(a)=\frac1\pi\oint\frac{\beta f(z)\,dz}{(z-\alpha)^2+\beta^2}$.
  3. Let $R\to\infty$: the arc contributes nothing, leaving the real-axis integral. Taking real and imaginary parts gives the same formula for the harmonic functions $u$ and $v$.
Worked example (Dass Ex 19) — the harmonic function in $y\gt0$ with boundary values $1$ for $x\lt-1$, $0$ for $|x|\lt1$, $-1$ for $x\gt1$
  1. $\phi(x,y)=\frac1\pi\left[\int_{-\infty}^{-1}\frac{y\,dw}{(w-x)^2+y^2}-\int_1^\infty\frac{y\,dw}{(w-x)^2+y^2}\right]$, using $\int\frac{y\,dw}{(w-x)^2+y^2}=\tan^{-1}\frac{w-x}{y}$.
  2. So $\phi=\frac1\pi\left[\left(\frac\pi2-\tan^{-1}\frac{1+x}{y}\right)-\left(\frac\pi2-\tan^{-1}\frac{1-x}{y}\right)\right]=\boxed{\frac1\pi\left[\tan^{-1}\frac{1-x}{y}-\tan^{-1}\frac{1+x}{y}\right]}$.
  3. Combining with the subtraction formula gives Dass's form $\frac1\pi\tan^{-1}\frac{2xy}{x^2-y^2-1}$. Take care: this single-arctangent form is only correct with the branch chosen continuously. In the region $x^2-y^2\gt1$ the principal $\tan^{-1}$ is off by $\pm\pi$ (e.g. it gives $\approx0$ instead of $1$ near the boundary at $x=-3$). The two-arctangent form is always right.
  4. Check: as $y\to0^+$ with $x\lt-1$, both arguments tend to $\pm\infty$ with opposite signs, so $\phi\to\frac1\pi\left(\frac\pi2+\frac\pi2\right)=1$ ✓.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: piecewise-constant boundary data on a line always gives a combination of angles $\frac1\pi\arg(z-x_k)$. Recognising this shortcut answers the "potential above a split plate" questions instantly.

9. Going deeper: from Cauchy's formula to Liouville, the fundamental theorem of algebra, and maximum modulus

1. Cauchy's estimates

Take the derivative formula $f^{(n)}(z_0)=\frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z-z_0|=R}\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^{n+1}}dz$. Suppose $|f|\le M$ on the circle. The ML estimate (length $2\pi R$, integrand at most $M/R^{n+1}$) gives

$$\boxed{|f^{(n)}(z_0)|\le\frac{n!\,M}{R^n}}$$

2. Liouville's theorem

If $f$ is entire and $|f|\le M$ everywhere, the estimate with $n=1$ holds for every $R$: $|f'(z_0)|\le M/R\to0$. So $f'\equiv0$ and $f$ is constant. More generally, if $|f(z)|\le C|z|^k$ for large $|z|$, then $f^{(n)}(z_0)=0$ for $n\gt k$, so $f$ is a polynomial of degree at most $k$.

3. Fundamental theorem of algebra

Let $p$ be a non-constant polynomial with no zero. Then $1/p$ is entire, and $1/p\to0$ as $|z|\to\infty$, so it is bounded. By Liouville $1/p$ would be constant, a contradiction. So every non-constant polynomial has a root in $\mathbb C$. This is the result quoted in 2.1.

4. Mean-value property and maximum modulus

Put $z=z_0+re^{i\theta}$ in Cauchy's formula:

$$f(z_0)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(z_0+re^{i\theta})\,d\theta .$$

The value at the centre is the average over any circle. An average cannot exceed every value it averages unless they are all equal. So $|f|$ has no interior maximum unless $f$ is constant. On a closed bounded region, $\max|f|$ is attained on the boundary. The real part $u$ inherits the same property: harmonic functions have their extrema on the boundary, which is the basis of uniqueness for Laplace's equation.

13. Summary

  • $\int_Cf\,dz=\int f(z(t))z'(t)\,dt$. With an analytic $f$ in a simply connected region, use an antiderivative (the result is path-independent).
  • $\oint(z-a)^ndz=2\pi i$ for $n=-1$, and $0$ otherwise.
  • Cauchy: $\oint_Cf\,dz=0$ if $f$ is analytic on and inside $C$. Deform contours freely around singularities.
  • $f^{(n)}(a)=\frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f\,dz}{(z-a)^{n+1}}$ for $a$ inside, and $0$ if $a$ is outside.
