14.3 Contour Integrals

Boas, Chapter 14, Section 3 · integrating $f(z)$ along paths, path independence, Cauchy's theorem (Theorem V), Cauchy's integral formula (Theorem VI) and its derivatives, and their surprising consequences.

Goals
  • Evaluate $\int_Cf(z)\,dz$ directly by parametrising the path, or with an antiderivative when allowed.
  • Know exactly when an integral is path-independent and when a closed integral vanishes.
  • Use Cauchy's integral formula and its derivative form to evaluate closed integrals in seconds.
  • Understand the one integral everything rests on: $\oint\frac{dz}{z-a}=2\pi i$.
  • Solve all 24 of Boas's Section 3 problems (all checked by numerical integration).

1. What a contour integral is

A contour integral is a line integral in the complex plane. With $f=u+iv$ and $dz=dx+i\,dy$ (Boas 3.2):

$$\int_Cf(z)\,dz=\int_C(u+iv)(dx+i\,dy)=\int_C(u\,dx-v\,dy)+i\int_C(v\,dx+u\,dy).$$

So it is two real line integrals of the kind in vector analysis (Chapter 6).

Working method: parametrise

If the path is $z=z(t)$, $a\le t\le b$, then

$$\boxed{\int_Cf(z)\,dz=\int_a^bf\big(z(t)\big)\,z'(t)\,dt}$$
PathParametrisation$dz$
straight segment from $p$ to $q$$z=p+(q-p)t$, $0\le t\le1$$(q-p)\,dt$

The most important integral in the chapter

On the circle $z=a+\rho e^{i\theta}$:

$$\oint(z-a)^n\,dz=\int_0^{2\pi}\rho^ne^{in\theta}\,i\rho e^{i\theta}\,d\theta=i\rho^{n+1}\int_0^{2\pi}e^{i(n+1)\theta}d\theta=\begin{cases}2\pi i,&n=-1\\0,&n\ne-1\end{cases}$$

(This is Boas Problem 5.1.) Every power of $(z-a)$ integrates to zero around $a$, except $\frac{1}{z-a}$, which gives $2\pi i$ for every radius. This one fact is the seed of Cauchy's formula and of the residue theorem.

The ML estimate (bound on any contour integral)

$$\left|\int_Cf(z)\,dz\right|\le M\cdot L,\qquad M=\max_C|f|,\quad L=\text{length of }C .$$

It is used constantly in 14.7 to show that integrals over big semicircles vanish.

2. Path independence and antiderivatives

When can you just use an antiderivative?

If $f$ is analytic in a simply connected region containing the path, and $F'=f$ there, then

$$\int_{z_1}^{z_2}f(z)\,dz=F(z_2)-F(z_1),$$

independent of the path. Example (Boas 3.2): $\int_0^{1+i}(z^2-z)\,dz=\left[\frac{z^3}3-\frac{z^2}2\right]_0^{1+i}=-\frac23-\frac i3$ along any path.

When you can NOT
  • $f$ not analytic (contains $\bar z$, $|z|$, …): the value depends on the path. Boas 3.12: $\int_0^{1+2i}|z|^2dz$ is $\frac53(1+2i)$ along the straight line but $\frac{13}3+\frac83i$ along the broken line.
  • The region has a hole (a singularity) between two paths: e.g. $\int\frac{dz}{z}$ from $1$ to $-1$ gives $i\pi$ along the upper semicircle and $-i\pi$ along the lower one. Their difference, $2\pi i$, is the closed integral around $0$.

3. Cauchy's theorem (Theorem V)

Theorem V — Cauchy's theorem

Let $C$ be a simple closed curve (it does not cross itself; finitely many corners are allowed). If $f(z)$ is analytic on and inside $C$, then

$$\oint_Cf(z)\,dz=0.\qquad(3.1)$$

Proof (assuming $f'$ is continuous). By Green's theorem in the plane (3.3), $\oint_C(P\,dx+Q\,dy)=\iint\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)dx\,dy$. Applied to the two real integrals in (3.2):

$$\oint(u\,dx-v\,dy)=\iint\left(-v_x-u_y\right)dx\,dy=0,\qquad\oint(v\,dx+u\,dy)=\iint\left(u_x-v_y\right)dx\,dy=0,$$

because both integrands vanish by the Cauchy–Riemann equations. $\blacksquare$ (Boas Problem 13 is the converse view: path independence needs CR, just as curl-free fields need $\partial F_y/\partial x=\partial F_x/\partial y$.)

Deformation of contours

A consequence used constantly: you may deform a contour freely as long as you do not pass it across a singular point; the integral does not change. In particular, an integral around a big complicated curve enclosing one singularity equals the integral around a tiny circle about it (Boas eq. 3.6).

4. Cauchy's integral formula (Theorem VI)

Theorem VI

If $f$ is analytic on and inside a simple closed curve $C$ and $a$ is a point inside $C$, then

$$\boxed{f(a)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{z-a}\,dz}\qquad(3.9)$$

or, renaming variables, $f(z)=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(w)}{w-z}\,dw$ (3.10). If $a$ is outside $C$, the integral is $0$ (Cauchy's theorem).

Proof. $\phi(z)=\frac{f(z)}{z-a}$ is analytic except at $a$. Cut out a small circle $C'$ of radius $\rho$ about $a$ and join it to $C$ by a cut (Boas Fig. 3.1). Cauchy's theorem on the region between gives $\oint_C\phi\,dz=\oint_{C'}\phi\,dz$ (both counter-clockwise). On $C'$, $z=a+\rho e^{i\theta}$:

$$\oint_{C'}\frac{f(z)}{z-a}dz=\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{f(a+\rho e^{i\theta})}{\rho e^{i\theta}}\,i\rho e^{i\theta}d\theta=i\int_0^{2\pi}f(a+\rho e^{i\theta})\,d\theta\xrightarrow{\rho\to0}2\pi i\,f(a).\ \blacksquare$$
Meaning
  • The values of an analytic function inside a curve are completely determined by its values on the curve. (A real smooth function has no such property.)
  • Mean-value property: taking $C$ to be a circle, $f(a)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(a+\rho e^{i\theta})d\theta$. The value at the centre is the average over the circle. The same holds for harmonic $u$ and $v$, so they cannot have a maximum or minimum inside a region.

Derivatives (Boas Problem 21)

Differentiating (3.10) with respect to $z$ under the integral sign, $n$ times:

$$\boxed{f^{(n)}(a)=\frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)\,dz}{(z-a)^{n+1}}}$$

This proves Theorem III (an analytic function has derivatives of all orders), and it evaluates integrals with higher-power denominators.

How to use the formulas: the checklist

  1. Write the integrand as $\dfrac{g(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}$ with $g$ analytic inside $C$. Pull out constants: $\frac{1}{2z-\pi}=\frac12\cdot\frac1{z-\pi/2}$, $\frac1{2\ln2-z}=-\frac{1}{z-2\ln2}$.
  2. Check whether $a$ is inside $C$. If not, the answer is $0$.
  3. Answer: $\dfrac{2\pi i}{n!}\,g^{(n)}(a)$.

7. Extra practice (exam level)

P1. $\oint_{|z|=2}\dfrac{e^z}{z^3}dz$. Easy
Show solution

$\frac{2\pi i}{2!}(e^z)''\big|_0=\pi i$.

P2. $\oint_{|z-i|=1}\dfrac{dz}{z^2+1}$. Easy
Show solution

$\frac{1}{(z-i)(z+i)}$: only $z=i$ is inside. With $g=\frac1{z+i}$: $2\pi i\cdot\frac1{2i}=\pi$.

P3. $\oint_{|z|=3}\dfrac{dz}{z^2+1}$. Medium
Show solution

Both $\pm i$ are inside. Partial fractions: $\frac1{z^2+1}=\frac1{2i}\left(\frac{1}{z-i}-\frac1{z+i}\right)$, so the integral is $\frac1{2i}(2\pi i-2\pi i)=0$. (Equivalently, deform $C$ to a huge circle where $|f|\sim1/R^2$ and use the ML estimate.)

P4. Use the ML estimate to bound $\left|\int_{C_R}\frac{dz}{z^2+4}\right|$ on the upper semicircle $|z|=R>2$, and show it tends to $0$. Medium
Show solution

On $|z|=R$: $|z^2+4|\ge R^2-4$, so $M\le\frac1{R^2-4}$ and $L=\pi R$. Hence $|\int|\le\frac{\pi R}{R^2-4}\to0$.

P5. Evaluate $\oint_{|z|=1}\dfrac{\cos z}{z^{2n+1}}dz$. Medium
Show solution

$\frac{2\pi i}{(2n)!}\cos^{(2n)}(0)=\frac{2\pi i(-1)^n}{(2n)!}$.

9. Going deeper: from Cauchy's formula to Liouville, the fundamental theorem of algebra, and maximum modulus

1. Cauchy's estimates

Take the derivative formula $f^{(n)}(z_0)=\frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z-z_0|=R}\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^{n+1}}dz$. Suppose $|f|\le M$ on the circle. The ML estimate (length $2\pi R$, integrand at most $M/R^{n+1}$) gives

$$\boxed{|f^{(n)}(z_0)|\le\frac{n!\,M}{R^n}}$$

2. Liouville's theorem

If $f$ is entire and $|f|\le M$ everywhere, the estimate with $n=1$ holds for every $R$: $|f'(z_0)|\le M/R\to0$. So $f'\equiv0$ and $f$ is constant. More generally, if $|f(z)|\le C|z|^k$ for large $|z|$, then $f^{(n)}(z_0)=0$ for $n\gt k$, so $f$ is a polynomial of degree at most $k$.

3. Fundamental theorem of algebra

Let $p$ be a non-constant polynomial with no zero. Then $1/p$ is entire, and $1/p\to0$ as $|z|\to\infty$, so it is bounded. By Liouville $1/p$ would be constant, a contradiction. So every non-constant polynomial has a root in $\mathbb C$. This is the result quoted in 2.1.

4. Mean-value property and maximum modulus

Put $z=z_0+re^{i\theta}$ in Cauchy's formula:

$$f(z_0)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(z_0+re^{i\theta})\,d\theta .$$

The value at the centre is the average over any circle. An average cannot exceed every value it averages unless they are all equal. So $|f|$ has no interior maximum unless $f$ is constant. On a closed bounded region, $\max|f|$ is attained on the boundary. The real part $u$ inherits the same property: harmonic functions have their extrema on the boundary, which is the basis of uniqueness for Laplace's equation.

13. Summary

  • $\int_Cf\,dz=\int f(z(t))z'(t)\,dt$. With an analytic $f$ in a simply connected region, use an antiderivative (the result is path-independent).
  • $\oint(z-a)^ndz=2\pi i$ for $n=-1$, and $0$ otherwise.
  • Cauchy: $\oint_Cf\,dz=0$ if $f$ is analytic on and inside $C$. Deform contours freely around singularities.
  • $f^{(n)}(a)=\frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f\,dz}{(z-a)^{n+1}}$ for $a$ inside, and $0$ if $a$ is outside.
