14.10 Some Applications of Conformal Mapping

Boas, Chapter 14, Section 10 · solving Laplace's equation in awkward regions by mapping them to simple ones: steady heat flow, electrostatics, and ideal fluid flow, with complex potentials, stream functions and the complex velocity.

Goals
  • Understand why conformal mapping solves boundary-value problems for Laplace's equation.
  • Solve temperature, potential and flow problems with $w=\ln z$, $z^2$, $1/z$, $z+\frac1z$, $\sin z$, $\ln\frac{z+1}{z-1}$, $w+e^w$.
  • Use the complex potential $\Phi+i\Psi$, streamlines, equipotentials, and the complex velocity $\overline{V}=\frac{d(\Phi+i\Psi)}{dz}$.
  • Solve all of Boas's Section 10 problems (1–17).

1. The idea

Many 2-D physical quantities satisfy Laplace's equation $\phi_{xx}+\phi_{yy}=0$ in regions with no sources:

The key theorem (Problem 1)

If $\phi(u,v)$ is harmonic and $w=f(z)=u+iv$ is analytic, then $\phi\big(u(x,y),v(x,y)\big)$ is harmonic in $x,y$.

Proof: $\phi$ is the real part of some analytic $g(w)$ (Theorem IV). Then $h(z)=g(f(z))$ is analytic (chain rule), so $\operatorname{Re}h=\phi(u(x,y),v(x,y))$ is harmonic.

Method: find a map $w=f(z)$ that carries the hard region (in $z$) to a simple region (in $w$) where the answer is known, such as a slab where $T$ is linear. Then substitute $u(x,y)$, $v(x,y)$. The boundary values carry over, because a point and its image have the same temperature. Insulated boundaries ($\partial T/\partial n=0$) also stay insulated, because normal derivatives just scale by $|dw/dz|$ (Problem 9.15). Isotherms map to isotherms, and flow lines to flow lines.

2. Heat flow (Boas Examples 1–2)

Example 1 — the slab

A plate $0\le v\le\pi$ (in the $w$-plane) with $T=0$ at $v=0$, $T=100$ at $v=\pi$, and insulated ends. Then $T=\frac{100}{\pi}v$. It is harmonic because $v=\operatorname{Im}w$ is the imaginary part of an analytic function.

Example 2 — a semicircular plate, via $w=\ln z$
  1. $w=\ln z=\ln r+i\theta$, so $u=\ln r$ and $v=\theta$ (10.1).
  2. $v=0$ is the positive $x$-axis, and $v=\pi$ the negative $x$-axis. The end $u=0$ becomes the circle $r=1$, and $u\to-\infty$ the origin (a bit of insulation there).
  3. So we have solved: a semicircular plate with its curved edge insulated, half of the flat edge at $0$ and half at $100$:
$$T=\frac{100}{\pi}\theta=\frac{100}{\pi}\arctan\frac yx\qquad(10.2)$$

The isotherms are the rays $\theta=$const. Heat flows along the circles $r=$const (the images of $u=$const).

3. Electrostatics (Example 3)

The same map solves a 3-D problem (all cross sections identical). In the $w$-plane, two parallel plates at $V=0$ and $V=100$. In the $z$-plane, one plane whose right half is at $0$ V and left half at $100$ V:

$$V=\frac{100}{\pi}\theta=\frac{100}{\pi}\arctan\frac yx .$$

The equipotentials are the rays $\theta=$const. The electric field $\mathbf E=-\nabla V$ is perpendicular to them, so the field lines are semicircles $r=$const.

4. Fluid flow: complex potential and stream function (Example 4)

Complex potential

For an ideal 2-D flow, $\mathbf V=\nabla\Phi$ with $\nabla^2\Phi=0$. Its harmonic conjugate $\Psi$ is the stream function. The analytic function $\Omega(z)=\Phi+i\Psi$ is the complex potential.

  • Streamlines: $\Psi=$const. The fluid moves along them, and any boundary must be a streamline.
  • Equipotentials: $\Phi=$const, orthogonal to the streamlines.
  • Complex velocity (Problem 8): $\dfrac{d\Omega}{dz}=\Phi_x+i\Psi_x=\Phi_x-i\Phi_y=V_x-iV_y$, so $|\mathbf V|=\left|\dfrac{d\Omega}{dz}\right|$. Points with $\Omega'=0$ are stagnation points.
Example 4 — channel flow → a source, via $w=\ln z$
  1. In the $w$-plane: uniform flow $V_0$ along the channel $0\lt v\lt2\pi$, with $\Omega=V_0w$.
  2. In the $z$-plane: $\Omega=V_0\ln z=V_0(\ln r+i\theta)$. The streamlines are rays (from a source at $0$), and the equipotentials are circles.
  3. $\mathbf V=\nabla(V_0\ln r)=\frac{V_0}{r}\mathbf e_r$: the speed falls off like $\frac1r$, as conservation of fluid demands.
  4. Swap the roles (streamlines = circles): a vortex. The same picture describes the magnetic field around a wire (circles) and the electric field of a line charge (rays).

H.K. Dass §22.16–22.17Flow problems: velocity potential, stream function, complex potential

Setting (§22.17). Consider a two-dimensional, irrotational flow of an incompressible fluid, with velocity $\vec V=v_x\hat i+v_y\hat j$.

  1. Irrotational ⇒ there is a scalar velocity potential $\phi(x,y)$ with $\vec V=\nabla\phi$, i.e. $v_x=\phi_x$ and $v_y=\phi_y$.
  2. Incompressible ⇒ $\nabla\cdot\vec V=0$, i.e. $\partial_xv_x+\partial_yv_y=0$, so $\phi_{xx}+\phi_{yy}=0$: $\phi$ is harmonic (§22.16).
  3. So $\phi$ is the real part of an analytic complex potential $w=f(z)=\phi+i\psi$. Here $\psi$ is the stream function.
  4. By Cauchy–Riemann, $\frac{dw}{dz}=\phi_x+i\psi_x=\phi_x-i\phi_y=v_x-iv_y$. So the speed is $|\vec V|=\left|\frac{dw}{dz}\right|=\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}$.

Worked example (Dass §22.18) — the electrostatic potential $\phi=3x^2y-y^3$: find the stream function
  1. $d\psi=\psi_x\,dx+\psi_y\,dy=-\phi_y\,dx+\phi_x\,dy=-(3x^2-3y^2)\,dx+6xy\,dy$.
  2. $=-d(x^3)+3\,d(xy^2)$, so $\psi=-x^3+3xy^2+c$.
  3. Together, $w=\phi+i\psi=-iz^3$. Check: $-iz^3=-i(x^3+3ix^2y-3xy^2-iy^3)=(3x^2y-y^3)+i(-x^3+3xy^2)$. ✓

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: given a potential, the field lines are the level curves of the harmonic conjugate. That is one integration of an exact differential.

Worked example (Dass §22.18) — $\psi=x^2-y^2+\dfrac{x}{x^2+y^2}$: find $\phi$
  1. $\psi_x=2x+\frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}$ and $\psi_y=-2y-\frac{2xy}{(x^2+y^2)^2}$.
  2. $d\phi=\phi_x\,dx+\phi_y\,dy=\psi_y\,dx-\psi_x\,dy$. Integrating: $\phi=-2xy+\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}+C$.
  3. Equivalently $w=\phi+i\psi=i\left(z^2+\frac1z\right)$: $\operatorname{Im}(iz^2)=x^2-y^2$ and $\operatorname{Im}\frac iz=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}$. ✓

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: recognising pieces like $\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}=\operatorname{Re}\frac1z$ and $x^2-y^2=\operatorname{Re}z^2$ lets you write $w$ down without integrating.

5. Example 5 — water leaving a channel / fringing of a capacitor

Use the map $z=w+e^w$ (10.3): $x=u+e^u\cos v$, $y=v+e^u\sin v$.

  • $v=0$ (the $u$-axis) maps to the whole $x$-axis.
  • $v=\pi$: $y=\pi$, $x=u-e^u$, which has a maximum $x=-1$ at $u=0$. So the line $v=\pi$ maps to the half-line $y=\pi$, $x\le-1$, traversed out and then back (folded back at $x=-1$). Similarly for $v=-\pi$.
  • So the strip $-\pi\lt v\lt\pi$ (uniform flow) maps to the whole plane outside two semi-infinite plates $y=\pm\pi$, $x\le-1$.

Flow: water comes down the channel from $x=-\infty$, leaves at $x=-1$, and spreads over the whole plane, even back along the outsides of the walls. Capacitor: with the plates at different potentials, $v=$const are the equipotentials and $u=$const the field lines, which bulge out at the ends. This is fringing (Fig. 10.5). H.K. Dass §23.23The Schwarz–Christoffel transformation: the upper half-plane onto a polygon

The problem. Laplace problems are easy in the upper half-plane. Real boundaries, however, are polygons: channels, steps, slots, the inside of a triangle. The Schwarz–Christoffel (SC) formula builds the conformal map from the upper half $z$-plane onto the interior of a polygon in the $w$-plane. The real axis goes to the sides of the polygon.

Schwarz–Christoffel formula

Let the polygon have vertices $w_1,\dots,w_n$ with interior angles $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n$. Let these vertices be the images of real points $x_1\lt x_2\lt\dots\lt x_n$. Then

$$\frac{dw}{dz}=A\,(z-x_1)^{\frac{\alpha_1}{\pi}-1}(z-x_2)^{\frac{\alpha_2}{\pi}-1}\cdots(z-x_n)^{\frac{\alpha_n}{\pi}-1},\qquad w=A\int(z-x_1)^{\frac{\alpha_1}{\pi}-1}\cdots(z-x_n)^{\frac{\alpha_n}{\pi}-1}\,dz+B.$$

$A$ (complex) fixes the size and orientation, and $B$ the position. Any three of the $x_k$ may be chosen freely. A vertex sent to $z=\infty$ is simply left out of the product.

Why it works (Dass's proof)

  1. Take arguments: $\arg dw=\arg dz+\arg A+\sum_k\left(\frac{\alpha_k}{\pi}-1\right)\arg(z-x_k)$.
  2. Let $z$ move along the real axis from left to right, so $dz\gt0$ and $\arg dz=0$. Between two consecutive $x_k$, every $\arg(z-x_k)$ is constant: it is $\pi$ for the points still ahead of $z$ and $0$ for those already passed. So $\arg dw$ is constant and $w$ moves along a straight line, one side of the polygon.
  3. When $z$ passes $x_k$, only $\arg(z-x_k)$ changes, from $\pi$ to $0$. So $\arg dw$ changes by $-\left(\frac{\alpha_k}{\pi}-1\right)\pi=\pi-\alpha_k$: the direction of travel turns anticlockwise by $\pi-\alpha_k$, the exterior angle at $w_k$. The next side starts there.
  4. So the real axis is traced as the closed polygon. The exterior angles add up to $\sum(\pi-\alpha_k)=2\pi$, as they must. One can also show that the upper half-plane goes to the interior.

All the cases you will meet.

  • Finite vertex, convex corner ($0\lt\alpha_k\lt\pi$): the exponent lies in $(-1,0)$, so the integrand has a mild singularity at $x_k$.
  • Re-entrant corner ($\pi\lt\alpha_k\le2\pi$): the exponent is positive. $\alpha_k=2\pi$ is the tip of a slit or a thin plate: the factor is $(z-x_k)^{+1}$.
  • Vertex at $w=\infty$ (a semi-infinite strip or channel): the "interior angle" is $0$, the exponent is $-1$, and the factor $(z-x_k)^{-1}$ integrates to a logarithm.
  • Vertex sent to $z=\infty$: drop its factor. This is how Dass handles the third vertex in Ex 30 and Ex 31.

Worked example (Dass Ex 30) — the semi-infinite strip $u\gt0$, $0\lt v\lt\pi$ onto the upper half $z$-plane
  1. Treat the strip as a "triangle" with vertices $Q=\pi i$ and $R=0$, each with interior angle $\frac\pi2$, and the third vertex at $w=\infty$ (angle $0$) sent to $z=\infty$. Put $Q\leftrightarrow z=-1$ and $R\leftrightarrow z=1$.
  2. SC: $\dfrac{dw}{dz}=A(z+1)^{\frac12-1}(z-1)^{\frac12-1}=\dfrac{A}{\sqrt{z^2-1}}$. So $w=A\cosh^{-1}z+B$.
  3. $R$: $w=0$ at $z=1$ gives $0=A\cosh^{-1}1+B=B$, so $B=0$.
  4. $Q$: $w=\pi i$ at $z=-1$, and $\cosh^{-1}(-1)=\pi i$, so $A=1$.
  5. Result: $\boxed{w=\cosh^{-1}z}$, i.e. $z=\cosh w$. This agrees with §23.22: $\cosh$ sends the half-strip onto the upper half-plane.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: the potential in a semi-infinite slot comes straight from this map, with no Fourier series. It is also the cleanest check that SC reproduces a map you already know.

Worked example (Dass Ex 31) — the interior of a triangle with angles $\alpha$ at $A$ and $\beta$ at $B$
  1. Send $A\to z=0$, $B\to z=1$ and $C\to z=\infty$ (so $C$'s factor is dropped). Put $A$ at $w=0$ and $B$ at $w=1$.
  2. SC: $\dfrac{dw}{dz}=K\,z^{\frac\alpha\pi-1}(z-1)^{\frac\beta\pi-1}$. Integrating from $0$ (so $w(0)=0$ and the constant is $0$): $w=K\displaystyle\int_0^z t^{\frac\alpha\pi-1}(1-t)^{\frac\beta\pi-1}dt$. The $(-1)$ factor from $(z-1)$ versus $(1-z)$ is absorbed into $K$.
  3. Fix $K$ with $w(1)=1$: $1=K\displaystyle\int_0^1t^{\frac\alpha\pi-1}(1-t)^{\frac\beta\pi-1}dt=K\,B\!\left(\tfrac\alpha\pi,\tfrac\beta\pi\right)=K\,\dfrac{\Gamma(\frac\alpha\pi)\Gamma(\frac\beta\pi)}{\Gamma(\frac{\alpha+\beta}\pi)}$.
  4. So $\boxed{w=\dfrac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\pi}\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac\alpha\pi\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac\beta\pi\right)}\displaystyle\int_0^z t^{\frac\alpha\pi-1}(1-t)^{\frac\beta\pi-1}dt}$: an incomplete Beta function.
  5. Check: the third vertex is $w(\infty)=\frac{\sin\beta}{\sin(\alpha+\beta)}e^{i\alpha}$, by the sine rule on the triangle with base $[0,1]$. The widget's readout compares this with the numerical integral at a far-away point.

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: it links conformal maps with the Beta and Gamma functions (Chapter 11). Expect "the exponent of $(z-x_k)$ for an interior angle $\alpha_k$ is $\frac{\alpha_k}{\pi}-1$" as a direct question.

Worked example (Dass Exercise 23.4) — three quick SC maps

Q1: the infinite strip $0\lt v\lt\pi$. It has a single "vertex" at $w=\infty$ with angle $0$. Send it to $z=0$: $\frac{dw}{dz}=Az^{-1}$, so $w=A\log z+B$. Choosing $A=1$, $B=0$ gives $\boxed{w=\log z}$: the positive real axis goes to $v=0$ and the negative real axis to $v=\pi$.

Q2: the half-strip bounded by $v=-b$, $u=0$, $v=b$. Scale and shift Ex 30: $w=\frac{2b}{\pi}\cosh^{-1}z-bi$. The factor $\frac{2b}{\pi}$ turns height $\pi$ into $2b$, and $-bi$ moves it down.

Q3: the half-strip $-b\lt u\lt b$, $v\gt0$. This is the $\sin$ half-strip of §23.21, rescaled: $\boxed{z=\sin\dfrac{\pi w}{2b}}$.

Link to Boas Example 5 above. There, $z=w+e^w$. With $\zeta=e^w$ (the upper half $0\lt v\lt\pi$ of the strip → the upper half-plane), $z=\zeta+\log\zeta$ and $\frac{dz}{d\zeta}=\frac{\zeta+1}{\zeta}$. That is an SC derivative: a factor $(\zeta+1)^{+1}$ (angle $2\pi$, the plate tips) and $\zeta^{-1}$ (the vertex at infinity inside the channel).

Why this matters for CSIR-NET / GATE: strip ↔ half-plane via $\log$ or $e^{(\cdot)}$, and half-strip ↔ half-plane via $\sin$ or $\cosh$, cover almost every SC question that is actually asked.

6. Boas Problems, Section 10 — solutions

Show solutions 1–3 (theory)

1. See the key theorem: $g(w)=\phi+i\psi$ is analytic; $h(z)=g(f(z))$ has $h'=g'(f)f'$, so it is analytic. Hence $\operatorname{Re}h=\phi(u(x,y),v(x,y))$ is harmonic.

2. Irrotational ($\nabla\times\mathbf V=0$) gives $\mathbf V=\nabla\Phi$. Incompressible ($\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=0$, the continuity equation with constant density) gives $\nabla^2\Phi=0$.

3. $\nabla\cdot(\epsilon\mathbf E)=\rho$ and $\mathbf E=-\nabla V$ give $-\epsilon\nabla^2V=\rho=0$, so $\nabla^2V=0$.

Show solutions 4–8

4. Quarter-circle plate, $x$-axis at $0$, $y$-axis at $100$, curved edge insulated. With $w=\ln z$, the $y$-axis is $v=\frac\pi2$. In $w$: a slab $0\le v\le\frac\pi2$ with $T=\frac{200}{\pi}v$. So $T=\frac{200}{\pi}\arctan\frac yx$. Isotherms: rays $y=x\tan\frac{\pi T}{200}$. (If the edges are labelled the other way round, use $T=100-\frac{200}{\pi}\theta$.)

5. The same mathematics: $V=\frac{200}{\pi}\arctan\frac yx$, and the equipotentials are rays from the corner.

6. $w=\frac1z$ gives $v=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}$. $v=0$ is the plane $y=0$ (at $T=0$), and $v=-1$ is the circle $x^2+(y-\frac12)^2=\frac14$ (the cylinder, at $T=100$). So $T=-100v=\dfrac{100y}{x^2+y^2}$. Isotherms $T=c$: circles $x^2+y^2=\frac{100}{c}y$, tangent to the plane at the origin. Heat-flow lines ($u=$const): circles $x^2+y^2=\frac xc$, tangent to the $y$-axis at $0$.

7. $w=z^2$ maps the first quadrant to the upper half-plane, where the flow is uniform: $\Omega=V_0w=V_0z^2$. Then $\Phi=V_0(x^2-y^2)$ and $\Psi=2V_0xy$. Streamlines: hyperbolas $xy=$const. $\mathbf V=\nabla\Phi=2V_0(x,-y)$, with $|\mathbf V|=2V_0r$, so the corner is a stagnation point.

8. $V_0\frac{dw}{dz}=\frac{d}{dz}(\Phi+i\Psi)=\Phi_x+i\Psi_x\overset{CR}{=}\Phi_x-i\Phi_y=V_x-iV_y$. Taking the modulus: $|\mathbf V|=V_0\left|\frac{dw}{dz}\right|$.

Show solutions 9–11

9. $w=z+\frac1z$. On $|z|=1$, $z=e^{i\theta}$ gives $w=2\cos\theta$, real. So the $u$-axis is the image of the real axis ($|x|\ge1$) plus the unit circle: the stream bottom with a semicircular hump $ABCDE$. Uniform flow $\Omega=V_0w$. Streamlines: $\Psi=V_0\left(y-\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}\right)=$const. The hump surface ($r=1$) is the streamline $\Psi=0$. Speed $V_0\left|1-\frac1{z^2}\right|$: zero (stagnation) at $z=\pm1$, and maximal ($2V_0$) at the top of the hump.

10. $w=\sin z=\sin x\cosh y+i\cos x\sinh y$. $v=0$ when $y=0$ ($|x|\le\frac\pi2$) or $\cos x=0$ ($x=\pm\frac\pi2$, $y\ge0$). So the $u$-axis maps to the boundary of the half-strip $|x|\le\frac\pi2$, $y\ge0$. Streamlines: $\cos x\sinh y=$const, flow coming down one side and up the other.

11. $w=\ln\frac{z+1}{z-1}$: $u=\ln\frac{|z+1|}{|z-1|}$ and $v=\arg\frac{z+1}{z-1}$.

  • $u=a$: Apollonius circles with centre $(\coth a,0)$ and radius $\frac{1}{|\sinh a|}$ ($a>0$: around $+1$; $a\lt0$: around $-1$; $a=0$: the imaginary axis).
  • $v=c$: circles through $\pm1$ with centre $(0,-\cot c)$ and radius $\frac{1}{|\sin c|}$. One arc has $v=c$, the other $v=c+\pi$. $c=\frac\pi2$ gives the unit circle.

The two families are orthogonal (both checked numerically).

Show solutions 12–17 (using the circles of Problem 11)

12. Inside $|z|=1$, $v$ runs from $\frac\pi2$ (lower half of the circle) to $\frac{3\pi}{2}$ (upper half). $T$ is linear in $v$: $T=10$ at $v=\frac\pi2$ and $30$ at $v=\frac{3\pi}2$, so $T=\frac{20}{\pi}v$. In $x,y$, with $\frac{z+1}{z-1}=\frac{(x^2+y^2-1)-2iy}{|z-1|^2}$:

$$T=20+\frac{20}{\pi}\arctan\frac{2y}{1-x^2-y^2}\qquad(x^2+y^2\lt1).$$

Check: $T\to30$ at the top, $10$ at the bottom, and $20$ at the centre.

13. In the same way, $V=\frac{V_1+V_2}{2}+\frac{V_2-V_1}{\pi}\arctan\frac{2y}{1-x^2-y^2}$.

14. $\Omega=V_0\ln\frac{z+1}{z-1}$: $\Phi=V_0u$ and $\Psi=V_0v$. Streamlines are the circular arcs through $\pm1$, and the unit circle itself ($v=\frac\pi2,\frac{3\pi}2$) is a streamline, so it can be a wall. Speed $|\mathbf V|=V_0\left|\frac{1}{z+1}-\frac1{z-1}\right|=\frac{2V_0}{|z^2-1|}$.

15. Streamlines $u=$const: circles around $\pm1$, i.e. two counter-rotating vortices. Speed $\frac{2V_0}{|z^2-1|}$: at $z=0$ it is $2V_0$, at $z=i$ it is $V_0$. So the fluid is faster through the narrow neck ✔. Circular walls along $u=\pm a$ do not disturb the flow.

16. Cylinders $u=\pm a$ have centres $\pm\coth a$ and radius $r=\frac1{\sinh a}$, so $d=2\coth a$ and $\frac{d}{2r}=\cosh a$. The potential is $V=\frac{V_0}{a}u=\frac{V_0}{a}\ln\frac{|z+1|}{|z-1|}$. With charge per length $q=\frac{V_0}{2a}$: $C=\frac{q}{2V_0}=\frac1{4a}=\dfrac{1}{4\cosh^{-1}(d/2r)}$ (Gaussian units).

17. The same circles give: (a) eccentric cylindrical capacitors (two $u$-circles on the same side); (b) magnetic field lines of two antiparallel currents ($u$-circles); (c) field lines of $\pm$ line charges ($v$-arcs); (d) any flow obtained by putting walls along streamlines.

7. Extra practice

P1. Find the steady temperature in the wedge $0\lt\theta\lt\alpha$ with $T=T_1$ on $\theta=0$ and $T=T_2$ on $\theta=\alpha$. Easy
Show solution

$T=T_1+(T_2-T_1)\frac\theta\alpha$, using $w=\ln z$.

P2. Uniform flow $U$ past a cylinder of radius $a$: find the stagnation points and the maximum speed. Medium
Show solution

$\Omega=U\left(z+\frac{a^2}z\right)$, so $\Omega'=U\left(1-\frac{a^2}{z^2}\right)=0$ at $z=\pm a$. On the cylinder $z=ae^{i\theta}$: $|\Omega'|=U|1-e^{-2i\theta}|=2U|\sin\theta|$, maximum $2U$ at the top and bottom.

P3. Potential between concentric cylinders $r=a$ ($V_a$) and $r=b$ ($V_b$). Easy
Show solution

$V=A\ln r+B=\operatorname{Re}(A\ln z)+B$, with $V=V_a+(V_b-V_a)\frac{\ln(r/a)}{\ln(b/a)}$.

8. Going deeper: the complex potential toolkit

For any 2-D field that is both irrotational and divergence-free, there is an analytic complex potential $\Omega(z)=\Phi+i\Psi$. Its two parts have separate roles:

  • $\Phi$ is the potential: the velocity potential, the electrostatic potential, or the temperature.
  • $\Psi$ is the stream (flux) function. Its level curves are the field lines, and they are orthogonal to the equipotentials.

The field itself comes from the derivative. By Cauchy–Riemann, $\Omega'=\Phi_x+i\Psi_x=\Phi_x-i\Phi_y$, so

$$\boxed{\vec v=\nabla\Phi\ \longleftrightarrow\ v_x+iv_y=\overline{\Omega'(z)}},\qquad|\vec v|=|\Omega'(z)| .$$

(In electrostatics $\vec E=-\nabla\Phi$, so $E_x+iE_y=-\overline{\Omega'}$.) Stagnation points are the zeros of $\Omega'$.

Why superposition plus mapping solves boundary-value problems

If $w=f(z)$ is conformal and $\Phi(u,v)$ is harmonic, then $\Phi(u(x,y),v(x,y))$ is harmonic too, because the composition of analytic maps is analytic. Boundary values are carried along with the boundary. So: map a hard region onto an easy one (a half-plane, strip or disk), write down the easy solution, and pull it back. Adding a vortex $\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi i}\ln z$ to the cylinder flow gives a lift force $\rho U\Gamma$ per unit length (Kutta–Joukowski).

Traps that cost marks
  • The velocity is the conjugate $\overline{\Omega'}$, not $\Omega'$. The speed is the same either way, but the direction is not.
  • The source and vortex potentials differ by a factor $i$. Swapping them swaps streamlines and equipotentials.
  • $\arg z$ is harmonic only away from the cut. Choose the branch to match the boundary.
30-second shortcuts
  • Upper half-plane with $\Phi=V_1$ on $x\lt0$ and $V_2$ on $x\gt0$: $\Phi=V_2+\frac{V_1-V_2}{\pi}\arg z$.
  • Coaxial cylinders: $\Phi=A+B\ln r$, the real part of $A+B\ln z$.

X1. A flow has complex potential $\Omega=z^2$. Find the speed at $z=1+2i$, to two decimal places. GATE NATEasy
Show solution

$|\Omega'|=|2z|=2\sqrt5=\mathbf{4.47}$. The velocity vector is $\overline{2z}=2-4i$.

X2. For $\Omega=U\left(z+\frac{a^2}{z}\right)$, the maximum speed on the cylinder $|z|=a$ is CSIR Part BEasy
(a) $U$(b) $2U$(c) $\sqrt2U$(d) $U/2$
Show solution

$\Omega'=U(1-a^2e^{-2i\theta}/a^2)=U(1-e^{-2i\theta})$, so $|\Omega'|=2U|\sin\theta|$. The maximum is $2U$, at the top and bottom. Option (b).

X3. The volume flux out of any closed curve around the origin for $\Omega=m\ln z$ ($m$ real) is CSIR Part BMedium
(a) $m$(b) $2\pi m$(c) $0$(d) $\pi m$
Show solution

The flux is the jump in $\Psi=m\theta$ around the curve, $2\pi m$. Equivalently, $\oint\Omega'dz=2\pi im$ has imaginary part $2\pi m$. Option (b).

X4. Two semi-infinite conducting plates meet at a right angle. The plate on $\theta=0$ is at $0$ V and the plate on $\theta=\pi/2$ is at $100$ V. Find $|\vec E|$ at distance $r=2$ m from the edge, in V/m, to two decimal places. GATE NATMedium
Show solution

$\Phi=\frac{100}{\pi/2}\theta$, so $|E|=\frac1r\left|\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\theta}\right|=\frac{200}{\pi r}=\frac{100}{\pi}=\mathbf{31.83}$.

X5. (Multiple correct) For an analytic complex potential $\Omega=\Phi+i\Psi$: CSIR Part CMedium

Show solution

(a), (b), (d). The velocity is $\overline{\Omega'}$.

X6. The boundary of the upper half-plane is held at $T=100$ on $x\lt0$ and $T=0$ on $x\gt0$. Find the steady temperature at $z=1+i$. CSIR Part CMedium
Show solution

$T=\frac{100}{\pi}\arg z$ is harmonic, equals $0$ at $\theta=0$ and $100$ at $\theta=\pi$. At $1+i$, $\theta=\frac\pi4$, so $T=\mathbf{25}$. The isotherms are rays from the origin.

12. Summary

  • Harmonic ∘ analytic = harmonic. Map the hard region to a slab or half-plane, solve there, and substitute back.
  • Boundary values, insulated edges, isotherms and flow lines all carry over.
  • Complex potential $\Omega=\Phi+i\Psi$: streamlines $\Psi=$const, speed $|\Omega'(z)|$, stagnation where $\Omega'=0$.
  • Key maps: $\ln z$ (wedge/half-plane ↔ strip), $z^n$ (corners), $\frac1z$ (tangent circles), $z+\frac1z$ (cylinder), $\ln\frac{z+1}{z-1}$ (two cylinders, source–sink), $w+e^w$ (channel end).
